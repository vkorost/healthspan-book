# Chapter 14: The Supplement Shelf

Two grams of NMN powder go under the tongue every morning. Blood NAD reportedly doubles within two weeks. David Sinclair takes it.<sup>[1]</sup> Tony Wyss-Coray, the Stanford neuroscientist who discovered that young blood can rejuvenate old brains, takes it too. He calls his own results "all anecdata."<sup>[2]</sup> Both men study aging for a living. Both supplement. Neither claims proof.

That is probably the most useful sentence in this chapter.

The gap between the best-supported compound on the shelf and the most speculative is not a gentle gradient. It is closer to the difference between a focused photograph and a blur that might, under generous inspection, contain an outline of something.

Creatine has two decades of controlled human data and a safety profile so clean that the main controversy is whether it causes hair loss (it does not).<sup>[3]</sup> NAD precursors have a plausible mechanism, rising blood levels, commercial enthusiasm, and no completed human trial showing an effect on lifespan, frailty, or any hard healthspan endpoint.<sup>[4]</sup> **Rapamycin** extends life in every model organism ever tested and has never finished a human longevity trial.<sup>[5]</sup> GLP-1 drugs are prescribed to one in eight American adults, and only eight of a hundred stay on them past three years.<sup>[6]</sup>

I noticed that last figure and had to reread it. One in eight start. Fewer than one in twelve remain.

**The supplement shelf** is not a recommendation list. It is a map of what exists, what the evidence actually supports, and where the confidence runs out. Some of what follows will be sharply resolved. Much of it will not. What struck me, working through the data, was how often a compound that looks unambiguous from one direction looks contested from another.

## The Well-Supported Few

Four compounds carry enough human data, taken together, that most clinicians in the dataset recommend them without caveat: creatine, vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3 fatty acids. In a contact sheet full of uncertain exposures, these are the frames where you can actually see what you are looking at.

### Creatine

The body manufactures about a gram of creatine per day from three amino acids in the liver and kidneys. Men produce more than women endogenously.<sup>[7]</sup> Supplementing with creatine monohydrate at five grams per day saturates muscle stores within three to four weeks, but the practical effect in the gym sounds modest: one to two extra reps on a set, roughly a second off a sprint. It does not build muscle by itself. It provides the energy for one more effort, and that effort, repeated over months, is what changes the body.<sup>[8]</sup>

I found the modesty of that mechanism reassuring.

Two decades of human data show no adverse kidney effects at up to ten grams per day in people with healthy kidneys.<sup>[9]</sup> Blood creatinine rises on supplementation, which is a normal metabolic consequence, not a sign of kidney damage. The recommendation that appears across seven channels struck me as practical rather than dramatic: tell your doctor you take creatine before bloodwork, so the creatinine number does not trigger unnecessary alarm.

A well-designed RCT specifically testing creatine's effect on DHT and hair density found no significant difference on either measure (see Chapter 3 for the full trial).<sup>[10]</sup> The prior concern came from a single earlier study that measured a DHT rise but did not actually measure hair outcomes. The newer, better-designed trial found nothing.

The fear persists anyway. It is one of those nutritional anxieties that outlives its evidence and keeps circulating under its own momentum, which, in my reading, is a pattern that recurs across supplement conversations more broadly.

What creatine does beyond muscle is where the picture develops in an unexpected direction. The brain is two percent of body weight and consumes twenty percent of the body's energy. Creatine helps recycle ATP, the fuel molecule, faster. At five grams per day, muscles consume most of the supplemental creatine before it reaches the brain, particularly in active people, and higher doses, possibly ten to twenty grams, appear necessary to meaningfully raise brain creatine levels.<sup>[11]</sup>

A meta-analysis of sixteen clinical trials confirmed cognitive benefit for memory tasks, but the clearest results came from people under brain stress: sleep deprivation, traumatic brain injury, depression.<sup>[12]</sup> Whether five grams per day improves cognition in a healthy, well-rested adult is genuinely unclear.

Product quality is a real problem. A study by Andy Galpin's research team found adulteration rates of ten to forty percent depending on country of origin.<sup>[13]</sup> Creatine gummies frequently contain little or no active creatine. Think of it as loading a camera with film and discovering, after an afternoon of shooting, that the canister was empty.

Third-party certified creatine monohydrate, particularly the Creapure-certified source, is the recommendation that appears most consistently across the dataset. The form matters less than people think: monohydrate is the evidence standard. Creatine HCl is more water-soluble, which reduces bloating in some users, but one expert argues higher solubility in liquid does not translate to different absorption once consumed.<sup>[14]</sup>

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D functions more like a hormone than a vitamin, participating in immune regulation, bone mineralization, calcium homeostasis, testosterone biosynthesis, and blood flow.<sup>[15]</sup> Deficiency is so widespread that it appears even in construction workers with forty to sixty hours per week of outdoor sun exposure and in professional athletes living in sunny climates.<sup>[16]</sup> I had assumed that enough time in the sun would settle the matter. It does not. Individual genetics governing conversion and utilization are a major factor, meaning sun exposure alone is frequently insufficient.

A Swiss randomized controlled trial combined omega-3, vitamin D3, and resistance training and found that the three-intervention arm showed approximately 66 percent lower invasive cancer risk and approximately 3.8 months of slowed epigenetic aging (see Chapter 11 for the primary findings).<sup>[17]</sup> Vitamin D alone did not slow epigenetic aging in that trial.

But in a separate study of severely vitamin D-deficient adults, 4,000 IU per day for six weeks reversed epigenetic aging markers, suggesting that the benefit may be greatest where the deficit is deepest.<sup>[18]</sup> The supplement helps most where the shortage is worst, and barely registers where it is not. To me, that logic is both tidy and, for people who are already supplementing, somewhat deflating.

The dosing question is genuinely contested. Expert-recommended doses of 5,000 to 8,000 IU per day exceed official guidelines of 600 to 800 IU by nearly an order of magnitude.<sup>[19]</sup> Multiple experts characterize the official recommended levels as outdated science from approximately 1960, and a named HHS official has used the word "overwhelming" to describe the data supporting higher targets.<sup>[20]</sup> Official guidance has not been updated. That gap between clinical practice and published guidelines is wide enough to walk through without bending.

Two cofactors are non-negotiable. Vitamin K2 must be taken alongside D3; without it, the calcium that vitamin D helps absorb deposits in arteries and kidneys rather than bone.<sup>[21]</sup> Magnesium is required for vitamin D metabolism; supplementing D without adequate magnesium limits the vitamin's effectiveness, because the enzymes that convert D3 to its active form require magnesium as a cofactor.<sup>[22]</sup>

Sunlight provides benefits that supplementation does not replicate: nitric oxide release from skin, cortisol and melatonin receptor resetting, and circadian signaling (see Chapter 7).<sup>[23]</sup> The supplement is the floor. The sun is the ceiling. Testing blood levels before and during supplementation is the only reliable way to calibrate dose, given how much the genetics of metabolism vary from one person to the next.

### Magnesium

Magnesium participates in more than 300 biochemical processes, from ATP production to nerve signaling to heart rhythm regulation.<sup>[24]</sup> Three hundred is not an approximation. It is a count, and when I read that number, the pattern of magnesium appearing across chapters that seem to have nothing else in common began to make sense.

Soil depletion from commercial farming has reduced plant magnesium content to roughly one-tenth of what it was a century ago, according to a 1936 US Senate document on soil nutrient decline that multiple experts still cite.<sup>[25]</sup> The consequence, to me, is plain: modern diets rarely supply enough, regardless of food quality. You could eat carefully and still fall short.

Standard blood tests are unreliable for magnesium. The body maintains serum levels by raiding intracellular stores, so blood levels can read normal while cellular magnesium is severely depleted.<sup>[26]</sup> It is the equivalent of checking a camera's light meter and getting a steady reading because the battery is draining from a compartment you cannot see.

Symptoms tell a more honest story: constipation, poor sleep, nighttime muscle cramps, irritability, low mood, and skipped heartbeats are all associated with magnesium deficiency in the clinical literature.<sup>[27]</sup> The form determines what the supplement actually does, and this matters more than most people realize.

Magnesium glycinate supports sleep and mood, is gentle on the gut, and is the most consistently recommended form for nighttime use.<sup>[28]</sup> Magnesium L-threonate is the only form documented to cross the blood-brain barrier and raise brain magnesium levels; Dr. Guosong Liu, who developed the compound, reported brain age reversal of 7.5 years in six weeks in a controlled trial (see Chapter 11 for the caution flags on this extraordinary claim).<sup>[29]</sup>

Magnesium citrate is an effective laxative but not suitable for systemic use. Magnesium oxide, the cheapest form found in most discount supplements, has the lowest bioavailability and the most gastrointestinal side effects.<sup>[30]</sup> Taking the wrong form for the intended purpose is one of the most common and correctable supplementation errors, and in my reading, one of the least publicized.

### Omega-3 (cross-reference)

Omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, are covered in detail in Chapter 11 alongside the omega-3 index research of Dr. Bill Harris and the Swiss RCT. Their inclusion in the well-supported tier rests on that evidence base: Japan's omega-3 index of 10 to 11 percent versus the US average of approximately 5 percent, correlated with an approximately five-year life expectancy difference.<sup>[31]</sup>

A Cleveland Clinic cardiologist survey of approximately 3,000 physicians circa 2014 found that **CoQ10** and fish oil were the supplements cardiologists were already incorporating into practice.<sup>[32]</sup> When the people whose profession is studying hearts are already taking a compound, the signal, to my eye, is worth weighing.

## Fueling the Cells

The compounds in this tier target mitochondrial function, glutathione production, and amino acid support. They have human mechanistic data and clinical use but lack the depth of the well-supported tier. If the first group is a clean, properly exposed print, this tier is a print that shows detail under good light but loses definition at the edges.

### GlyNAC

**NAC**, N-acetylcysteine, is the standard hospital treatment for acetaminophen overdose because it restores depleted glutathione, the body's primary intracellular antioxidant.<sup>[33]</sup> That clinical application is well established. The supplement application extends the same chemistry: NAC donates a sulfhydryl group that reduces oxidized molecules, recharging the glutathione redox system.<sup>[34]</sup> The same compound that saves lives in emergency rooms has a second, quieter career as a daily supplement. That clinical pedigree, to me, is what makes NAC more interesting than most items on this shelf.

Oral glutathione supplements are largely ineffective. Poor absorption and rapid degradation mean swallowed glutathione does not reliably raise cellular levels. The preferred route is GlyNAC, the combination of NAC and glycine, which provides the two precursors the body needs for sustained endogenous glutathione production.<sup>[35]</sup> Glycine constitutes approximately one-third of all amino acid residues in collagen, and a study measuring plasma levels found that one gram of supplemental glycine raised plasma concentration from approximately 250 to 400 micromoles per liter, suggesting the amino acid reaches collagen-synthesizing tissues.<sup>[36]</sup>

The FDA attempted to reclassify NAC as a drug after it entered clinical trials, which would have removed it from the supplement market. It remained available after industry pushback.<sup>[37]</sup> The regulatory lesson, as I read it, is plain: once a compound enters pharmaceutical drug development, the rules can shift, and availability is not guaranteed. A supplement can become a drug not because its chemistry changed but because someone filed the paperwork.

**TMG**, trimethylglycine, is a related methyl donor that supports creatine synthesis and a wide range of methylation reactions. Observational data from one clinical practice spanning approximately 150,000 patients showed TMG lowering homocysteine from high double digits to low single digits.<sup>[38]</sup> That is a single practice's observation across a large patient base, not a controlled trial. The caution is direct: that homocysteine reduction can lower blood pressure sufficiently to require dose adjustment in patients on antihypertensive medication. Physician consultation before starting is not optional for that group.<sup>[39]</sup>

### CoQ10

CoQ10, in its reduced form ubiquinol, is an electron carrier in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, shuttling electrons between Complexes 1, 2, and 3.<sup>[40]</sup> It sits at the center of both ATP synthesis and mitochondrial antioxidant defense. The practical question, to me, is whether taking it as a supplement makes a measurable difference, and the answer depends on who is taking it.

The clearest case is statin users. Statins block the mevalonate pathway, which produces CoQ10 as a downstream product alongside skeletal muscle proteins and vitamin K2. The depletion causes myalgia, muscle soreness, and impaired mitochondrial function.<sup>[41]</sup> Co-supplementation with CoQ10 or ubiquinol is widely recommended for all statin users, and that recommendation extends to anyone taking red yeast rice extract, which functions as a natural statin. Beyond statin users, the picture is less certain. Well-nourished individuals without deficiency may experience little measurable effect.<sup>[42]</sup>

CoQ10 is considered broadly beneficial for long-term cardiovascular health with reasonably good study support, and the same Cleveland Clinic physician survey found it already in widespread clinical use.<sup>[43]</sup> A standard dose is 200 milligrams per day in the morning.<sup>[44]</sup> Supplement quality varies widely. Blood levels of CoQ10 are measurable and can plummet when switching to an inferior brand.<sup>[45]</sup> That measurability is itself valuable: unlike most supplements, you can test whether what you are buying is doing anything at all. GMP certification and third-party testing are, in my reading, the minimum due diligence.

### Taurine

**Taurine** is a conditionally essential amino acid found predominantly in animal foods. It plays roles in brain development, blood pressure regulation, blood glucose stabilization, and vision protection.<sup>[46]</sup> Endogenous synthesis from other amino acids exists but is insufficient in volume for vegans, making taurine one of several nutrients, alongside creatine, carnitine, choline, and B12, commonly deficient on poorly designed plant-based diets.<sup>[47]</sup>

The evidence base for taurine is thinner than for the other compounds in this section, and it is worth being direct about that. A single unnamed study found that 50 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day improved thermoregulation during hot-weather exercise, delaying the onset of rising core temperature and increasing heat transfer.<sup>[48]</sup>

The study was not conducted in resting or sleeping conditions, and the dose of approximately four grams for an 80-kilogram person is extrapolated from that single protocol. The calming and sleep-promoting properties attributed to taurine in the dataset rest primarily on practitioner anecdote rather than controlled trial.<sup>[49]</sup> That is not a verdict against taurine. It is a report of where the data stand.

## The Longevity Shelf

These compounds have plausible mechanisms, animal data, and varying degrees of early human evidence. None has a completed human randomized controlled trial showing an effect on lifespan or a hard healthspan endpoint. They are bets, not certainties. In darkroom terms, the negatives look promising under the safelight, but nobody has yet made a finished print from any of them.

### NAD Precursors

NAD is present in every cell and participates in over 300 reactions. Its two primary roles are mitochondrial energy production and cellular maintenance, fueling the sirtuin enzymes that manage DNA repair and metabolic resilience. NAD levels decline by approximately half every twenty years.<sup>[50]</sup> I found that figure worth rereading. Halving every two decades means a sixty-year-old has roughly one-eighth the NAD of a newborn.

Supplementing with NMN or **NR** does raise blood NAD levels. Clinical studies confirm that proxy endpoint. But no human randomized controlled trial has shown effects on lifespan, frailty, or other hard healthspan outcomes.<sup>[51]</sup> The gap between "it raises NAD in the blood" and "it makes you live longer or better" is precisely where the disagreement lives. It is a large gap, and it has been open for years.

David Sinclair's personal protocol of one to two grams of sublingual NMN daily is the most widely cited.<sup>[52]</sup> Rhonda Patrick takes NR and reports energy and recovery benefits but would not rank it among her top supplements if budget-constrained.<sup>[53]</sup> Tony Wyss-Coray takes NMN and reports subjective energy gains. He also reports faster hair and nail growth, which he flags as a caution signal for indiscriminate growth stimulation rather than a benefit.<sup>[54]</sup> Three prominent researchers, three personal protocols, no proof between them. They are each developing film in separate darkrooms, and none of them has seen the other's prints.

The mechanistic debate is sharper than the personal protocols suggest. Dr. Nichola Conlon argues that the primary bottleneck for age-related NAD decline is not raw precursor shortage but reduced activity of NAMPT, the rate-limiting enzyme in the salvage pathway.<sup>[55]</sup> If the enzyme is the constraint, flooding the system with precursors may raise blood levels without meaningfully increasing intracellular NAD where it is needed. Flooding may also burden methylation pathways, especially in people with MTHFR variants.<sup>[56]</sup>

The problem, in that reading, is a bottleneck in processing the chemistry rather than a shortage of it, and the relevant question is whether raising that number actually does what you think it does.

A separate concern: NMN and NR injected into tumor-bearing mice accelerated the growth of rare pancreatic cancer cells.<sup>[57]</sup> A counterbalancing finding showed nicotinamide prevented a type of skin cancer. The effects appear to be cancer-type-specific and context-dependent, not a blanket contraindication. But the accelerated-hair-growth signal that multiple users report is consistent with indiscriminate growth stimulation, and the honest statement is that nobody knows yet whether that signal matters. The safety picture, to me, is murkier than the marketing implies.

Product quality is a documented problem. A study testing ten commercially available NMN preparations found that many did not contain the labeled amounts.<sup>[58]</sup> NMN is chemically unstable, making third-party testing essential.

### Spermidine

A twenty-year European epidemiological study followed 829 people and found that higher dietary spermidine intake was associated with reduced all-cause mortality. The finding was replicated in the Sapphire cohort of 1,770 people and held after adjusting for income, education, BMI, physical activity, and diet quality.<sup>[59]</sup> A 2018 study confirmed an inverse association between dietary spermidine and cardiovascular and cancer-related mortality independent of confounders.<sup>[60]</sup> The consistency across those analyses is not common for a dietary association; most do not survive that many adjustments.

Spermidine is a polyamine present in every cell. It promotes autophagy, the cellular recycling process, and inhibits apoptosis. In yeast, the mechanism was confirmed definitively: when autophagy genes were knocked out, the lifespan-extending effect of spermidine disappeared entirely.<sup>[61]</sup> That is a clean result. Remove the recycling, and the benefit vanishes. Spermidine also crosses the blood-brain barrier, enabling direct neuroprotection. A University of Graz study showed spermidine-induced autophagy improved mitochondrial function in the brain.<sup>[62]</sup>

The dietary sources are specific and, in my reading, unexpectedly appetizing: mushrooms carry the highest concentration at roughly 160 micrograms per gram, followed by aged cheeses like Parmigiano Reggiano and Manchego, fermented meats, natto, kimchi, chicken liver, and walnuts.<sup>[63]</sup> Gut bacteria also produce spermidine from dietary precursors, which may partly explain the longevity associations with fermented diets.

The associations are consistent, the mechanism is plausible, and the yeast data links spermidine directly to autophagy-dependent lifespan extension. The human evidence, however, remains correlational.<sup>[59]</sup><sup>[60]</sup> Observational design cannot confirm causation. People who eat mushrooms, aged cheese, and fermented foods may differ from those who do not in ways that have nothing to do with spermidine. Everything lines up. Nothing proves it. That gap, between consistent association and confirmed cause, is where much of this chapter lives, and I find it worth naming plainly rather than filling with optimism.

### Urolithin A

Urolithin A is a postbiotic molecule produced by gut bacteria from ellagitannins found in pomegranates, raspberries, and walnuts. Only approximately 12 to 40 percent of people carry the required gut bacterial species to perform this conversion. The rest derive no urolithin A from dietary sources regardless of how many pomegranates they eat.<sup>[64]</sup> The supplement may matter most for the people who cannot produce the compound on their own, and that turns out to be the majority. I paused on that arithmetic: more people lack the required bacteria than have them.

The compound's primary documented mechanism is mitophagy, the identification and recycling of damaged mitochondria. This process slows naturally with aging. Direct supplementation produces substantially higher and more reliable blood levels than eating ellagitannin-rich foods, even in people who can convert them endogenously (see Chapter 9 for the conversion biology).<sup>[65]</sup> The gap between dietary intake and supplemental dosing is large enough that eating more pomegranates is not a substitute.

Urolithin A is marketed as "exercise in a bottle." The slogan overpromises. A four-month clinical trial of 500 milligrams per day in adults aged 40 to 65 showed approximately 12 percent improvement in leg muscle strength.<sup>[66]</sup> The compound did not improve exercise capacity, muscle strength beyond that single measure, or any functional outcome that would resemble replacing a training program. Twelve percent leg strength over four months is a measurable addition to training.

The superager correlation is more striking. Healthy individuals aged 90 to 100 and older carry the urolithin A-producing gut bacteria at 50 to 70 percent prevalence, compared to 12 to 20 percent in the general population.<sup>[67]</sup> Correlation, not causation. But a population that lives longest has three to five times the prevalence of bacteria that make a compound known to recycle damaged mitochondria. That is the kind of association that earns a place on the shelf, even if it cannot yet earn a conclusion. To me, it remains one of the more intriguing signals in this chapter.

The commercial conflicts in this space are substantial. Multiple prominent speakers in the dataset, including Dave Asprey and Steven Gundry, have affiliate or sponsorship relationships with Timeline Nutrition, the company that produces the most-studied urolithin A supplement.<sup>[68]</sup> Claims from those speakers should be read with that incentive structure in view. When the loudest voices are also the funded ones, the volume itself becomes a data point.

### Sulforaphane

Sulforaphane is a plant isothiocyanate produced when glucoraphanin in cruciferous vegetables meets the enzyme myrosinase upon chewing, crushing, or freezing. Broccoli sprouts contain 10 to 100 times more glucoraphanin per gram than mature broccoli.<sup>[69]</sup> I expected a factor of two or three. A factor of up to a hundred makes the sprout a different proposition entirely.

In high-pollution regions of China, a broccoli sprout sulforaphane extract increased urinary excretion of the carcinogen benzene by approximately 60 percent within 24 hours.<sup>[70]</sup> That is a measurable detoxification effect in human subjects, not a petri dish. The mechanism is NRF2 activation, which upregulates the phase-2 detoxification enzymes that make carcinogens and environmental toxins water-soluble and excretable. Sulforaphane is described across four channels as the most potent naturally occurring dietary activator of this pathway.<sup>[71]</sup> It also upregulates glutathione in both plasma and brain, providing a dietary route to the same antioxidant defense that NAC supplies pharmaceutically.<sup>[72]</sup>

Andrew Huberman began taking sulforaphane specifically as a microplastic and PFAS mitigation strategy after discussing the data with Mark Hyman.<sup>[73]</sup> That is one researcher's personal protocol, not a clinical recommendation, and it carries the weight of any single person's choice rather than the weight of a trial.

The practical tip is simple and, to my eye, elegant: freezing broccoli sprouts before adding them to a smoothie ruptures the cell vacuoles and maximizes sulforaphane release, equivalent to thorough chewing. An ice cube tray makes portioning straightforward.<sup>[74]</sup> For supplement users, Rhonda Patrick takes glucoraphanin, the stable precursor, rather than sulforaphane itself, citing thirteen clinical studies using the Avmacol formulation.<sup>[75]</sup>

### Senolytics: Fisetin and Quercetin

**Senescent cells** are sometimes called zombie cells: they stop dividing but refuse to die, accumulating with age and secreting inflammatory signals that damage surrounding tissue. Quercetin and **fisetin** are plant polyphenols that selectively induce apoptosis in these cells, clearing them.<sup>[76]</sup> The biology of clearance is, in my reading, one of the cleaner ideas in the longevity field, though the execution is considerably messier.

Quercetin was among the first two compounds identified as SIRT1 activators, discovered by Conrad Howitz at Biomol before resveratrol, launching the entire sirtuin-activating compound field.<sup>[77]</sup> At 500 milligrams per day, it inhibits xanthine oxidase, the same enzyme targeted by the gout drug allopurinol, and lowered uric acid by 8 percent in two weeks in a small RCT of 22 young men.<sup>[78]</sup> It also functions as a natural antihistamine, without the drowsiness of conventional alternatives.<sup>[79]</sup> The range of documented effects is, for a single plant polyphenol, unusually wide.

The **senolytic** evidence is more dramatic but harder to interpret. The first human senolytic trial combined dasatinib, a prescription cancer drug, with quercetin in pulmonary fibrosis patients. Breathing improved within one month. Every participant wanted to repeat the treatment despite gastrointestinal side effects.<sup>[80]</sup> That trial used a pharmaceutical-grade drug alongside the supplement. Whether supplement-dose quercetin and fisetin alone achieve comparable senolytic clearance remains an open question in the field.<sup>[81]</sup> The most compelling result, to me, came from a combination that includes a prescription cancer drug, which makes it difficult to credit the supplement alone.

The dosing protocol reflects the biology rather than the marketing calendar: senescent cells re-accumulate over weeks after clearance, supporting a periodic approach of two consecutive days per month rather than daily use.<sup>[82]</sup> Senolytic use is not recommended before approximately age 40 to 45, because some senescent cell accumulation may serve beneficial signaling roles earlier in life.<sup>[83]</sup>

A fundamental limitation sits behind all of this: there is currently no non-invasive method to confirm senolytic activity in vivo. Tissue biopsy is the only confirmation method. All clinical studies rely on patient-reported outcomes, not direct measurement of senescent cell clearance.<sup>[84]</sup> You take the compound. You cannot, at present, confirm it worked. As far as I can tell, no one has proposed a practical way around that problem.

## Collagen

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the body, comprising approximately 20 to 40 percent of total body protein. Skin is roughly 80 percent collagen by dry weight. Bone, ligaments, tendons, and cartilage are largely collagen as well.<sup>[85]</sup> It is, by volume, the protein the body is mostly built from.

The central controversy is whether eating collagen rebuilds collagen in the places that matter. The skeptics have a clean argument: collagen molecules are too large to absorb intact; they break down into generic amino acids with no known targeting mechanism to direct them to skin or joints.<sup>[86]</sup>

Steven Gundry states it flatly: "Eating collagen doesn't grow collagen, just as eating nails doesn't grow nails." He adds that nearly all collagen research is industry-funded.<sup>[87]</sup> The flatness of that statement is useful. The funding note is also useful. To my eye, the tracer evidence and the skeptics' argument answer slightly different questions: one asks whether the fragments arrive, the other asks whether they rebuild anything once they do.

The response is isotopic tracer data, and it complicates the dismissal. Studies have shown that hydrolyzed collagen breaks down not fully into single amino acids but into di- and tripeptides that resist further digestion, enter circulation intact, and reach chondrocytes, where they increase chondrocyte activity.<sup>[88]</sup> Multiple systematic reviews report benefits for skin outcomes at 15 to 30 grams per day.<sup>[89]</sup> The mechanism exists. Its clinical magnitude is, to me, genuinely uncertain rather than settled in either direction.

What is not debated: collagen is an incomplete protein, lacking adequate leucine and branched-chain amino acids for muscle protein synthesis. It is not a substitute for whey or essential amino acids for building or maintaining muscle.<sup>[90]</sup> Taking collagen for joints and skin while relying on it for muscle is a common error, and one that, in my reading, the marketing of collagen products does nothing to correct.

Vitamin C is required as a cofactor for collagen synthesis. Without it, collagen breaks down faster than the body can rebuild it, the biochemical basis of scurvy.<sup>[91]</sup> Smokers deplete vitamin C rapidly via oxidative stress, which explains why the accelerated collagen degradation and wrinkling associated with smoking is so visible and so consistent.

Women lose approximately 30 percent of facial and skin collagen as estrogen declines during perimenopause and menopause (see Chapter 10 for the WHI findings that collapsed hormone therapy prescriptions).<sup>[92]</sup> That loss is hormonally driven and cannot be fully replaced by oral collagen supplementation alone. The supplement can assist, but it cannot reverse the endocrine cause on its own.

## What GLP-1 Drugs Actually Do

GLP-1, glucagon-like peptide-1, is a hormone the gut releases after a meal. It tells the brain the body has eaten enough, prompts the pancreas to release insulin, and slows the rate at which the stomach empties. When natural GLP-1 release falters, often because inadequate dietary fiber has starved the gut bacteria that produce it, the result is a persistent hunger signal that drives fat storage.<sup>[93]</sup>

Pharmaceutical GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide) mimic this hormone to suppress appetite, regulate insulin, and slow gastric emptying, overriding rather than repairing the underlying gut-brain satiety mechanism.<sup>[94]</sup> That distinction matters, to me, more than the marketing suggests.

In patients with PCOS and insulin resistance, the primary action is insulin sensitization and glucose clearance; appetite suppression is secondary. Dr. Thais Aliabadi's clinical data from GLP-1 use in PCOS patients since 2014 shows an average of approximately 24 pounds of weight loss over four months, menstrual cycle regularization, and substantial symptom reduction.<sup>[95]</sup> Those numbers come from a single clinician's practice and carry the limitations of any uncontrolled observation.

One in eight US adults currently use GLP-1 drugs, yet only approximately eight of a hundred remain on medication after three years.<sup>[96]</sup> That dropout rate is itself a finding. Without concurrent resistance training, adequate protein intake, and behavioral change around food, weight regain after stopping is described by clinicians as near-universal.<sup>[97]</sup>

A significant proportion of the weight lost on GLP-1 drugs is lean muscle mass rather than fat. Without resistance training to counteract this, muscle and bone density loss are real risks.<sup>[98]</sup> The weight on the scale goes down. What comes off is partly the tissue you most need to keep. I was surprised by how little attention this receives in the popular conversation about these drugs; the lean-mass question deserves to be at the center, and it sits at the margin.

GLP-1 drugs reduce what users call "food noise," the constant mental preoccupation with food.<sup>[99]</sup> That cognitive effect is consistently reported and may be the drug's most underappreciated benefit, freeing mental bandwidth for people whose relationship with hunger has dominated their attention for years. The phrase itself, "food noise," struck me as unusually precise for patient-reported language.

GLP-1 receptor agonists have been in clinical use for approximately twenty years and used by millions. That track record makes major unforeseen delayed effects less likely, though not impossible.<sup>[100]</sup> GLP-1 receptors are expressed on many tissues beyond the gut and pancreas, and the systemic receptor distribution creates open questions about both benefits and risks in diverse organs.<sup>[101]</sup> The twenty-year safety record is reassuring. The breadth of receptor expression, in my reading, qualifies the reassurance.

Microdosing, defined as two to twenty-five percent of the standard high maintenance dose, is an emerging practice among longevity-oriented clinicians.<sup>[102]</sup> The rationale is to remain below the nausea threshold while preserving metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and behavioral benefits. No randomized controlled trial has validated this approach. It is practitioner-designed, not evidence-established. That distinction, by now, should be familiar to anyone who has read this far.

GLP-1 longevity effects appear distinct from and potentially additive to caloric restriction effects, based on biological aging clock data. Each mechanism appears to slow the pace of aging through different pathways rather than one being a proxy for the other.<sup>[103]</sup> Whether GLP-1 drugs confer geroprotective benefit independent of weight loss and metabolic correction in non-obese individuals is an open question with no current answer.<sup>[104]</sup> That question, in my reading, is the one most likely to reshape clinical practice if answered clearly.

This chapter explains the mechanism. It does not prescribe the drug. GLP-1 agonists are prescription medications requiring physician supervision, individualized dosing, and concurrent lifestyle modification to produce durable results. The decision is yours and your doctor's.

## The Prescription Frontier

Five interventions appear repeatedly in the longevity conversation. All are prescription-gated or not readily obtainable. None is actionable from this book. Each is defined here because a reader who encounters the healthspan field will hear these terms, and understanding what they mean and what is contested about them is more useful than pretending they do not exist. These are, to extend the analogy one final time, negatives that have been filed and cataloged but never printed.

### Rapamycin

Rapamycin inhibits mTOR signaling and extends lifespan in every model organism where it has been tested: yeast, worms, flies, and mice.<sup>[105]</sup> No completed human randomized controlled trial for longevity has been published. Peter Attia classifies it as "promising, not proven" for geroprotection, distinguishing it from interventions with stronger human evidence.<sup>[106]</sup> That phrase does a great deal of work in a small space.

Longevity-protocol use typically involves intermittent low dosing, roughly five to six milligrams weekly, designed to avoid the immunosuppression seen at the much higher transplant doses.<sup>[107]</sup> The immune question is not straightforward: at certain doses and schedules, rapalogs have actually enhanced rather than suppressed vaccine responses, suggesting the net immunological effect is dose-schedule dependent, not simply suppressive.<sup>[108]</sup> That reversal, in my reading, makes the immune question genuinely complicated rather than merely worrisome.

The debate is whether animal data obtained in pathogen-free cage conditions translate to humans living in environments full of infections.<sup>[109]</sup> The lifespan extension data is real. The human translation is the contested question. Rapamycin sits on the line between a credible longevity candidate and an unproven extrapolation from caged mice. It is a bet worth watching. It is not an actionable protocol.

### Metformin

Metformin improves insulin sensitivity and is a primary treatment for type 2 diabetes. It is inexpensive and long off-patent.<sup>[110]</sup> The TAME trial, Targeting Aging with Metformin, is the first FDA-sanctioned trial with "aging" as a clinical endpoint. It is ongoing and has not reported results.<sup>[111]</sup> To me, the fact that the trial exists at all is as significant as whatever it eventually finds.

The debate is whether metformin extends healthspan in people who are already metabolically healthy. The evidence that it helps diabetics and pre-diabetics is not in serious dispute.

The trade-off for healthy, active individuals is specific: metformin blunts the aerobic training response and reduces strength adaptation in older adults.<sup>[112]</sup> For someone whose primary longevity strategy is exercise, metformin may be actively counterproductive. The TAME trial exists to answer precisely this question. Until it reports, the answer is genuinely unknown. The tension is worth stating clearly: the drug most commonly proposed for longevity may undermine the behavior with the strongest longevity evidence.

### Statins

Statins lower LDL cholesterol by blocking HMG-CoA reductase in the mevalonate pathway. Converging evidence from Mendelian randomization studies and randomized controlled trials indicates that regardless of the mechanism by which LDL is lowered, the same proportional cardiovascular risk reduction follows, supporting LDL/ApoB lowering itself as the causal factor.<sup>[113]</sup> That convergence from two independent methods is unusually persuasive.

The cost is biological, not just financial. Blocking the mevalonate pathway reduces not only cholesterol but also CoQ10, menaquinone 4 (vitamin K2), and skeletal muscle proteins.<sup>[114]</sup> The resulting myalgia and mitochondrial impairment are sufficiently common that CoQ10 co-supplementation is now standard clinical advice for statin users. To me, that co-supplementation itself tells a story: the drug works, and it imposes costs that require a second compound to manage.

A minority position in the dataset questions whether LDL is independently causal for cardiovascular disease or merely a marker that matters only in combination with elevated triglycerides, Lp(a), and ApoB.<sup>[115]</sup> That position contradicts extensive Mendelian randomization evidence and is not supported by named peer-reviewed literature in this corpus. The mainstream consensus treats LDL/ApoB as independently causal for atherosclerosis. The debate is how aggressively to lower it pharmacologically and at what point lifestyle alone is insufficient.

### Testosterone Replacement Therapy

Testosterone levels in men have declined at the population level by approximately 30 percent compared to the prior generation at the same age. A 2020 report placed the prevalence of testosterone deficiency at 10 to 40 percent of adult males, attributed primarily to lifestyle and environmental factors.<sup>[116]</sup> The breadth of that range struck me as itself diagnostic: the field cannot agree on how widespread the problem is.

Low testosterone is frequently misdiagnosed as depression and treated with antidepressants, which can cause emotional blunting, fatigue, weight gain, and further libido suppression. Sleep disturbance, not erectile dysfunction, is the most commonly reported first symptom.<sup>[117]</sup> I expected the reverse. Most of the popular conversation centers on libido and muscle; the clinical data say the body's first complaint is that it cannot sleep.

The lifestyle interventions for raising testosterone without prescription are covered in Chapter 10: micronutrient optimization (vitamin D, zinc, magnesium), resistance training, sleep, and reducing endocrine disruptor exposure. TRT itself targets a total testosterone of approximately 1,000 nanograms per deciliter, described as the range providing motivational and cognitive benefits without major side effects.<sup>[118]</sup> Co-administration of HCG, clomiphene, or enclomiphene can maintain fertility during TRT.<sup>[119]</sup> That fertility preservation option is, to me, an underappreciated detail in the TRT conversation.

The cardiovascular safety question has not been fully resolved. An original 1990s study linking testosterone to elevated heart attack risk is characterized by multiple guests as methodologically flawed, with approximately 24 to 25 subsequent studies said to have contradicted it.<sup>[120]</sup> Evidence overall moves toward safety at physiologic doses, but guideline bodies still apply caution. TRT is a physician-supervised medical decision, not a supplement protocol. As far as I can tell, the field has moved past the original alarm but has not reached consensus on what should replace it.

### Growth Hormone Secretagogues

Peptide-based growth hormone secretagogues, including Tesamorelin, Ipamorelin, CJC-1295, and Sermorelin, stimulate the body's own GH production. MK-677 is an oral non-peptide alternative with the trade-off of increased appetite.<sup>[121]</sup> Nearly every person who has used a modest dose reports feeling subjectively better. Formal randomized controlled trial evidence at longevity doses is lacking.<sup>[122]</sup> That pattern (universal subjective improvement, absent formal proof) is itself worth weighing carefully.

The fundamental tension is biological. High GH and IGF-1 activity maintains the body in a pro-growth but also pro-aging state. Valter Longo points out that protein restriction, which lowers IGF-1 and GH signaling, is the only intervention that has consistently extended lifespan in rodents over 100 years of research.<sup>[123]</sup> If lowering the growth signal extends life in animals, raising it with secretagogues may do the opposite.

The biohacking community frames GH optimization as healthspan-preserving. The caloric restriction research community frames it as aging-accelerating. Both positions are backed by data in different species and at different doses, and neither can be definitively resolved from transcript evidence alone. Two groups are developing the same negative and seeing different images. I take that disagreement seriously; neither side is obviously wrong, and neither is obviously right.

The precautionary concern with chronic use is the potential to vascularize or accelerate growth of existing subclinical tumors. No human data confirm this risk at typical longevity doses, but the biological plausibility leads some clinicians to avoid these agents entirely.<sup>[124]</sup> PSA should be monitored when using any GH-stimulating agent, based on a case report of rapid PSA elevation on Sermorelin.<sup>[125]</sup> That case report is not proof of causation, but it is the kind of signal that warrants attention.

These compounds occupy a regulatory gray area. They are neither explicitly approved nor prohibited for human longevity use. They are compounded, off-label, or research-only in most jurisdictions. The book notes their existence and their debate, and leaves the recommendation to the prescribing physician.
