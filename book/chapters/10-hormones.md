# Chapter 10: Hormones

Here is a question worth sitting with. Men in their thirties today carry lower testosterone than men in their fifties did forty years ago. Not lower than they themselves will carry at fifty. Lower than a previous generation already had at that age. The decline is not genetic; it has happened too quickly for that. A 2020 report put the prevalence of clinical testosterone deficiency in adult males at somewhere between 10 and 40 percent.[1] That range spans a fourfold difference, which is : a prevalence estimate that cannot decide whether one in ten or four in ten men qualifies amounts to a concession rather than a measurement. The definition is contested. The testing is inconsistent. The reference ranges were built on a population that no longer exists.

Whatever the true count, the direction is not in dispute. Something in the way people live, eat, sleep, and absorb chemicals has been quietly hollowing out the hormonal signal that underwrites muscle, mood, metabolism, and reproductive drive in both sexes. The question is what. The answer turns out to be almost everything.

That is the territory of this chapter: the levers that raise or protect hormone function without a prescription pad. Testosterone-replacement therapy and growth-hormone secretagogues are prescription interventions covered as vocabulary in Chapter 14; they are not prescribed here. What follows is the lifestyle machinery, the thyroid layer most panels miss, the peculiar regulatory status of **DHEA**, the Women's Health Initiative story that left an entire generation of women undertreated, and the sexual-health science that rarely makes it into a doctor's visit.

I should say at the outset that this chapter covers more contested ground than most. Several claims rest on practitioner experience rather than randomized trials, and I will mark each one as it appears. The named concept is **hormonal health**, and it refers to the state in which the body's major endocrine signals (testosterone, thyroid hormone, DHEA, estrogen, and progesterone) are produced, converted, and received at levels adequate for the tissue that needs them, not optimal in the biohacker sense, which usually means maximal.

Think of it as a thermostat rather than a throttle: the thermostat rewards holding the room steady, not cranking it higher. Hormones sit on a narrow band, and the problems at the top of that band are different from the problems at the bottom but no less real. The more-is-better instinct, applied to this particular system, is precisely the instinct that breaks things. Every hormone chapter in the podcast literature eventually arrives at this point: the patient or the practitioner pushes for more, and the body pushes back. I will come back to that thermostat image in this chapter, because it is the most useful frame I have for how hormones actually operate.

## The lifestyle levers for testosterone

The two most impactful inputs are resistance training and sleep. The resistance-training story contains a detail I had not expected. Leg-focused resistance work drives the largest androgen-receptor upregulation, because skeletal muscle in the legs carries the highest androgen-receptor density in the body.[2] Not the chest. Not the arms. The legs. The receptor sits in the tissue where the most muscle mass lives, and the training that loads it coaxes the body into using the hormone it already has.

I keep returning to that point because it upends a common assumption. People associate testosterone with upper-body size and visible muscle, but the receptor density is highest in the legs.

Vigorous exercise also increases the transport of hormone precursors into the brain by roughly fourfold, improving tissue sensitivity to circulating testosterone independently of total levels.[3] That second finding reframes the whole testosterone conversation. A person can carry the same serum number and respond to it very differently depending on how much receptor waits at the other end. The conversation fixates on the lab report, but the number means little without the receptor density that determines what the body does with the molecule, the lab report is the thermostat reading, and the receptor is the furnace that either responds or does not.

Sleep continuity, not raw hours, determines how much testosterone the body produces overnight. The mechanism runs through the pituitary, which releases luteinizing hormone in pulses during deep N3 sleep. Those pulses trigger Leydig-cell testosterone synthesis in the testes. They work like timed firing cycles in a control system: each must complete before the next starts, and each adds to the running total, so fragmented sleep, even at adequate total duration, blunts the pulse amplitude and reduces the overnight yield.[4]

Each micro-awakening (noise, phone alerts, temperature swings, partner movement) shaves a fraction off the hormonal output that was supposed to accumulate by morning. That is one reason two people sleeping "eight hours" can wake up with meaningfully different testosterone. The governing signal is interruption count, not raw hours. The sleep architecture described in Chapter 7 matters here for a reason beyond cognitive restoration: it is the production window for the hormone that protects muscle, bone, and mood.

Dr. Kyle Gillett, an endocrinologist who appeared on the Huberman Lab podcast, proposed a framework he calls the Big Six: diet, resistance training, stress optimization, sleep, sunlight and outdoor exposure, and social or spiritual health, applied consistently over years rather than intensively over weeks.[5] The framework is notable less for any single element than for its insistence on duration. Hormones respond to a sustained environment rather than a sprint, and a three-week protocol covering all six levers will produce less lasting change than two or three held steady for two years.

Diet belongs here because Leydig-cell synthesis requires raw materials: dietary fat, vitamin D, zinc, magnesium, and vitamins A, K2, and E. Caloric restriction in the context of metabolic syndrome can improve testosterone by improving insulin sensitivity, but chronic undereating impairs synthesis outright.[6] The dose-response is not linear. Eating too little damages the system as surely as eating poorly, and the damage arrives without warning. Testosterone drifts downward without triggering a symptom alarm. A person restricting calories to lose body fat may succeed at the fat loss while silently undermining the hormonal system that protects the muscle they are trying to keep. The thermostat dims while the person watches the scale.

Then there is sunlight, and this one I read carefully. A study published in Cell Reports identified a skin-brain-gonad axis: UVB light hitting the skin triggers a signaling cascade that reaches the pituitary, stimulates luteinizing hormone release, and raises gonadal testosterone production in both sexes.[7] The proposed protocol from the podcast literature is twenty to thirty minutes of UVB exposure to maximal skin surface, two to three times per week, without glass or a windshield in the way, since both filter UVB. Sunglasses may also block relevant ocular signals, though this point is less established.

The caveat matters. The primary mechanistic data come from mice, and the human arm of the study focused on psychology and sexual behavior rather than testosterone assays. The finding is biologically plausible, but the testosterone-specific claim rests primarily on animal data; the human behavioral arm does not confirm it as a testosterone-raising protocol. Plausible is not proven. I note the distinction because this chapter contains several claims in that category, and it helps to mark each one plainly.

Overtraining works in the opposite direction. Excessive endurance volume suppresses testosterone by stressing the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. One guest on the Huberman Lab podcast cited roughly twenty to twenty-two hours per week of cycling as his personal suppression threshold, noting that the threshold is highly individual.[8] The mechanism reads clearly: the body interprets chronic physical stress the same way it interprets chronic caloric deficit, as a signal that reproduction is not the priority. The HPG axis does not distinguish between types of stress. It reads volume and recovery debt and adjusts the setting accordingly. Stress is stress, and the body counts every hour of it.

Nicotine cessation is cited as an amplifier of lifestyle-based testosterone improvements, alongside the less obvious suggestion that pursuing hormone optimization as a household unit, involving a partner, produces better compliance and outcomes than pursuing it alone.[9] I find the pairing interesting: one is a straightforward subtraction (stop smoking), the other is a social intervention applied to an endocrine problem. Neither is the kind of advice you typically receive from a lab report.

Andrew Huberman disclosed starting testosterone-replacement therapy at approximately 125 milligrams per week at age forty-five, framing it as a personal clinical decision after optimizing lifestyle factors first. He strongly discouraged use in men under roughly thirty-five who have not exhausted the non-prescription levers.[10] That age distinction matters. The decision to start TRT should be symptom-driven rather than solely threshold-driven. The functional indicators are difficulty building muscle, reduced libido relative to personal baseline, persistent low energy, and insulin resistance. No assay exists for androgen receptor density, so lab numbers alone cannot predict individual response.[11] Two men with the same testosterone level can have entirely different clinical pictures.

At therapeutic physiologic doses, TRT is reported to improve bone density, reduce frailty, and improve wellbeing; cardiovascular risk and prostate cancer risk are not clearly elevated in the available data, and the behavioral aggression popularly associated with testosterone is attributed to supraphysiologic dosing only.[12] The popular fear is overblown. The clinical caution is reasonable. TRT remains a prescription frontier, discussed as vocabulary in Chapter 14 and not prescribed here.

## The thyroid layer

I had assumed, going into this material, that thyroid and testosterone occupied separate clinical worlds. They do not. Thyroid dysfunction and low testosterone travel together more often than most clinicians check for, and multiple practitioners in the corpus describe cases where fixing one without the other produced incomplete results. The sequence matters: thyroid first, then sex hormones, because thyroid hormone sets the metabolic rate that everything downstream depends on.[13] Women with low free T3 are advised to prioritize thyroid optimization before addressing estrogen, progesterone, or testosterone, because thyroid is the energetic foundation enabling sex hormone function.[14] The master thermostat must be set before you can calibrate the room.

The core mechanism explains why the standard screening test misses so many people, and it deserves a slow pass. Roughly 80 percent of circulating T3, the active thyroid hormone, is produced outside the thyroid gland itself. The gland secretes mostly T4, a precursor. The liver and other tissues convert T4 to T3 through enzymes called deiodinases.[15]

Two of those enzymes, D1 and D2, perform the activating conversion. A third, D3, converts T4 to reverse T3, an inactive form that effectively parks the hormone without letting it do its job. The architecture is strange, and I want to be clear about why. The control system carries a feedback loop and a kill switch in the same circuit. If conversion fails, T3 drops even though the gland is producing adequate T4 and TSH may look entirely normal. Blood TSH may not accurately reflect thyroid hormone activity in individual tissues. The standard test reads the thermostat. It does not tell you whether heat is reaching the rooms.

That conversion step requires selenium. Selenium enters the methylation pathway and becomes selenomethionine and then selenocysteine, which catalyzes the deiodinase reaction. Zinc, magnesium, thiamine, iodine, and tyrosine are also cited as cofactors.[16] MTHFR gene variants, which reduce methylation capacity, may therefore contribute to impaired T4-to-T3 conversion by reducing available selenocysteine.[17] The practical implication deserves plain language: a person whose thyroid gland is producing adequate T4 can still be functionally hypothyroid if the conversion machinery is undernourished. The gland works. The wiring does not. That distinction clarifies something I had been puzzling over: how a person can test "normal" and still feel wrong.

When Gary Brecka worked with Dana White, the protocol centered on selenomethionine and methylated vitamin cofactors. Brecka reports normalizing White's T3 without thyroid medication.[18] That is a single case with no control, no blinding, and no published data. It illustrates the cofactor thesis. It does not prove it. Brazil nuts, sometimes recommended as a food-based selenium source, are discouraged in the corpus due to wide variability in selenium content per nut and mold contamination risk. Topical selenium is contraindicated due to toxicity risk.[19]

Underneath all of this sits a testing debate that I believe is genuinely unresolved. Mainstream endocrinology, represented in the corpus by Dr. Antonio Bianco on Peter Attia's podcast, holds that TSH plus free T4 is sufficient for diagnosing hypothyroidism. T3, in this view, has no role in initial diagnosis because it remains normal until late-stage disease.[20] Functional medicine practitioners, represented by Gary Brecka and McCall McPherson on Dave Asprey's show, argue the opposite: free T3 and reverse T3 are diagnostically essential, and TSH-only testing misses the majority of dysfunction. They recommend a full panel including TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3, and anti-thyroid antibodies for Hashimoto's screening.[21]

The mainstream position carries the weight of clinical guidelines. The functional position carries a plausible mechanism and a growing body of anecdotal clinical experience. Neither side has produced the definitive trial that would close the question, and the patient sits between them without a clear arbiter.

TSH itself occupies a narrow optimal range. Unlike blood pressure or glucose, where being below a threshold is simply good, both too-high and too-low TSH values cause problems. Precision matters more than simply being "within normal range."[22] Some patients remain symptomatic with normalized TSH, suggesting tissue-level T3 availability may differ from what the blood test reports. This is an emerging but contested clinical paradigm.[23]

Treatment is equally debated. Mainstream guidelines recommend levothyroxine, a T4-only medication, as first-line therapy. Functional practitioners advocate combination T3/T4 therapy or desiccated thyroid for patients who remain symptomatic on T4 alone. Some evidence supports combination therapy in selected patients; the mainstream position does not dismiss it outright but notes evidence gaps.[24] I mark this because the debate is not academic; it determines which prescription a symptomatic person receives.

What is not contested is the symptom picture, and I found this list quietly alarming. Low thyroid presents as unexplained weight gain, diffuse hair thinning, loss of the outer third of the eyebrow, menstrual irregularity, facial puffiness, and fatigue.[25] Every item on that list has another plausible explanation. Every one of those explanations is the kind a busy clinician might accept without digging further. **Hashimoto's thyroiditis**, the most common autoimmune thyroid disease, frequently presents with that same hair pattern and can, if untreated, damage ovarian tissue and impair fertility. A shortened luteal phase, under eleven days, is flagged as an early warning sign of ovulation disorders, warranting a workup that includes prolactin, thyroid, AMH, and PCOS evaluation.[26]

Fasting complicates thyroid interpretation, and the complication is worth stating carefully. Caloric restriction physiologically reduces T3 as an energy-conservation adaptation, a survival feature, not a disease. When someone on an aggressive intermittent-fasting protocol shows low T3, the lab result may reflect the body's intentional downshift rather than thyroid pathology.[27] The distinction matters because the treatments are opposite: one calls for thyroid medication, the other for more food. The pattern recurs throughout this chapter: the same lab reading, two opposite interpretations, two opposite prescriptions.

One practitioner in the corpus flags intermittent fasting as potentially contraindicated when HbA1c is below roughly 5.2, arguing that prolonged fasting at already-low blood sugar may suppress TSH and reduce T3 further.[28] That threshold is a practitioner heuristic, not a validated guideline, but the underlying logic tracks: the body reads caloric scarcity as a reason to conserve, and thyroid output is one of the first things it conserves.

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (parabens, phthalates, and synthetic fragrances) are associated with rising pediatric thyroid disorders in the corpus, with childhood chronic exposure flagged as a potential contributing factor.[29] The causal link is not firmly established. It is an association, not a verdict.

Claims about fluoride and bromide as thyroid suppressors at typical Western water-system concentrations are not supported by evidence at those exposure levels; evidence from high-fluoride regions cannot be extrapolated to typical drinking water, and dose distinctions matter here as they do throughout this territory.[30]

## DHEA: the precursor in regulatory limbo

DHEA and its sulfated form, DHEA-S, are produced by the adrenal glands and serve as upstream "mother hormones" that the body converts into testosterone, estrogen, and cortisol depending on the individual's genetics, **aromatase** activity, and baseline hormone levels.[31] Pregnenolone occupies a similar upstream position. Both decline with age, and functional practitioners use the decline as a marker of adrenal capacity and stress resilience.

DHEA-S has one practical advantage as a lab marker: it is resilient. Unlike cortisol or testosterone, which can swing wildly by the hour, DHEA-S is not significantly altered by acute exercise, dietary variation, or single-day stress.[32] The DUTCH test, a dried urine collection that captures twenty-four-hour cortisol and hormone patterns, is preferred by some practitioners over a blood snapshot for assessing DHEA and adrenal function.[33] I mention the DUTCH test here because it reflects a broader tension in this chapter: the question of whether a single blood draw can capture a system that operates in rhythms.

The conversion direction is the critical variable, and it turns the male and female stories into entirely separate accounts. In women, DHEA supplementation is more likely to raise testosterone and free androgens. In men, particularly those with elevated body fat or genetically active aromatase, DHEA preferentially converts to estrogen rather than testosterone.[34] I was struck by the implication: the same molecule, taken by two people, runs through different enzymatic pathways and produces opposite hormonal results. That sex-differentiated conversion is precisely the reason the male DHEA debate has never been settled.

Peter Attia and the host of More Plates More Dates stated on Attia's podcast that in males, DHEA supplementation produces no meaningful effect on testosterone levels and at best generates an estrogen spike. Brecka, Greenfield, and several functional medicine practitioners argue the opposite, citing case reports and clinical experience.[35] The peer-reviewed literature more consistently supports the skeptical position for males. The functional community relies on anecdotal and mechanistic reasoning. Both sides appear in the corpus. Neither has the trial that settles it. I leave it there.

Chronic stress compounds the picture. ACTH from the pituitary drives both cortisol and DHEA production in the adrenal glands; chronic stress both depletes DHEA reserves and perturbs the balance between cortisol and its hormonal counterweight.[36] Recovery from what some practitioners call adrenal burnout is described as a process taking three months to two years, requiring monitoring of cortisol timing and sex hormones throughout.[37] The timeline stayed with me: three months at the optimistic end, two years at the other. The endocrine system, once depleted, does not bounce back quickly.

Before starting TRT, the corpus recommends checking DHEA-sulfate and vitamin D3; if both are clinically low, correcting them first may explain suppressed testosterone in younger males without requiring replacement.[38] The logic is worth marking: correct the precursors before replacing the end product.

In women, the clinical picture is clearer and considerably more established. Intravaginal DHEA, sold as Intrarosa (prasterone), is FDA-approved for vaginal pain and genitourinary syndrome of menopause, acting locally to support the testosterone and estrogen pathway in genital tissue.[39] The safety profile extends further than many assume: vaginal estrogen or DHEA is described as safe for preventing recurrent urinary tract infections and improving genital health in women at any age, including those with BRCA mutations or a breast cancer history.[40]

At 50 milligrams per day orally, DHEA has been shown in some studies to restore total and free testosterone in women taking combined oral contraceptives, which suppress ovarian testosterone production.[41] That finding carries a quiet implication: a widely prescribed medication is suppressing an upstream hormone, and a widely available supplement may partially reverse the suppression. In post-menopausal women, replacing DHEA and pregnenolone as precursors (not just the end-point hormones like estrogen and testosterone) is recommended as part of comprehensive hormone-replacement therapy by several practitioners in the corpus.[42]

The regulatory picture deserves its own paragraph, and I confess it is the kind of story that makes you wonder whether anyone in charge has ever sat in the same room and compared notes. In the United States, DHEA is sold over the counter with a GRAS designation. In Canada, it is classified as Schedule 1, more restricted than anabolic steroids. USADA bans it identically to testosterone for competitive sport drug testing purposes, regardless of its over-the-counter availability in the US.[43] An athlete who buys it at a health-food store in Florida and tests positive at a USADA-sanctioned event faces the same sanction as someone who injected testosterone. Same molecule, three jurisdictions, three verdicts. I concede that, after reading this far, the incoherence no longer surprises me.

The **TRIIM** trial, published in Aging Cell and co-authored by Steve Horvath, tested growth hormone plus DHEA plus metformin and reported epigenetic age reversal measured by the Horvath clock.[44] Multiple podcast guests referenced this trial as evidence for DHEA's role in longevity. The counterargument, articulated by Attia and by Derek of More Plates More Dates, is that DHEA contributed nothing in that protocol and that the active agent was recombinant growth hormone. No controlled data isolate DHEA's contribution. The trial is interesting as a whole. The DHEA signal within it is entirely unresolved.

## The WHI story and women's hormones

On a summer day in 2002, the Women's Health Initiative held a press conference announcing the early termination of its combined hormone-replacement therapy arm after an increased breast cancer signal was detected in women receiving estrogen plus progestin. Within months, HRT prescriptions collapsed. Physicians stopped writing them. Women stopped asking for them. An entire category of treatment was functionally removed from most women's care. I want to be precise about what happened next, because the details matter.

What received far less coverage was the other arm, the one studying hysterectomized women who took estrogen without progestin. That arm showed a non-significant reduction in breast cancer risk, the opposite direction of the combined arm.[45] I expected a nuance. What I found was something larger. Two studies involving two different populations had produced findings pointing in opposite directions, and the press conference compressed them into a single headline carrying only the frightening half. The distinction between combined and estrogen-only therapy, and between the populations studied, evaporated in that urgency, and that distinction mattered for every treatment decision that followed.

Fewer than 4 percent of women currently eligible for hormone therapy are on any form of it.[46] That figure does not reflect a medical consensus against HRT. It reflects a cultural overcorrection driven by a press event that conflated two different findings. I read that number and sat with it. Four percent.

Women's testosterone begins declining in the thirties, not only at menopause. At ovulatory peak, women carry roughly ten times more testosterone than estradiol when expressed in the same units.[47] I paused on that ratio, because it runs counter to the popular understanding of testosterone as predominantly a male hormone. Combined oral contraceptives suppress ovarian testosterone production further and raise sex-hormone-binding globulin, reducing free testosterone even below the already declining baseline. By the time perimenopause arrives, the hormonal substrate for libido, mood, bone density, and skin integrity has been thinning for years, a trajectory whose skin-collagen consequences are addressed in Chapter 16.

Libido loss at menopause is often framed as psychological, a loss of desire. The corpus frames it differently and more precisely: it is a raw-material deficit. Estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone act as neurotrophic hormones in the brain's sexual circuitry. When they decline, the circuitry does not disappear, but it runs without fuel.[48] The framing shifts the clinical question entirely: the treatment for a psychological problem is therapy, while the treatment for a substrate deficit is the substrate, and the two diagnoses call for different interventions.

The question of how long to continue HRT is itself contested. One source in the corpus advocates continuing indefinitely, arguing that bone density gains reverse rapidly on cessation and that stopping may destabilize arterial plaques.[49] Major menopause societies do not mandate stopping at ten years but recommend periodic risk reassessment. The "no limit" position is progressive and evolving, not current consensus. The decision is individual, and reasonable physicians can disagree about the right stopping point. What is no longer defensible is withholding the option from an entire population on the basis of a press conference that misrepresented the evidence it was announcing.

## Sexual health and the nitric oxide connection

Erectile dysfunction affects approximately 40 percent of men by age forty and roughly half by fifty, and clinicians in the corpus report seeing it increasingly in men as young as thirty-five.[50] Most people assume this is primarily hormonal. It is not. It is primarily vascular. Erections require rapid dilation of penile blood vessels, driven by nitric oxide, which is produced by endothelial nitric oxide synthase and requires NADPH (derived from **NAD**) and L-arginine as substrates.

Here is the number that rewrites the pharmacology: approximately half of men given PDE5 inhibitors like sildenafil (Viagra) or tadalafil (Cialis) do not respond, because their bodies cannot produce adequate nitric oxide in the first place.[51] The drugs sustain a signal that may not be there. PDE5 inhibitors do not create nitric oxide; they prevent its breakdown by inhibiting the enzyme that degrades cyclic GMP, the downstream molecule that keeps smooth muscle relaxed and blood flowing in. If the upstream nitric oxide is absent, there is nothing for the drug to preserve.[52] It is as if the thermostat were set correctly but the furnace had no fuel: the control signal fires, and nothing responds. I find this reframing striking, because it turns an assumed pharmacological failure into a diagnostic clue.

NAD deficiency offers one mechanistic explanation: NADPH is the electron donor for the eNOS enzyme that synthesizes nitric oxide, so a deficit in the NAD pool structurally impairs the enzyme's capacity to fire.[53] The mechanism is plausible. The clinical evidence for NAD precursor supplementation specifically improving erectile function in humans remains limited. The gap is a common pattern in this material: a clear biochemical pathway, an absent clinical trial.

ED is also an early marker for cardiovascular disease, sometimes preceding a cardiac event by years, because the endothelium that cannot dilate penile arteries is unlikely to be performing well elsewhere.[54] The mouthwash finding covered in Chapter 8 connects here: antiseptic mouthwash kills the oral bacteria that convert dietary nitrate to nitrite, a precursor step in nitric oxide production. The same pathway that governs blood pressure governs penile vasodilation.[55] I mention the link because it captures a theme of this chapter: the body's control systems are more intertwined than any single specialty's scope can cover.

Greater muscle mass improves erectile function through better endothelial health, improved insulin sensitivity, and increased nitric oxide production.[56] That connection links the training chapters to the hormonal outcomes covered here: the same exercise that builds muscle also maintains the vascular system on which erections depend. Daily low-dose tadalafil is described by multiple sources as one of the most effective vascular-health tools for men, with proposed benefits extending to cardiovascular and potentially cognitive health through sustained nitric oxide signaling. The off-label longevity use lacks large randomized trial support.[57]

Testosterone deficiency impairs libido and mood more strongly than it impairs the mechanical ability to achieve an erection. I noticed this distinction because it runs counter to the popular assumption. The hydraulics depend more on nitric oxide production, vascular health, and psychological state than on testosterone level alone. Severe hypogonadism can impair erections indirectly, particularly nocturnal erections, but the primary gatekeeper is the blood vessel, not the hormone.[58]

For men with secondary hypogonadism (where the problem is low luteinizing hormone from the pituitary rather than testicular failure) clomiphene and enclomiphene offer a non-replacement route. These drugs stimulate the HPG axis, can double or triple testosterone levels over three to six months, and preserve fertility because they work through the body's own signaling rather than overriding it; neither is a scheduled substance.[59]

Enclomiphene specifically preserves natural LH and FSH signaling, which also maintains endogenous pregnenolone and DHEA production, avoiding the neurocognitive side effects some men report when those upstream hormones are suppressed by exogenous testosterone.[60] The distinction between replacement and stimulation matters: one overrides the body's own control system, the other works through it.

Overtraining suppresses testosterone via HPA-axis dysregulation: elevated cortisol chronically produces secondary hypogonadism that resembles primary testicular failure but reverses with adequate recovery.[61] The fix is rest; the thermostat resets itself when you stop overloading the system.

PT-141, also called bremelanotide, works through an entirely different pathway. It is a melanocortin receptor agonist that acts in the brain to improve sexual desire and arousal in both men and women. It received FDA approval as Vyleesi for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women. Nausea is a common side effect.[62] Its mechanism is entirely central; it does not touch the vascular machinery. That distinction matters: PDE5 inhibitors address the blood-flow signal; PT-141 addresses the desire signal. When the first fails, it may be because the problem was never vascular. Oxytocin, a related neurochemical, is identified in the corpus as a component of erection and arousal distinct from its more commonly associated role in emotional bonding.[63]

## Women's sexual health

Approximately 85 percent of post-menopausal women report sexual dysfunction, and even among women aged eighteen to forty, the rate is roughly 35 percent.[64] The contributors are hormonal, structural, relational, and neurological. The hormonal piece has been covered already: declining estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone reduce the brain's sexual circuitry to a low-fuel state. But anatomy and education play roles that are harder to quantify, and the numbers in what follows carry their own weight.

The clitoris contains over 8,000 nerve roots, extends internally with crura along the labia minora, and includes vestibular bulbs that engorge with blood during arousal. It is far more extensive than the visible external structure suggests. Approximately 90 percent of women reach orgasm through external clitoral stimulation; this is a normal physiological pattern, not an exception.[65] In one study, 41 percent of Gen Z men could not accurately identify the clitoris on a diagram.[66] The journal was not cited, but the statistic, if even approximately correct, identifies an education gap with practical consequences for partnered sexual function, the anatomy exists while familiarity with it does not.

Spontaneous sexual desire, the kind that arrives unbidden, predominates in men and in roughly 15 percent of women. Most women experience responsive desire, where arousal builds in response to engagement rather than preceding it, a difference in wiring, not a deficit.[67] I notice the framing because it shifts the conversation from deficit to difference. Couples who grasp it can stop treating a desire mismatch as a problem with someone and start treating it as a difference in wiring. One source in the corpus recommends simultaneous hormone optimization of both partners when one initiates TRT or HRT, arguing that hormonal mismatch within couples is a leading driver of relationship breakdown.[68]

Topical vaginal and labial testosterone, applied to the labia and clitoris, is used off-label to increase local blood flow and tissue health in women. Dosing must be individualized and supervised.[69] I note this as another case where a hormone is used locally for tissue-specific effects, reinforcing the theme that hormones operate at the level of the target tissue, not just the bloodstream.

For women on combined oral contraceptives who report low libido, the mechanism is direct: the pill suppresses ovarian testosterone production and raises **SHBG**, reducing free testosterone to levels that leave the desire circuitry underfueled. DHEA at 50 milligrams per day has been shown in some studies to partially reverse this suppression.[70] The thermostat analogy applies here in its starkest form: the medication adjusts one setting and inadvertently dims another.

The landscape of women's sexual-health interventions stretches well beyond what the evidence can currently support. Shockwave therapy, **PRP** injections, and various regenerative approaches are reported in the corpus with high patient satisfaction but limited controlled data.[71] The pattern is familiar by now: patient demand runs ahead of clinical proof, and the gap between what people want and what trials have confirmed is wide, even as the science in this area is still young.
