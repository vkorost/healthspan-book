# Chapter 8: Heart and Metabolism

I had not expected the list to be this long. Fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, ApoB, Lp(a), VO2max, coronary calcium score, DEXA-measured visceral fat, homocysteine, nocturnal blood pressure dip, oral nitric oxide capacity, continuous glucose variability, the lean-mass hyper-responder phenotype, estrogen-linked lipid shifts. Each one, according to somebody in this literature, is the thing your standard panel is failing to measure, the signal that matters most, the number that would have caught the problem a decade earlier. I counted thirteen before stopping. If even half are genuinely useful, the standard metabolic blood draw is missing rather a lot.

Consider the case of Dana White, president of the UFC. Three blood pressure medications held his numbers in range, but nothing about the underlying problem had changed. The clinical term was brittle hypertensive: controlled on the surface, unstable underneath. Then Gary Brecka, a biohacker and self-described human biologist, ran a methylation panel, adjusted a handful of nutrient cofactors, and White reportedly discontinued all three medications.<sup>[1]</sup>

That story is a single anecdote. No control group. No blinding. No independent verification. It circulated because of who Dana White is, not because of what was measured. But the question it raises is the right one for this chapter: what if the standard way cardiovascular and metabolic risk gets managed is looking at the wrong layer entirely?

Here is how I understand the clinical sequence. Glucose creeps up. A doctor orders HbA1c. The number comes back normal. Everything looks fine. Years later the number is no longer normal, and by then the patient has a disease. The argument running through the research gathered here is that every major cardiovascular and metabolic risk factor follows the same pattern: the early signal exists, but the standard panel does not capture it.

## The insulin signal

Steven Gundry, a former cardiac surgeon turned longevity clinician, puts the timeline bluntly: fasting insulin rises six to fourteen years before HbA1c or fasting glucose become abnormal.<sup>[2]</sup> Six to fourteen years. That is a window wide enough to fit an entire childhood into.

For more than a decade, the body is compensating, the pancreas overproducing insulin to force glucose into resistant cells, and the standard screening test cannot see any of it. Think of it as a circuit running at twice the rated voltage, where every connection still holds and every light stays on, but the insulation quietly degrades inside the walls. By the time HbA1c crosses the diagnostic threshold, the wiring has been overloaded for years.

The mechanism running underneath that silent window is not subtle. Insulin resistance sits on a spectrum. At one end, cells respond efficiently to insulin and clear glucose from the blood without difficulty. At the other end, cells have stopped responding, insulin piles up in the bloodstream, and the pancreas is in overdrive.

Every postprandial glucose spike along the way inflicts a small amount of mitochondrial damage, drives glycation, and triggers systemic inflammation.<sup>[3]</sup> The body's answer is to release still more insulin, which forces glucose into liver, muscle, and fat. When those storage tissues are full, the glucose has nowhere productive to go, and the excess current finds its way into less forgiving pathways.

What follows is a cascade, and a self-amplifying one at that. When cells become resistant, the pancreas overcompensates, driving blood glucose transiently low. The resulting energy crash triggers hypothalamic hunger signals and cravings for high-glycemic food, which spikes glucose again, which triggers more insulin, which drives another crash.<sup>[4]</sup> I read that sequence twice before I trusted the timescale. Years of this cycle humming along, invisible beneath a normal HbA1c. The diagnostic light stays green while the system behind it shorts out, one meal at a time.

Gundry advocates targets for these markers that are considerably stricter than most published guidelines: fasting insulin below six, ideally below three to four, and HOMA-IR below one.<sup>[5]</sup> Standard clinical cutoffs sit higher, at HOMA-IR below two or two and a half. The disagreement is genuine. Functional medicine practitioners set tighter targets; most evidence-based guidelines use the broader range.

What nobody disputes is that fasting insulin moves years before glucose does, and that most standard panels do not include it. The test that catches this early is straightforward enough: fasting serum insulin combined with HOMA-IR.

So why does no one order it? The explanation I encountered across multiple channels is regulatory rather than scientific: no drug is approved specifically for hyperinsulinemia, so the test has no therapeutic endpoint attached to it. If you cannot prescribe for a finding, the finding tends not to get ordered. A structural gap, not a scientific one. The fuse is available, yet it rarely gets installed.

There are visible signs too, signs a doctor or patient can spot without ordering a blood draw. Elevated fasting insulin is associated with skin tags, acanthosis nigricans (the darkened skin folds at the neck and armpits), colon polyps, and increased visceral fat.<sup>[6]</sup> Each of these reflects insulin's growth-hormone-like signaling. A body swimming in insulin grows things it should not, in places it should not. The body is trying to tell you. You just have to know what to look for.

### The strongest lever

The lifestyle intervention with the strongest claim in this territory is strength training. Contracting muscle draws glucose from the bloodstream independently of insulin, through the GLUT4 transporter pathway.<sup>[7]</sup> This is not a modest effect. A post-meal walk of ten minutes, a set of calf raises, any contraction of large muscle groups blunts the postprandial glucose peak by routing glucose directly to the working tissue.<sup>[8]</sup>

The muscle acts as a kind of ground wire for excess current, draining glucose out of circulation before it can do damage. Over time, building more muscle mass increases the total surface area of insulin-sensitive tissue, giving the body a larger sink for circulating glucose. The bigger the ground, the more current it can safely absorb.

The reverse is equally important and equally relentless. Age-related sarcopenia, the progressive loss of roughly three percent of muscle mass per year after forty, steadily reduces the body's capacity to absorb glucose.<sup>[9]</sup> Less muscle means a smaller glucose sink, which means higher insulin output for the same meal, which means faster progression toward resistance. I am not sure the connection between muscle loss and insulin resistance gets the emphasis it deserves in most discussions of metabolic health. The insulin problem and the muscle problem are the same problem, viewed from opposite ends of the wire.

There is a nutritional trick here that deserves more attention than it typically gets. It has been validated on continuous glucose monitors: a tablespoon of vinegar in water before a meal can reduce the glucose spike of that meal by up to thirty percent, by slowing starch digestion.<sup>[10]</sup> Starting a meal with non-starchy vegetables achieves a similar effect, slowing gastric emptying through fiber content and blunting the subsequent glucose excursion.<sup>[11]</sup> Neither qualifies as a medical intervention. Both are sequencing. I mention them because they cost nothing and appear to work.

And there is one dietary pattern for what it does not show on a glucose monitor at all. Fructose in liquid form (from juice, smoothies, or concentrated fruit drinks) bypasses the bloodstream entirely and travels directly to the liver, where it is converted to triglycerides and uric acid.<sup>[12]</sup> It does not spike glucose on a continuous glucose monitor because it never enters the blood as glucose. But the uric acid it produces is itself a driver of insulin resistance.

The monitor reads normal. The damage proceeds. This is a particularly unsettling version of the chapter's theme, and I suspect it is more common than the literature acknowledges: a monitoring tool working exactly as designed, every reading accurate, and still missing the problem.

### The overfeeding window

One study makes the speed of metabolic damage uncomfortably clear. Young, otherwise healthy men were overfed by twelve to fifteen hundred extra calories a day for five days, heavy on saturated fat and sugar. In less than a week, brain insulin resistance was detectable by MRI. No meaningful weight gain had occurred. Not a pound that would trouble a bathroom scale, and yet the metabolic damage to hypothalamic insulin signaling was already visible on imaging. The dysfunction preceded any change on the scale.<sup>[13]</sup>

Five days. That is the window. I had assumed metabolic dysfunction begins with obesity, that the weight arrives first and the metabolic trouble follows it some months or years later. It appears to be the other way around entirely. The dysfunction starts with the diet, and the body composition catches up afterward, the circuit already faulted long before the breaker trips.

### Berberine and the metformin question

For readers already managing blood sugar, one compound keeps surfacing in the clinical data, and it is not the compound you would expect. **Berberine**, a plant alkaloid derived from goldenseal and barberry, activates AMPK, the same metabolic sensor targeted by **metformin**. Multiple randomized controlled trials have compared berberine head-to-head with metformin for HbA1c reduction, and several show comparable or slightly superior results for berberine.<sup>[14]</sup>

That comparison is worth sitting with. A compound available without prescription, performing on par with the most widely prescribed diabetes drug in the world in the trials that have tested them side by side. I had to reread the comparison to make sure the direction of the result was what it appeared to be. The head-to-head data are real, though the studies are smaller and shorter than the metformin literature, and berberine lacks the decades of post-market safety data that metformin carries. Promising, but not yet proven at the same scale.

Metformin itself appears as a frontier compound in Chapter 14, where its longevity implications and the ongoing TAME trial are treated as vocabulary. Here, the relevant point is the comparison: berberine does a similar thing to blood sugar through a similar pathway, and it sits on a supplement shelf.

### Snacking and the eating window

A pancreas that never rests is a pancreas that wears out. Every snack between meals triggers another round of insulin secretion, and each round is a small demand on a gland with finite capacity. The meal-timing chapter (Chapter 6) covers the eating-window mechanics in full.<sup>[15]</sup>

## Arterial protection

If the insulin section of this chapter is about a clock that ticks for six to fourteen years before anyone notices, the arterial section is about a clock that ticks across an entire lifetime. Atherosclerosis is both concentration-dependent and time-dependent: any lipoprotein particle under seventy nanometers carrying ApoB can penetrate the endothelial wall, and the damage compounds with every year of exposure.<sup>[16]</sup>

Humans are born with relatively low ApoB, which rises with age. The longer the exposure, the greater the plaque burden, which is why Peter Attia and mainstream cardiology argue that lifelong low LDL is far more protective than reducing it late.<sup>[17]</sup> This is cumulative wiring damage, not a single event, accumulating one particle at a time in one direction only.

The mechanism itself is mechanical, and once you see it clearly it is hard to stop thinking about it. Dense LDL particles get pushed into arterial walls at sites of turbulent blood flow, exactly the kind of flow that occurs at arterial branch points and bends. Macrophages arrive to clean up the intrusion, engulf the particles, and become foam cells.

Foam cells aggregate into fatty plaques. Soft, uncalcified plaques pose the highest rupture risk, which is why a coronary calcium score of zero does not rule out dangerous plaque.<sup>[18]</sup> The whole sequence has the relentlessness of a power grid accumulating micro-faults: each one survivable on its own, no single particle catastrophic, but the damage does not reset overnight, and the total load grows in one direction only.

There is a gut connection that deserves attention here. Small dense LDL can bind LPS, the endotoxin that leaks from a permeable gut. Macrophages that attempt to engulf the LDL-LPS complex cannot fully digest it, which accelerates foam cell formation.<sup>[19]</sup> This links gut permeability directly to the initiation of atherosclerosis, a connection explored more fully in Chapter 9. A leaky gut can short-circuit an artery. I did not expect to write that sentence.

Whether LDL is independently causal or merely a bystander in an inflammation-driven process is one of the sharpest disagreements in this entire field. Attia and mainstream cardiology, supported by Mendelian randomization studies that control for confounders genetically, treat ApoB-containing lipoproteins as directly causal for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.<sup>[20]</sup> Gundry, on the other side, calls cholesterol an "innocent bystander" patching inflammation-damaged vessels, citing patients with LDL above four hundred and zero plaque. Brecka and others argue that metabolic health and insulin matter more than LDL alone.

The minority positions are worth hearing, and I am not going to pretend this disagreement is settled, because it is genuinely live. But the weight of evidence is not evenly distributed. Mendelian randomization, multiple drug classes lowering LDL through different mechanisms, and decades of randomized controlled trial data all point to the same proportional risk reduction for each unit of ApoB lowered. Not a coin flip.

### Diet and the lipid panel

For those managing LDL through lifestyle rather than pharmacology (and I recognize that is a trade-off each person resolves differently), several findings from the source material deserve attention. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat produces greater LDL reduction and appears more cardioprotective than replacing with monounsaturated fat alone, based on multiple meta-analyses.<sup>[21]</sup> A Mediterranean-keto approach that substitutes saturated fats with olive oil, avocado, and nuts produces a better cardiovascular risk profile than high-saturated-fat keto while maintaining the metabolic benefits.<sup>[22]</sup>

Dietary fiber lowers LDL modestly.<sup>[23]</sup> The effect is real but small relative to what pharmacological options can achieve, which is worth knowing if you are trying to decide whether diet alone can do the job.

One small dairy detail is worth pausing on. Cream has a neutral LDL effect, while butter increases LDL. The difference is attributed to the loss of the milk fat globule membrane during churning; that membrane appears to protect lipid profiles.<sup>[24]</sup> A single finding, but it neatly illustrates how processing alters a food's metabolic impact well beyond its macronutrient content. I had not expected the difference between cream and butter to come down to a membrane that survives in one and gets destroyed in the other.

One pattern worth flagging in the cardiovascular context is the lean-mass hyper-responder phenotype. In some individuals on very-low-carbohydrate diets, LDL rises not because of saturated fat intake but because of fuel partitioning: the body starts using LDL as an energy-transport shuttle when carbohydrate is absent. These tend to be lean, insulin-sensitive people, and carbohydrate reintroduction reverses the elevation rapidly. The clinical significance of this LDL pattern is still being studied. Nick Norwitz's peer-reviewed n=1 Oreo self-experiment, covered in Chapter 5, put a striking data point on the phenotype.

ApoE4 carriers on ketogenic diets may face a different problem entirely: LDL that rises while HDL falls, which is the opposite of what most ketogenic advocates promise.<sup>[25]</sup> For these individuals, individual lipid tracking during any ketogenic transition is not optional. It is the difference between metabolic optimization and silent arterial loading.

### Women and the menopause shift

One detail matters considerably for women approaching menopause, and it is one I think the broader health conversation underweights. Estrogen promotes LDL receptor upregulation in a manner similar to a PCSK9 inhibitor; when estrogen declines at menopause, ApoB rises, sometimes sharply.<sup>[26]</sup> Measuring ApoB and Lp(a) around the menopause transition is not optional testing. It is the moment the cardiovascular risk profile shifts, and the standard lipid panel may not capture the magnitude of that change until the arterial damage has compounded.

Statins, the pharmacological standard for LDL lowering, are addressed in Chapter 14 as part of the prescription frontier. This chapter stays with what lifestyle and measurement can do before pharmacology enters.

## Blood pressure

The nocturnal blood pressure dip is one of the body's quiet cardiovascular resets. During sleep, parasympathetic activation slows the heart rate, relaxes the vasculature, and lets blood pressure fall. Think of it as a transformer stepping the voltage down for the overnight shift, giving the whole system a few hours at lower load. Eating close to bedtime sustains sympathetic tone and prevents this dip from occurring.<sup>[27]</sup> A continuous-monitoring blood pressure study found that stopping eating three hours before bed was associated with approximately twenty percent lower cardiovascular event risk compared to eating later.<sup>[28]</sup>

That finding is observational, and the study itself is not named by the speaker who cites it, which means I cannot independently verify it from the source material alone. But the underlying mechanism is well understood: digestion keeps the sympathetic nervous system engaged, and a heart that never fully downshifts at night accumulates damage over months and years. A transformer that never steps down wears differently from one that does.

I did not know, before reading through this material, that the brain has its own blood pressure sensor. The OVLT, a structure in the hypothalamus, monitors pressure and triggers hormonal cascades through vasopressin to regulate renal fluid and salt balance.<sup>[29]</sup> This is the machinery behind the relationship between sodium and blood pressure, though that relationship turns out to be more individually variable than population-level guidelines suggest. Some people are genuinely salt-sensitive. Many are not. The sodium debate is a real one, but its resolution is individual, not universal.

Homocysteine, the amino acid intermediary that rises when methylation pathways stall, irritates the arterial endothelium and narrows vessels.<sup>[30]</sup> The Dana White case at the opening of this chapter sits squarely in this territory. Brecka's argument is that nutrient deficiencies in methylation cofactors, particularly B vitamins and selenomethionine, were driving the vascular dysfunction that three medications were failing to correct. The mechanism is plausible. It would explain why the medications managed the symptom without touching the cause. But the evidence for it amounts to one famous person and one clinician's word. I leave it to you to weight that accordingly.

Sauna use has cardiovascular mortality data behind it, and not from a small trial. The Finnish sauna research, covered in full in Chapter 12, shows a dose-response relationship between sauna frequency and cardiovascular mortality reduction: more sessions per week, lower risk, in a graded and consistent fashion.<sup>[31]</sup>

The relevance for this chapter is that heat stress trains the same vascular flexibility that blood pressure management depends on. Repeated dilation and contraction of blood vessels under thermal load appears to function as cardiovascular conditioning in its own right, a kind of exercise for the arterial walls themselves.

### The eighty-five-year study

The Harvard Study of Adult Development offers a different kind of cardiovascular data entirely, and it is the kind that none of the preceding biomarkers can account for. This eighty-five-year longitudinal study, begun in the late 1930s, tracked hundreds of men across their entire adult lives. Its headline finding was that the quality of social relationships was a stronger predictor of health and happiness in old age than cholesterol, blood pressure, or smoking.<sup>[32]</sup>

I paused on that finding longer than on any biomarker in this chapter. Eighty-five years of data, hundreds of lives tracked from youth to old age, and the variable that predicted healthy aging better than anything a lab could draw from a vein was whether the person had someone to talk to.

That is not a claim about supplements or biomarkers, and I am aware it sits oddly in a chapter otherwise built on lab values. It is an epidemiological finding suggesting the cardiovascular system responds to stressors no lipid panel can measure. Chronic loneliness elevates cortisol, sustains sympathetic activation, raises resting blood pressure, and accelerates atherosclerosis through inflammatory pathways. The mechanism is stress physiology, and the prescription, insofar as there is one, is relational.

## Cardio fitness as a longevity signal

The mortality differential between the lowest and highest fitness quartiles dwarfs the effect sizes of blood pressure, cholesterol, BMI, and smoking: cardiorespiratory fitness, measured by VO2max, is the single strongest predictor of all-cause mortality in the epidemiological literature.<sup>[33]</sup> I will ask you to read that sentence a second time. Not a moderate contributor. Not one factor among many. The strongest predictor. Even the step from the lowest quartile to the next one up produces a reduction in mortality risk larger than most pharmaceutical interventions deliver. The full data are covered in Chapter 2.

The point, as I read it, is not the training protocol (which lives in the movement chapters) but the framing. Cardiovascular health is often discussed regarding what to remove, lower the LDL, lower the blood pressure, lower the blood sugar, whereas cardiorespiratory fitness is the variable to build.

The heart is a muscle, and the demand placed on it by vigorous training causes eccentric loading of the left ventricle, which thickens cardiac muscle and increases stroke volume.<sup>[34]</sup> Greater stroke volume means more oxygen delivered per heartbeat. That adaptation comes from building capacity rather than from avoiding risk factors. I find this a useful reframe for a chapter dominated by things that go wrong: the goal is upgrading the generator, not simply reducing the resistance in the circuit.

Two people with the same blood pressure, the same LDL, and the same fasting glucose can sit at vastly different points on the mortality curve depending on their VO2max. Someone at fifty milliliters per kilogram per minute and someone at thirty are both "non-disabled" by medical definitions, yet they have vastly different functional capacities and vastly different mortality risk.<sup>[35]</sup> The standard medical panel captures none of that difference: two identical reports on paper, and two vastly different prognoses in practice.

## Visceral fat

Not all fat is the same organ, and the difference between the two main kinds matters enormously for everything discussed so far in this chapter. I suspect this is a distinction that most people have heard stated but not fully absorbed.

Subcutaneous fat, the kind sitting under the skin, is metabolically relatively benign. Visceral fat, the kind wrapping around the liver, kidneys, heart, and pancreas, is an endocrine organ in its own right, continuously releasing inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids into the bloodstream.<sup>[36]</sup> The difference between the two is roughly the difference between insulation wrapped neatly around a wire and insulation packed directly against a circuit board. One protects. The other generates heat where you least want it.

What makes it matter practically is that you can be lean by every external measure and still carry dangerous amounts of visceral fat. Steve O'Reilly's "bathtub model" describes how when subcutaneous fat storage capacity is overwhelmed, excess energy overflows into ectopic depots around organs, and this ectopic fat strongly predicts coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes.<sup>[37]</sup>

You can be overweight by BMI with low visceral fat and remain metabolically healthy. The reverse is also true and, as far as I can determine from this literature, more dangerous: normal BMI with high visceral fat is the phenotype that standard screening misses entirely.

Gundry estimates that approximately half of normal-weight people are metabolically unhealthy by this definition, carrying hidden visceral fat despite normal HbA1c and fasting glucose.<sup>[38]</sup> That estimate is Gundry's, not a universally cited figure, and it should be read with the appropriate caution. But the concept it points to is well established in the broader literature: fasting insulin (target below six, and certainly below ten) and HOMA-IR are the tests that unmask the skinny-fat phenotype. The standard metabolic panel does not. Once again, the early signal exists, and the standard workup does not include it.

### The self-reinforcing loop

Here is where the chapter's logic turns circular, and the circularity is real. Visceral fat is insulin-resistant. Unlike its subcutaneous counterpart, it does not suppress lipolysis when insulin rises, and so it continuously releases free fatty acids into the liver, driving systemic insulin resistance.<sup>[39]</sup>

This creates a self-reinforcing loop: insulin resistance promotes visceral fat accumulation, and visceral fat promotes insulin resistance. Cortisol from chronic stress accelerates the cycle further.<sup>[40]</sup> It is the metabolic equivalent of a short circuit that generates its own heat: the more it runs, the hotter the wire, and the hotter the wire, the faster the insulation fails.

The loop has a cardiac dimension that deserves close attention. Perivascular fat, the fat that accumulates on and around the coronary arteries themselves, correlates directly with coronary artery disease and local vascular inflammation. Gundry, drawing on his years of experience as a cardiac surgeon, describes seeing scar tissue on coronary arteries in patients who appeared lean externally.<sup>[41]</sup>

Enlarged perivascular fat cells secrete myeloperoxidase, which oxidizes LDL and decreases adiponectin, a hormone that normally keeps blood vessels flexible and thermogenesis active.<sup>[42]</sup> Less adiponectin means stiffer vessels, higher blood pressure, and less metabolically active fat tissue. The visceral fat problem is not a weight problem. It is a vascular one. And it compounds in the same arteries the rest of this chapter is trying to protect.

The measurement tool that catches visceral fat is DEXA, covered in Chapter 1. BMI, for all its ubiquity, cannot distinguish between visceral and subcutaneous compartments. It sees total mass. It has no idea where the mass sits. I keep returning to this limitation because it is the same blind spot wearing different clothes in every section of this chapter. A DEXA scan can make that distinction, and it is one of the reasons the measurement chapter opens this book.

### The training order

One practical question is whether exercise order matters for fat loss. It appears that it does. A twelve-week RCT in obese young males found that performing strength training before cardio, rather than the reverse, produced significantly greater abdominal and body fat loss.<sup>[43]</sup> The study is small. It is limited to one demographic.

It would be a mistake to build too much on it. But the finding aligns with metabolic logic: strength work depletes glycogen first and shifts the body toward fat oxidation, so the cardio session that follows draws preferentially from fat stores, and the order of those operations matters.

Fasted cardio, by contrast, does not appear to confer a meaningful advantage for fat loss, despite its persistent popularity. A four-week study by Schoenfeld and Aragon in college-age women found that when total daily calories were equated, fasted and fed cardio produced the same result.<sup>[44]</sup> The morning run on an empty stomach may feel more virtuous. The body, as best I can tell from this data, does not seem to agree.

### Fat storage and women

I notice that this chapter keeps returning to the same uncomfortable point: where the problem sits matters more than how much of it there is. Fat storage location shifts with age in women, and the shift matters more than the quantity.

Before menopause, the body preferentially deposits fat in peripheral sites like the hips and thighs, which are metabolically less dangerous. After menopause, the distribution shifts toward abdominal and visceral depots, driven largely by the accompanying hormonal changes.<sup>[45]</sup> This redistribution is one of the mechanisms behind the sharp increase in cardiovascular risk that follows menopause. It is not weight gain per se that does the damage. It is where the weight goes.

Some Asian populations show a related phenotype, and a worrying one. Subcutaneous fat storage capacity is genetically limited in these groups, forcing all excess energy into visceral depots.<sup>[46]</sup> The result can be metabolically worse than classical obesity, because there is no subcutaneous buffer to absorb the overflow. Normal BMI, dangerous visceral load, and no external sign that anything is wrong. The circuit looks clean from the outside while the overload is entirely internal.

## Nitric oxide

I have been tracing separate threads through this chapter, but they converge on the same target. Blood sugar damages the vasculature. LDL invades it. Blood pressure stresses it. Fitness strengthens it. Visceral fat inflames it. Nitric oxide is the molecule that holds the line, the one signal that tells blood vessels to relax and stay open.

The body produces nitric oxide through three enzyme systems: endothelial NOS, which drives vasodilation; inducible NOS, which is associated with inflammation; and neuronal NOS, which supports brain function.<sup>[47]</sup> The balance among these three determines whether nitric oxide is protective or destructive, and what struck me in reading through this material is how easily it tips.

When the system works properly, endothelial NO relaxes blood vessels, lowers blood pressure, and improves blood flow to every organ in the body. When it fails, the vasculature stiffens, blood pressure rises, and erectile dysfunction is often the first clinical sign.

That last point is not a digression; it may be the most practically important sentence in this section. Erectile dysfunction is fundamentally a vascular problem: the inability of blood vessels to dilate on demand via nitric oxide signals systemic vascular dysfunction that is likely affecting the coronary arteries as well.<sup>[48]</sup>

It is one of the earliest detectable markers of cardiovascular disease, often appearing years before a cardiac event. By now I trust you see the pattern: the early signal exists, and it is often ignored or treated as a local symptom rather than read as a systemic warning. The blood vessels in one part of the body are telling you what the blood vessels everywhere else are doing.

### The mouthwash problem

Here is a piece of information that sounds trivial and is not. The oral nitrate-to-nitrite-to-nitric-oxide pathway, one of the body's major routes for producing this protective molecule, depends on specific bacteria living on the back of the tongue that reduce dietary nitrates from beets, arugula, and leafy greens into nitrite, which then gets converted to nitric oxide systemically, and antiseptic mouthwash kills them.<sup>[49]</sup>

I would not have guessed that a bottle of mouthwash in a bathroom cabinet could be quietly working against the cardiovascular system, but the data are fairly straightforward on the point. Multiple human studies have shown that habitual mouthwash use raises blood pressure.<sup>[50]</sup> The mechanism is direct: destroy the oral bacteria that convert nitrate to nitrite, and you cut off a major source of systemic nitric oxide. Blood vessels lose their signal to relax. Pressure goes up. A hygiene product undermining a system it has nothing to do with.

The practical alternative cited across multiple sources is oil pulling with coconut or olive oil, which cleans the mouth without sterilizing the bacterial colonies the cardiovascular system depends on.<sup>[51]</sup> It is not the most glamorous cardiovascular intervention in this chapter, but it may be one of the simplest to act on. Chapter 16 addresses the oral-health implications from the dental side.

### Dietary nitrate and sunlight

The food sources of nitric oxide precursors are well established and reassuringly ordinary: beets, watermelon, arugula, pumpkin seeds, and extra virgin olive oil.<sup>[52]</sup> Of these, beetroot concentrate has the most striking performance data. It provides a measurable VO2max boost in amateur athletes through nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, opening capillary beds that were previously constricted. The effect disappears in elite athletes who already have highly developed NO pathways, but for recreational exercisers the vasodilation is real and measurable.<sup>[53]</sup>

Nasal breathing during exercise produces more nitric oxide than mouth breathing, because the paranasal sinuses produce and deliver nitric oxide through nasal airflow.<sup>[54]</sup> The physiology is direct: breathing through the nose during moderate-intensity work is, for all practical purposes, a free vasodilator available to anyone willing to keep their mouth closed.

Sun exposure stimulates nitric oxide release directly from the skin, causing vasodilation and increasing arterial elasticity.<sup>[55]</sup> This is a cardiovascular benefit entirely distinct from vitamin D production; the nitric oxide comes from the skin itself, not from the liver or kidneys. The connection between sun exposure and lower cardiovascular mortality has been observed in population studies, and the nitric oxide pathway is one proposed mechanism for it. I cannot say the data are conclusive, but the direction is consistent.

There is also a metabolic crossover that ties the end of this chapter back to its beginning, and it took me a second pass through this material to see how neatly the loop closes. Elevated insulin suppresses nitric oxide production, causing vasoconstriction, increasing peripheral resistance, and raising blood pressure.<sup>[56]</sup>

This creates yet another self-reinforcing loop: insulin resistance reduces NO, reduced NO raises blood pressure, and elevated blood pressure compounds cardiovascular risk. The insulin signal from the first pages of this chapter and the nitric oxide signal near the last are the same vascular system failing at two different points in the same circuit.

### Red light and glucose

A small study found that exposing subjects to red light before an oral glucose tolerance test blunted the blood sugar peak. The proposed mechanism runs through mitochondrial glucose utilization: red light dissociates nitric oxide from cytochrome c oxidase, freeing the enzyme for oxygen binding and ATP production, which in turn allows cells to process glucose more efficiently.<sup>[57]</sup>

The research comes from Dr. Glen Jeffery and Mike Powner, and blue or short-wavelength light produced the opposite effect in the same study, worsening glucose disposal rather than improving it. The evidence is preliminary. The study was small, and the mechanism is not definitively established. But it sits at the intersection of two well-characterized systems (light-driven mitochondrial activation and glucose disposal) which, to my reading, makes it worth watching rather than dismissing.

Every thread in this chapter leads back to the same vascular wall. Insulin damages it silently for a decade before glucose tests move. Lipoproteins invade it one particle at a time across an entire lifetime. Blood pressure stresses it while the body sleeps. Visceral fat inflames it from the inside, wrapped around the very arteries it is degrading. And nitric oxide is the molecule that keeps the whole circuit from going dark.

The standard panel checks glucose, cholesterol, and blood pressure. The early signals (fasting insulin, ApoB, VO2max, visceral fat distribution, oral nitric oxide capacity) sit outside that panel. The instruments exist. The question is whether anyone orders them before the damage is done.
