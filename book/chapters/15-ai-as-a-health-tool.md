# Chapter 15: AI as a Health Tool

Here is a question that kept surfacing as I read through this material, and that I could not quite put down: can a machine that has never had a body tell you what is wrong with yours?

The answer, as of 2026, depends entirely on what you mean by "tell." A large language model can summarize a journal paper in seconds flat. It can cross-reference a thousand biomarkers at once. It can scan every approved medication on the market and flag repurposing candidates that no human team would have thought to test. What it cannot do is take your pulse, notice that your hands have begun to tremble, or order the follow-up test that catches what the algorithm missed. These are not minor omissions but the difference between information and care. I assumed, going in, that the boundary between those two would be obvious, though it proved otherwise.

The question, then, is not whether AI belongs in health. It already lives there, woven into drug-repurposing pipelines, wearable algorithms, diagnostic imaging software, molecular design platforms, and the search bars that people type their symptoms into at two in the morning. The real question is where AI helps, where it misleads, and how you tell the difference.

Consider, as an opening case, David Fajgenbaum, who nearly died five times from Castleman disease before he stopped waiting for someone else to find a treatment.<sup>[1]</sup> A physician and researcher at the University of Pennsylvania, Fajgenbaum built Every Cure, a nonprofit that uses artificial intelligence to scan every approved medication on the market for repurposing candidates in rare and treatment-resistant diseases. The logic behind it is as blunt as a hammer, and I appreciate that bluntness. There are thousands of approved drugs, thousands of orphan conditions, and not enough humans or hours to test every combination. So the AI does the initial matching, and clinicians do the confirming.

The approach has identified candidates that no research team would have prioritized on its own, because no research team can hold the full pharmacopeia in working memory at once.<sup>[2]</sup> I paused at that. A single nonprofit, armed with pattern-matching software, is doing what the entire pharmaceutical apparatus was not structured to attempt.

**AI for health** spans a spectrum, and its width is part of the difficulty of writing about it. At one end sits a person pasting a journal abstract into a chatbot for a plain-language summary. At the other sits a system redesigning a drug molecule atom by atom. Between those poles lies everything this chapter covers: research acceleration, biological profiling, personalized coaching, commercial health platforms, lab-scale genomics, diagnostic imaging, mental health triage, and the risks that ride alongside each of them.

## The Research Accelerator

The most validated use of AI in personal health is also the least dramatic. That contrast says something about where the real value actually concentrates.

Here is what the least dramatic use looks like in practice. A person reads a paper, does not understand it, feeds the text to a large language model, and receives a plain-language explanation in seconds. Fifteen years ago, that same person would have needed a graduate-level background or a physician willing to sit down and translate. Now the barrier is a browser tab.<sup>[3]</sup>

That shift happened remarkably fast, faster than I would have guessed. Access to primary literature was, for most of the history of medicine, a professional gate. Patients received conclusions. Physicians held the reasoning. AI dissolved that arrangement in approximately two years. A person investigating their own NLRP3 inflammasome markers can now query an AI system with their personal inflammation context, identify candidate interventions like EGCG, and arrive at a clinician visit carrying a specific, partially informed question rather than a vague complaint.<sup>[4]</sup> That is a fundamentally different kind of patient, and I am not sure the medical system has fully reckoned with that change. Whether it is ready for that patient is a separate question, and not one the dataset resolves.

Dave Asprey recommends a specific workflow for navigating AI's limitations: use at least two different AI systems, Claude and ChatGPT among them, and have one generate health advice while the other critiques it.<sup>[5]</sup> The method amounts to simple adversarial checking. It exists because AI systems hallucinate. They produce confident-sounding statements about events that never occurred, cite studies that do not exist, and invent dosages for real compounds.

Asprey himself recounts encountering a hallucinated fact produced by an AI he was using for health research, a fabrication plausible enough that without independent verification it would have been accepted and acted upon.<sup>[6][7]</sup> Think of it as a musician who plays every note with perfect confidence and occasionally inserts a note that does not exist in the score, producing a composition that is, in places, simply wrong. You would not know unless you checked the sheet music yourself.

Cross-referencing between models catches some of these fabrications, though not all. Two models trained on overlapping data can share the same blind spot, the way two recordings of the same performance will both miss a note that was never played in the studio. The recommendation that surfaces across multiple channels is clear: treat AI as a research accelerator, never as a primary source for clinical decisions.<sup>[8]</sup>

The same principle extends to consumer-facing research tasks. Asprey suggests querying AI to aggregate batch oxidation data across fish oil brands or to compare label claims against third-party testing results.<sup>[9]</sup> The AI compiles and cross-references faster than any human shopper could manage. It can also fabricate the data with exactly the same fluency it uses to report the real thing. That is the core difficulty, and the verification step is not optional.

The practical problem is that people do not use AI the way experts recommend and typically type a question, receive an answer, and act on it. The gap between "use AI to check your thinking" and "let AI do your thinking" is precisely where the risk concentrates, and the gap is not closing.

Multiple voices in the dataset raise a concern that I think deserves stating without softening. They warn that overreliance on AI for reasoning, rather than using it to sharpen reasoning you have already done, may erode cognitive capacity over time. Asprey draws a sharp line: using AI to strengthen your arguments and check your logic is enhancement; handing your thinking to AI entirely is outsourcing. Greenfield and his guest Jordan Wood echo the concern, framing the distinction as an active discipline that requires resistance, not passive acceptance.<sup>[10][11]</sup>

I should concede that the evidence base for that concern is not yet from controlled studies. It is expert intuition about a tool that has existed for roughly three years. But the concern maps onto a well-documented cognitive pattern: skills you stop practicing atrophy. If you stop doing arithmetic because your phone has a calculator, your arithmetic degrades. If you stop reasoning through a health question because an AI provides an answer, the same principle should apply. The scale of the effect is unknown, though its direction is not seriously contested.

## Reading the Body at Scale

The more ambitious AI applications try to read biological data the way a language model reads text, searching for patterns across dimensions that no human clinician can hold in mind simultaneously.

The ambition of these platforms is striking. The evidence underneath them is persistently thin. It is worth walking through three of them, because the gap between promise and proof is where the reader's judgment will be most useful.

Dr. Tony Wyss-Coray, the Stanford neuroscientist whose parabiosis work showed that young blood can rejuvenate old brains (see Chapter 11), co-founded Vero Biosciences and built the Vero Compass platform.<sup>[12]</sup> It combines AI with organ-age biological signatures drawn from wearable data, blood panels, and clinical records to predict which organ in a given body is aging fastest, and which interventions might slow that decline.

The concept is elegant: instead of treating the body as a single chronological age, decompose it into organ-specific aging trajectories and prioritize the one that is failing first. Think of an orchestra where every section (strings, brass, woodwinds, percussion) ages at its own tempo. The conductor who can hear which section is falling behind can intervene before the whole performance deteriorates. That is the premise of Vero Compass, and as a premise it is genuinely attractive.

The concept is also, as of this writing, unproven, and the gap between concept and proof matters.

Vero Compass is an early-stage product without long-term clinical outcomes data.<sup>[13]</sup> No trial has demonstrated that acting on its organ-age predictions improves healthspan or prevents disease. The platform represents a hypothesis about how AI could personalize medicine, not evidence that it does. Wyss-Coray's scientific credibility is genuine; his parabiosis research changed the field. But that credibility does not transfer automatically to a commercial product resting on a different kind of evidence, and I would not want any reader to assume otherwise.

A similar pattern holds for AI-based brain volumetric imaging. Dr. Cyrus Raji at Washington University uses AI to measure brain volume from MRI scans and predict cognitive decline trajectory.<sup>[14]</sup> The technique detects structural changes earlier than standard clinical assessment allows. A brain losing volume in the hippocampus at an accelerated rate may be heading toward cognitive decline years before symptoms surface. The measurement itself is real and genuinely useful as a diagnostic marker. The clinical question (whether intervening on the basis of that early measurement changes the outcome) remains open, and it is not the kind of question that resolves quickly. Detection is not prevention until someone proves the link (see Chapter 11 for the dementia-prevention levers that might fill that gap).

A third entry in the same category is the TruDiagnostic TruHealth Test, which applies AI analysis to approximately 1,000 biomarkers drawn from epigenetic testing to identify which interventions are most likely to move an individual's longevity markers.<sup>[15]</sup> The product is commercial, the biomarker count is striking as a number, and the validation question is identical to the ones I have already posed above. Can AI, reading a thousand data points simultaneously, identify an intervention that a physician reading twenty data points would miss? Plausibly. None has been shown to improve outcomes in a controlled trial.

The honest summary across all three platforms, if I am reading the evidence correctly, comes down to the same gap. The measurement is real, earlier and more comprehensive than what came before, and the clinical value of acting on that earlier measurement has not been established. That distance between measurement and outcome is where the next decade of evidence will either vindicate these platforms or quietly deflate them.

Studies have also shown that biological age can be estimated from facial appearance using AI analysis.<sup>[16]</sup> No specific citation accompanies that claim in the source material, which is itself a useful data point. The technology exists. The validation is vague. The commercial incentive to offer a "face scan" that tells you your biological age is obvious enough to need no elaboration. Whether the output is more useful than a mirror and a blood panel is, in my view, an entirely separate question.

## The Personalized Coach

Ben Greenfield, the biohacker and endurance athlete, tested a more aggressive version of the AI-as-health-tool idea. He fed all of his personal health data into an AI system: epigenetic test results from TruDiagnostic, lactate threshold numbers, biometrics, training logs, supplement schedules. The AI generated a complete daily training and supplement plan, adjusted dynamically as new data came in. A physician using the same approach achieved a Guinness World Record speed ascent of Kilimanjaro.<sup>[17]</sup>

One person, one record, one mountain.

The result is real. The attribution to AI coaching is the climber's own claim. The sample size is one, and it cannot be anything else, because the intervention is personalized by definition and the outcome is singular. I do not dismiss the achievement, but I cannot build much on it either. No controlled comparison tested whether the same person with the same physiology and the same commitment would have performed worse with a human coach, or with no coach at all but equivalent training volume. The anecdote demonstrates that AI can synthesize complex personal data into a coherent plan. It does not demonstrate, and cannot demonstrate from a single case, that the plan was superior to what a skilled human coach would have produced.

## The Commercial Layer

Where research use is cautious and coaching is anecdotal, the commercial layer operates at full volume. The confidence of the claims, I could not help observing, rises in rough inverse proportion to the evidence behind them.

Dave Asprey built axo.health, an AI health platform trained on his own content and designed to accept users' lab results and genetic test data for personalized recommendations. Gary Brecka built "Ask Gary Anything," an AI trained on Brecka's own content library, accepting the same kinds of inputs.<sup>[18][19]</sup> Both products represent a bet that a large language model fine-tuned on one practitioner's philosophy can deliver useful health guidance at scale. Both accept the kinds of data (genetic tests, blood panels, symptom histories) that a physician would normally use to make clinical decisions, though neither product is one.

The structural problem here deserves plainer language than it usually gets: the training data. When Asprey's AI is asked about supplements, it draws on Asprey's views about supplements. When Brecka's AI is asked about methylation, it draws on Brecka's methylation framework. If either corpus overweights a claim or underweights a risk, the AI reproduces that bias with the polished appearance of algorithmic objectivity.

The user sees a clean, confident answer. The user does not see the training data that shaped it. It is as though you tuned a radio to receive on a single frequency and mistook the signal for the entire broadcast.

Neither product has been independently validated. The recommendation from the source material itself: all outputs should be verified by a qualified practitioner.<sup>[20]</sup> That recommendation, coming from the people selling the products, deserves careful attention. I am inclined to take it at face value while noting the obvious conflict.

A similar product, the Neo-7 protocol platform, pushes the concept further still. It uses AI combined with long-read exome sequencing of blood and urine to prioritize which of the hallmarks-of-aging pathways are most dysfunctional in a given individual and then designs personalized peptide protocols to address them.<sup>[21]</sup> The language surrounding it is dense: "precision-based immuno-molecular augmentation." The clinical trial evidence is not cited, and the product is commercial. The promise is that AI can read your genome, identify your weakest aging pathway, and prescribe accordingly. That promise is substantially ahead of the published evidence, and I see no reason to close that gap with enthusiasm.

A hybrid model proposed by Ben Greenfield through the Ways2Well clinic attempts to split the difference between pure AI and pure physician care.<sup>[22]</sup> In this arrangement, AI handles data analysis and patient matching while a human physician remains in the clinical chain for every patient. The pitch is that this combination delivers personalized care comparable to concierge medicine, which runs 100,000 to 150,000 dollars per year, at a fraction of that cost and without becoming what Greenfield calls "internet pill mills" that prescribe without physician oversight.

I want to be direct about the commercial landscape here. Asprey owns axo.health. Brecka monetizes his AI. Greenfield has a financial stake in Ways2Well. The Neo-7 platform is a commercial product. Each creator is selling the tool they recommend. That does not mean the tools are worthless. It means the enthusiasm behind each recommendation should be weighed against the financial incentive producing it, and it means the verification step that every one of these creators nominally endorses is the only thing standing between a useful recommendation and an expensive mistake.

## AI in the Lab

The applications with the strongest scientific footing are consistently the ones farthest from the consumer, and that pattern held all the way through the material, a reminder that the closer AI gets to a paying customer, the weaker the evidence tends to become.

The convergence of AI with molecular biology and genetic engineering has made it possible to run experiments at genome scale: testing every gene across the entire genome to identify mechanisms governing immune function, cancer response, and autoimmunity, at a speed that was not previously achievable.<sup>[23]</sup> This is infrastructure for discovery, not a consumer product. The experiments that once took a research team months to design and years to execute can now be run computationally in weeks, with the wet-lab validation concentrated on the most promising candidates rather than spread thinly across the entire possibility space.

Here is the tension that runs through all of AI-assisted biomedical research, and I want to state it plainly because it is easy to miss behind the excitement. The pace of candidate identification has accelerated dramatically. The pace of clinical validation has not. AI can screen a million compounds overnight. The randomized controlled trial that proves one of them works in a human body still takes a decade. The tempo mismatch is like a recording studio that can produce an album in a single afternoon while the mastering engineer still needs months to finish the work. Speed at one stage does not compress the stage that follows it.

Here is a concrete example, and I include it because the promise and the caveat are both worth holding in mind simultaneously. Stanford reportedly developed a next-generation GLP-1 agonist using AI-guided molecular design, targeting only hunger centers in the brain without producing the nausea that plagues current GLP-1 drugs.<sup>[24]</sup> The report comes from a news article, not a peer-reviewed paper. The clinical stage of the compound is unstated. If the molecule works as described, AI will have shortened the design phase from years to months and solved one of the most common reasons patients discontinue GLP-1 treatment. But the compound still needs the same human trials every other drug needs (see Chapter 14 for the GLP-1 mechanism and the finding that only eight of a hundred patients stay on current GLP-1 drugs past three years).

Every Cure's drug-repurposing pipeline follows the same logic at the other end of the drug lifecycle. Instead of designing new molecules, it scans existing approved medications for uses nobody has tried. AI identifies candidates faster than any human team could manage.

But identification, as I kept reading, turns out to be the easy part. Validation, the slow, expensive, and irreplaceable part, remains human work.<sup>[25]</sup>

## AI and Mental Health

A quieter AI application is emerging in psychiatric care. It addresses a problem that deserves more attention than it typically receives in the popular health literature.

Tara Mind is a platform that uses AI to identify patients currently on antidepressants who are not improving and redirects them toward more appropriate treatments, with the goal of matching biomarkers to therapeutic interventions.<sup>[26]</sup> The premise targets a genuine clinical failure. A substantial proportion of patients on first-line antidepressants do not respond, and the standard clinical approach amounts to sequential trial-and-error: trying one medication after another until something works or the patient abandons the process entirely. If AI can shorten that loop by matching patient profiles to likely responders, the clinical value would be clear and immediate.

The platform has not published peer-reviewed outcome data in the source material. The concept is sound. The execution is unproven. By this point in the chapter, I suspect the reader has already noticed the refrain. That repetition is itself informative.

Open Evidence operates in a related space, providing physicians with AI-curated summaries of randomized controlled trial evidence on topics like testosterone replacement therapy and anabolics.<sup>[27]</sup> This tool is designed for clinicians, not patients. It sits inside the clinical workflow rather than outside it. That positioning, in my reading of the evidence, matters more than any single feature of any platform discussed so far. An AI that summarizes trial evidence for a physician who can evaluate the summary is structurally different from an AI that hands a patient a treatment recommendation the patient has no framework to evaluate.

That distinction points toward what may be the most important conclusion in this entire chapter, and it deserves marking clearly. The highest-value AI health tools may not be the ones patients interact with directly. They may be the ones that make physicians faster, better informed, and less likely to miss a relevant finding buried deep inside a literature no clinician can read in full.

## AI and the Brain

Two findings sit at the intersection of AI and neuroscience, one foundational and one cautionary. The foundational one gets far less attention in the popular discussion, an imbalance that says something about how the field presents itself.

The foundational finding: computational models originally built for AI, specifically reward prediction error models, provided the key insight into how dopamine drives learning, motivation, and decision-making in the human brain.<sup>[28]</sup> The math that teaches a machine to optimize a reward function turned out to describe the same process that teaches a human being to repeat a behavior that felt good. The prediction error (the gap between expected reward and actual reward) is what triggers dopamine release.

When the outcome is better than expected, dopamine fires. When the outcome matches expectation, dopamine holds steady. When the outcome disappoints, dopamine drops. That signal drives learning in machines and in human brains by the same underlying logic. That convergence is genuinely remarkable when you sit with it for a moment, and I confess it stopped me in my reading. A tool we built to teach machines had, it turned out, been describing how we teach ourselves all along.

That convergence now informs treatments for depression and behavioral disorders.<sup>[29]</sup> Dopamine dysregulation in depression can be understood through the same computational framework that an AI uses to learn from feedback. AI did not merely borrow from neuroscience. Neuroscience borrowed back. That exchange deserves remembering when the discussion turns, as it always does, to the risks.

The cautionary finding is speculative but widely cited. Iain McGilchrist, the Oxford psychiatrist and neuroscientist, argues that technology and AI keep individuals locked in constant left-hemisphere task mode, suppressing right-hemisphere activity responsible for meaning, mystery, and holistic perception.<sup>[30]</sup> The claim rests on McGilchrist's hemispheric lateralization framework, which posits that the left hemisphere handles focused, analytic, detail-oriented processing while the right hemisphere handles broad contextual awareness, metaphor, and the sense of meaning that gives life its felt texture.

That framework is itself debated within neuroscience. Hemispheric specialization is real but considerably more nuanced than a clean left-right division suggests. The specific assertion that AI drives right-hemisphere suppression is an extrapolation from that framework, not a finding from controlled study. It belongs in the category of plausible concern rather than established risk. McGilchrist's underlying point (that modern life overweights analytic task completion at the expense of meaning and contemplation) does not require the AI-specific mechanism to carry weight.

A related and more firmly grounded worry involves social connection. Andrew Huberman and Marc Brackett flagged AI-mediated social interaction replacing face-to-face human connection as a longevity risk, citing human relationships as among the strongest predictors of health and lifespan.<sup>[31]</sup> The Harvard Study of Adult Development, running for 85 years, found that the quality of social relationships is the single strongest predictor of health and happiness in old age (see Chapter 8). If AI substitutes for human contact rather than supplementing it, the trade is bad on its face. A chatbot that keeps someone company is not equivalent to a friend who notices you have lost weight, asks how you are sleeping, and insists you see a doctor.

Whether that substitution is happening at a scale that affects population health is not yet established. The causal relationship between AI use and human relationship quality has not been directly studied. The concern rests on two premises that are individually well-supported: relationships protect health, and AI changes how people interact. The connection between those two premises is assumed, not proven.

The assumption feels more plausible each year, and the people raising it have no financial stake in the answer.

## The Mattress and the Algorithm

The most quietly successful AI health application may be the one nobody recognizes as AI at all.

The Eight Sleep Pod uses machine learning to track individual sleep patterns and dynamically adjust mattress temperature across sleep stages throughout the night.<sup>[32]</sup> Head elevation adjusts automatically for snoring. The system learns preferences over time and adapts without requiring the user to do anything at all. It is, by any functional definition, an AI making real-time decisions about a person's sleep environment based on biometric data it collects while that person sleeps.

The distinction from the commercial health platforms discussed earlier matters, and it is structural rather than cosmetic. The mattress does not give advice. It does not generate a protocol for the user to evaluate or a recommendation the user must weigh against their own judgment. It adjusts a single physical variable (temperature) in response to measured data about sleep stage and movement. The feedback loop is closed entirely. The algorithm acts. The sleeper sleeps.

The product is sponsor-integrated content in the dataset, meaning the hosts who discuss it have financial relationships with the company.<sup>[33]</sup> That caveat applies and should not be dismissed.

But the underlying concept (an algorithm that adjusts a physical environment in response to biometric data without requiring interpretation from the user) represents, I suspect, the form AI health tools are most likely to take in the near term. Invisible, embedded, doing one thing well rather than trying to replace a physician. The wearable that adjusts a recommendation, the thermostat that responds to heart rate, the lighting system that tracks circadian timing: these are small, closed-loop applications where AI's pattern-matching strength is matched to a task with defined boundaries and low stakes per individual decision. They are, in a sense, like a metronome keeping time behind a musician. The musician still plays. The timing is held steady by something that never loses the beat.

## The Self-Treatment Problem

The sharpest disagreement in the source material is whether AI can safely substitute for a physician, and on this point I am not going to equivocate. The evidence says it cannot, not yet, and the people making the case for it are the same people selling the tools.

Dave Asprey presents AI as a viable tool for interpreting lab results and guiding thyroid or hormone optimization in people who cannot access functional medicine doctors.<sup>[34]</sup> The access argument behind that claim is real and worth acknowledging directly. Functional medicine physicians are expensive, geographically concentrated, and often not covered by insurance. Many people who would benefit from a comprehensive thyroid panel beyond the standard TSH test (see Chapter 1) cannot get one interpreted by someone who knows what to do with the results.

The specific example Asprey gives is T3 management, the active thyroid hormone. The safety caution that accompanies it is direct: self-treatment with thyroid medications without physician supervision is hazardous.<sup>[35]</sup> T3 has a narrow therapeutic window. Too much accelerates heart rate, disrupts bone metabolism, and can trigger arrhythmia. An AI that reads a lab value and suggests a dose adjustment monitors none of those downstream effects: it takes no pulse, orders no follow-up ECG, and does not notice that the patient's hands are shaking.

A similar risk applies to AI-generated supplement protocols for contested conditions. Asprey describes parents of autistic children using AI to identify personalized amino acid and supplement regimens rapidly.<sup>[36]</sup> The speed is real. The desperation of parents seeking help for a child with a complex condition is real. But autism supplement treatment is a contested area where the evidence base is thin, the emotional stakes are high, and the risk of acting on a confident-sounding but wrong recommendation is compounded by the vulnerability of the population receiving it. AI recommendations in this context require medical review. The source material says so explicitly. Whether the parents who receive those recommendations actually seek that review is the practical question nobody in the dataset can answer.

The pattern holds across every self-treatment scenario the source material presents, without exception. AI lowers the barrier to information. It does not lower the barrier to judgment. A person with no medical training who receives an AI-generated protocol has more information than they had before and no better framework for evaluating whether that information applies to their specific body, their specific medications, their specific contraindications. Physician oversight is not a formality and not a legal disclaimer. It is the part of the system that catches what the algorithm cannot see, and in my reading of this material it is the part most likely to be skipped by the people who need it most.

## The Semaglutide Signal

One finding deserves its own section because it illustrates how AI-scale data analysis can surface patterns that no clinical trial was designed to find.

Hospital records from approximately 10,000 patients with cocaine use disorder showed that those who happened to be taking semaglutide, the GLP-1 agonist, had reduced substance use.<sup>[37]</sup> Nobody had prescribed semaglutide for addiction. The patients were taking it for diabetes or weight management. The association emerged from large-scale record analysis, the kind of pattern-matching across enormous datasets that AI enables and that no human epidemiologist could perform at the same speed or scale. That, it seems to me, is what AI in health looks like when it is working exactly as it should.

The proposed mechanism connects GLP-1 to satiety pathways most closely associated with swallowing behaviors (eating and drinking). If the same satiety signal suppresses reward-seeking behavior broadly, it could dampen the compulsive loop that drives substance use.<sup>[38]</sup> The mechanism is hypothesized, not confirmed. Randomized controlled trials are now underway. The clinical implications remain unproven. I raise it here because the distinction between hypothesis and proof is, by now, the central theme of this chapter.

But the method of discovery is the part of the story that generalizes. A drug prescribed for one condition produces an unexpected effect in an entirely different condition. That effect is invisible in any individual patient's chart. It becomes visible only when thousands of charts are read simultaneously and a statistical pattern emerges from the noise. AI does the reading. A human team formulates the hypothesis. A clinical trial tests it. Each step requires the one before it, and none replaces any of the others. It is, in the end, like a sound engineer who isolates a faint harmonic in a dense recording that no human ear could have picked out of the mix. The signal was always in the data. You needed the right equipment to hear it.

The semaglutide-and-addiction signal may prove to be a coincidence, a confound, or a genuine breakthrough. The process that found it (AI scanning records at a scale no research team could manage by hand) is already established as a valid method of hypothesis generation. It tells the trial where to look rather than replacing it. That division of labor may be the most honest description of AI's role in medicine that this chapter can offer.

## Where This Stands

AI models, it turns out, have measurable psychological profiles. When tested with standardized human psychological instruments, different AI systems show different propensities for manipulation, distortion, and reliability.<sup>[39]</sup> Model selection matters in ways the casual user cannot easily see: the tool you ask is, in a real sense, part of the answer you get. Two AI systems given the same health question will produce different answers, weighted by different training corpora, shaped by different failure modes. The user who asks only one model and treats the response as authoritative has been used by the tool rather than using it.

Here is my honest reading of AI in health as of 2026. It accelerates research. It reads data at scales no human can match. It personalizes plans from complex inputs. And it hallucinates with the same confidence it applies to factual statements. The strongest applications sit in discovery pipelines and environmental controls, where AI handles the pattern-matching and humans handle the deciding. The weakest sit in unsupervised self-treatment, where the absence of human judgment turns AI's speed into a liability. That is a reason to use the technology correctly.

***The evidence that AI improves health outcomes when embedded in clinical or research workflows is accumulating steadily. The evidence that AI improves health outcomes when used as an unsupervised replacement for clinical judgment does not yet exist.***

No one in the dataset, not the enthusiasts and not the skeptics, argues that AI should be trusted without verification. Asprey, who sells an AI health product, says verify with a practitioner. Brecka, who sells an AI health product, says the same thing. Greenfield, who has a stake in an AI-physician hybrid clinic, says keep the physician in the loop. The disagreement among them is not about whether verification matters. It is about how much verification is enough, who provides it, and what happens when the person receiving the AI output decides to skip it entirely.

The answer depends on the stakes. Reading a paper summary carries low stakes. Comparing fish oil brands carries low stakes. Adjusting a thyroid dose does not. Designing a peptide protocol for aging does not. Choosing supplements for a child with autism does not. The tool does not know the difference. You do.
