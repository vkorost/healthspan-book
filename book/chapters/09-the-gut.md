# Chapter 9: The Gut

The majority of a typical stool sample, by weight, is bacteria.<sup>[2]</sup> Not food residue. Not water. Not the various digestive secretions you might reasonably expect to dominate the output of a digestive system, but bacteria. Living ones, dead ones, and the metabolic remnants of ones that were performing useful biochemistry right up until their departure. I read that fact and then read it again, because it rearranges the picture rather thoroughly.

I had always thought of it as a processing facility, something that takes food in at one end and extracts nutrients on its way to the other. closer to a compost bed in full ferment, teeming with several trillion organisms that break things down, release gases, compete for nutrients, and manufacture compounds the rest of the body has quietly come to depend on.

A woman in Japan reached the age of 117. When her doctors, her family, and eventually the obituary writers noted her diet, nobody singled out the sushi or the rice. What kept coming up was the miso. The pickled vegetables. The fermented soybean paste she ate every morning for the better part of a century.<sup>[1]</sup> One life is not a clinical trial. But the pattern underneath that life, the one connecting fermented food consumption to better health outcomes across whole populations, has been accumulating in the data for decades. It is the kind of observation that makes a researcher stop, count, and then count again.

That pattern now has a name, though perhaps not the name you would expect. The story of gut health is not really about the stomach at all. It is about the several trillion organisms living below it, collectively called **the microbiome**, and about the startling range of body systems they appear to influence: immunity, mood, blood sugar, inflammation, even the pace at which the brain deteriorates. The field is young. Parts of it are oversold. More than a few of the loudest voices in this space have something to sell. What follows is what the sourced evidence actually supports, sorted by the quality of the data behind it.

## The invisible organ

The gut microbiome is a community of bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses residing primarily in the large intestine. Its aggregate mass is comparable to the liver's. If you have ever pulled back the mulch on a healthy garden bed, you will have seen something analogous: a dense, layered community invisible from the surface, yet responsible for nearly everything that thrives above it.

Diversity is the headline metric, and it took me a while to understand why researchers emphasize it so heavily. It behaves in the gut much the way species diversity behaves in a healthy ecosystem. Higher microbiome diversity correlates with better healthspan markers, lower chronic disease burden, and slower biological aging. Low diversity shows up alongside chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and autoimmune conditions.<sup>[3]</sup>

The centenarian data adds something specific and, to my eye, hard to dismiss. Japanese centenarians produce higher levels of exhaled hydrogen, a byproduct of fiber-fermenting gut bacteria, than age-matched controls.<sup>[4]</sup> Patients with Parkinson's disease tend to carry fewer hydrogen-producing species.<sup>[5]</sup> Both findings are correlational. Neither proves that the bacteria cause longevity or prevent neurodegeneration. But the arrow always faces the same way: a diverse, well-fed microbiome tracks with better outcomes.

The microbiome earns its influence through several channels. Its bacteria ferment soluble fiber and polyphenols into short-chain fatty acids, known as SCFAs, principally butyrate, propionate, and acetate. Butyrate is the primary fuel for the cells lining the colon and the key regulator of intestinal barrier integrity.<sup>[6]</sup>

When that barrier fails (a condition called intestinal permeability, or leaky gut) bacterial fragments, notably lipopolysaccharide, or LPS, enter systemic circulation and trigger chronic low-grade inflammation linked to cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration.<sup>[7]</sup> If you read Chapter 8, you will recognize the connection: the CRP and homocysteine markers discussed there trace back, often enough, to a gut barrier that has stopped doing its job. The gut is often where the fire starts.

The gut also talks to the brain, and the breadth of that conversation caught me off guard. Bacteria produce precursors to serotonin, dopamine, and GABA, and they relay signals through the vagus nerve.<sup>[8]</sup> They communicate with mitochondria through gasotransmitters: hydrogen, methane, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and nitric oxide, molecules that function as signaling compounds, not waste products.<sup>[9]</sup> And the majority of the immune system is physically located in the gut, where microbiome composition shapes immune tolerance and inflammatory set-points.<sup>[10]</sup>

That last point bears repeating. Most of your immune system lives in your intestine. I had not expected that to be literally true.

Diet is the single most powerful modifiable lever for microbiome composition. That claim is not contested across any channel in the corpus. Fiber, fermented foods, and polyphenols are the three inputs cited most consistently as the drivers of a healthy microbiome.<sup>[11]</sup> The question is what happens when you get the sequence wrong.

## Fermented foods: the Stanford finding

The study that anchors this chapter is a randomized controlled trial from Stanford, and it delivered a result that upends common dietary advice. Justin Sonnenburg and Christopher Gardner enrolled approximately 36 participants for 10 weeks and assigned them to one of two diets: dramatically increased fermented food intake, or dramatically increased high-fiber intake.<sup>[12]</sup>

The fermented food group saw improved microbiome diversity and lower inflammatory markers. Predictable. What nobody anticipated was the fiber group's response. Participants who entered the study with low baseline microbiome diversity and then loaded fiber actually showed increased pro-inflammatory markers.<sup>[13]</sup> More fiber made things worse. I had to sit with that for a moment, because it runs directly against the advice most people receive.

The interpretation, now widely cited, is that if your microbiome lacks the diversity to handle a sudden fiber increase, the fiber feeds the wrong bacteria, and inflammation rises before it falls. This single finding reframes the standard advice. "Eat more fiber" is not wrong, but for a subset of people, "eat more fermented foods first" may be the prerequisite.<sup>[75]</sup> A Stanford-referenced protocol reinforces the point: inulin fiber alone produced no measurable microbiome benefit; the benefit appeared only when fermented foods were added.<sup>[14]</sup>

The effective dose from the Stanford data is 1 to 4 servings per day of low-sugar fermented foods: sauerkraut, kimchi, kefir, yogurt, kombucha, miso.<sup>[15]</sup> I emphasize the low-sugar qualifier because it matters. High-sugar commercial kombuchas and flavored yogurts can negate the benefit entirely by feeding harmful bacteria instead of helpful ones.<sup>[16]</sup>

This trial is well-corroborated across the corpus, appearing independently in at least four channels, cited by hosts and guests who do not share a research affiliation.<sup>[17]</sup> The 2025 US Dietary Guidelines formally incorporated fermented foods as a recommended food category for the first time, catching up to what the Stanford data had suggested years earlier, with the science arriving first and the guidelines following.<sup>[18]</sup>

## What fermentation actually does

Fermentation is, at bottom, pre-digestion by bacteria. The organisms consume plant sugars, starches, and antinutrients such as lectins and phytates during the process, improving nutrient bioavailability before the food reaches you.<sup>[19]</sup>

The bacteria that do this work also seed the colon with species that continue fermenting dietary substrates once they arrive, producing SCFAs along the way.<sup>[20]</sup> In ecological terms, it is inoculation: you introduce a working culture into the system and it colonizes the environment, provided conditions are favorable. The soil has to be ready for the seed.

Traditional cultures used fermentation for exactly this purpose long before anyone sequenced a genome. I found the specific examples instructive. Sourdough fermentation partially degrades gluten and other antinutrients, potentially making bread better tolerated by those with mild gluten sensitivity, though it is not safe for celiac disease.<sup>[21]</sup> Kimchi fermentation breaks down compounds in red pepper peels and seeds that would otherwise be poorly absorbed.<sup>[22]</sup> These are not theoretical mechanisms. They are observable biochemistry, and they are the reason fermented foods appear in traditional diets on every inhabited continent.

One question keeps surfacing about whether pasteurized fermented products, where the bacteria are dead, still confer benefit. Steven Gundry argues yes, pointing to the postbiotic mechanism. Dead bacteria and their cell wall fragments, peptidoglycans and lipoteichoic acids, still stimulate intestinal immune cells and strengthen the mucus layer.<sup>[23]</sup> This is a minority position from a practitioner with commercial affiliations. Mainstream probiotic guidance emphasizes live cultures as necessary for colonization benefit. No head-to-head RCT has settled the question, which means the honest answer is that nobody yet knows for certain. Treat this claim with caution.

## Probiotics, prebiotics, and how they fit together

The failure mode of the supplement-a-day model is a sequencing problem, and I think it is worth laying out the full chain because the terms are often used interchangeably when they should not be. Most commercially available probiotic supplements do not permanently colonize the gut. They transit through, providing transient benefit and training the immune system rather than establishing a lasting community.<sup>[24]</sup>

What they need to survive and produce lasting benefit is substrate, something to eat and ferment once they arrive. That is what prebiotics supply. Prebiotics are dietary substrates (fiber, polyphenols, resistant starch) that selectively feed beneficial gut bacteria. Probiotics are the live microorganisms those substrates sustain. Postbiotics are the metabolic byproducts the bacteria produce when they ferment prebiotic substrates.

The sequence matters: without prebiotic substrate, the probiotic has nothing to work with. Without working probiotics, there are no postbiotics. It is the gardener's error of scattering seeds over ground that has never been turned or watered. You can buy the finest seeds available, but without prepared soil, nothing germinates.<sup>[25]</sup>

Spore-forming probiotics, Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus coagulans among them, survive gastric acid better than vegetative strains because their spore coats resist the stomach's pH.<sup>[26]</sup> It is though, that the blanket claim that most standard probiotics are killed before reaching the colon overstates the case. Some vegetative strains, particularly Lactobacillus rhamnosus and L. acidophilus, do survive gastric transit in meaningful numbers, and enteric-coated capsules address the acid problem for other formulations.<sup>[27]</sup>

If I were to reduce this section to a single line of practical advice, it would be: use food-based sources before supplements. The 1 to 4 servings of fermented foods from the Stanford data doubles as both a probiotic and a prebiotic delivery system. When supplements are needed, strain specificity matters more than the CFU count printed on the label, though mainstream microbiology still considers CFU relevant for minimum effective dosing in well-characterized indications.<sup>[28]</sup>

One risk deserves a flag here. High-dose supplemental Lactobacillus may, in some individuals, produce excess D-lactic acid in the colon. This crosses the blood-brain barrier and can contribute to brain fog.<sup>[29]</sup> The evidence is limited and not widely replicated, but it supports a principle that recurs throughout this book: more is not automatically better. Two servings per day of fermented food, combined with adequate but not excessive protein intake, is one cited protocol that balances benefit against this risk.<sup>[30]</sup>

## Polyphenols and the gut

Polyphenols are plant compounds found in berries, tea, dark chocolate, coffee, spices, mushrooms, olive oil, and pomegranate. Here is the number that caught my attention: only 1 to 10 percent of polyphenols are absorbed intact in the small intestine. The gut microbiome ferments the rest into bioavailable compounds the body can actually use, making the microbiome the essential intermediary in polyphenol metabolism.<sup>[31]</sup>

To put that more plainly: nine-tenths of what you consume in a cup of tea or a handful of berries passes straight through the upper digestive tract and becomes useful only because bacteria in the colon finish the job.

That intermediary role makes polyphenols a form of prebiotic. They selectively feed beneficial genera including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Akkermansia muciniphila, a keystone species that maintains the mucous layer lining the intestinal wall.<sup>[32]</sup> Low Akkermansia is associated with increased gut permeability.<sup>[33]</sup> Polyphenols from pomegranate, green tea, and cranberry specifically feed this species, and accumulating evidence suggests polyphenols may be as important as fiber (possibly more so for certain keystone species) in supporting microbiome diversity.<sup>[34]</sup><sup>[76]</sup>

The polyphenol-to-postbiotic cascade proceeds as follows, and it is worth tracing because it connects this chapter to the pharmaceutical discussion. Gut bacteria ferment polyphenols and fiber into SCFAs, which stimulate differentiation of intestinal L-cells. Those L-cells produce GLP-1, the same hormone that pharmaceutical GLP-1 agonists target. The gut's own GLP-1 production supports blood sugar regulation and satiety signaling.<sup>[35]</sup> The mechanism is real. The magnitude is far smaller than a pharmaceutical dose. That distinction matters, because some voices in the corpus suggest that feeding gut bacteria enough fiber and polyphenols can replicate the effect of a GLP-1 drug. It cannot. The gap between what your gut can produce naturally and what a pharmaceutical delivers is not small, and confusing the two does the reader no service.

Coffee is the number-one source of polyphenols in the American diet. Up to three cups daily has shown antifibrotic effects on the liver, a finding now included in European and American clinical guidelines for metabolic liver disease. The beneficial component is likely the polyphenols rather than caffeine alone.<sup>[36]</sup>

A note on chocolate (and I expect this section will be popular). Thirty grams of dark chocolate, greater than 72 percent cacao, daily for three weeks increased microbiome diversity and improved focus and mood in one cited study. Dutch-processed and milk chocolate do not count, because the processing destroys the polyphenols.<sup>[37]</sup>

One more detail worth knowing is that adding milk to tea, chocolate, or any polyphenol-rich beverage binds the polyphenols to milk proteins and eliminates their prebiotic effect.<sup>[38]</sup> The same principle applies to the omega-3 and fiber discussions in Chapters 5 and 6: nutrient delivery depends not just on what you eat but on the matrix surrounding it.

Diversity of polyphenol intake matters as much as quantity. Different gut bacteria need different polyphenol types, much as different plants in a garden require different nutrients from the soil. Spices and herbs, rosemary and oregano in particular, contain far more polyphenols per gram than common vegetables and remain an underappreciated source.<sup>[39]</sup> Aged balsamic vinegar concentrates resveratrol and other polyphenols during longer aging.<sup>[40]</sup>

Turkey tail mushrooms contain 38 types of flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic acids, and their polysaccharides travel undigested to the colon where they feed Akkermansia and other commensal bacteria.<sup>[41]</sup> Thirty-eight types from a single mushroom. I had to go back and verify that count.

## Urolithin A: the conversion problem

Here is a finding that complicates the simple instruction to eat more plants. Pomegranate, berries, and nuts contain ellagitannins, which specific gut bacteria metabolize into urolithin A, a compound that improves mitochondrial function and muscle cell quality. Only approximately one-third of people carry the gut bacteria capable of making this conversion.<sup>[42]</sup><sup>[77]</sup>

The other two-thirds eat pomegranate and receive none of the urolithin A benefit, regardless of how much they consume. Same food. Different bacteria. Entirely different outcome. I keep coming back to that split because it undercuts the assumption that food is food, the same for everyone.

The food doses required even for the fortunate third are impractical: six glasses of pomegranate juice daily, or three full bowls of raspberries, to reach meaningful levels.<sup>[43]</sup> Nobody eats three bowls of raspberries. This is why supplemental urolithin A exists, and its clinical trial outcomes are covered in the chapter that follows. What matters here is the microbiome lesson: the same food can produce entirely different metabolic results in different people, depending on which bacteria they carry. Your gut determines what your food actually delivers, and no amount of careful shopping can override that.

## Early-life disruption and antibiotics

The microbiome's development begins at birth, and the mode of arrival matters. Babies born vaginally are inoculated with the mother's vaginal microbiome during delivery. Babies born via cesarean section miss this inoculation, resulting in microbiome differences associated with higher rates of allergies, asthma, and autoimmune conditions.<sup>[44]</sup> Vaginal seeding and probiotic supplementation have been proposed to partially restore the missed colonization, but evidence for long-term benefit remains preliminary.<sup>[45]</sup> The intention behind both proposals is sound. The clinical data to back them up is not there yet.

A Mayo Clinic study tracking antibiotic use in the first two years of life found long-term associations with metabolic and immune health outcomes, and the associations were not small.<sup>[46]</sup> This is consistent with the broader consensus that the infant gut is a critical developmental window, and that disrupting it with broad-spectrum antibiotics carries consequences extending well beyond the infection being treated.<sup>[78]</sup>

Bifidobacteria, the dominant genus in healthy infants, play a central role in immune education, and the specifics of that role are worth understanding. They interact with IgA immunoglobulin responses and determine whether dietary antigens are treated as threats or tolerated. Levels decline with age and can be depleted by antibiotics and certain medications.<sup>[47]</sup>

I paused on the shape of this argument. The bacteria you acquire in infancy may set the immune system's tolerance thresholds for life. Lose the wrong species early and the calibration may never fully reset.

## What damages the adult microbiome

Three categories of damage come up in the corpus more than any others, and they deserve to be stated plainly. Ultra-processed foods, artificial sweeteners, and emulsifiers that degrade the mucus layer are the three inputs cited most consistently as harmful to the adult microbiome.<sup>[48]</sup>

The artificial-sweetener question carries particular urgency because sucralose and aspartame are everywhere. Evidence that they damage gut bacteria is strongest in animal studies. Human data at typical consumption levels is mixed, with some studies showing disruption at high doses and others showing no significant effect.<sup>[49]</sup> The honest summary, and I want to state it carefully: the mechanism is plausible, the animal data is concerning, and the human evidence is not yet definitive.

GLP-1 drugs slow gut transit time, which may cause secondary dysbiosis through prolonged fermentation of gut contents.<sup>[50]</sup> The clinical significance of this effect in humans is not established, and it appears here not to alarm but to keep the record honest. It deserves attention, though not alarm, and it deserves monitoring as GLP-1 use scales.

Antacids and proton-pump inhibitors neutralize the acid gradient of the small intestine, potentially allowing colonic bacteria to migrate upward.<sup>[51]</sup> That upward migration is one of several proposed pathways to the condition covered next.

## The fasting-prebiotic experiment

A Chinese study produced one of the more striking gut-related findings in the corpus. The result looks improbable until you understand the mechanism, so the details matter. Healthy volunteers on a water-only fast experienced strong hunger, as expected. A second group, given just 100 calories per day of non-digestible prebiotic soluble fiber during fasting, reported no hunger at all.<sup>[52]</sup> Not reduced hunger. None.

The fiber group's gut bacteria, fed by the prebiotic, produced satiety signals that suppressed hunger entirely. To me this is not a weight-loss protocol recommendation. It is a demonstration of the gut-brain satiety axis: gut bacteria, when fed their preferred substrate, generate signals that communicate fullness to the brain. Ultra-processed foods bypass this mechanism, which is one reason they promote overconsumption.<sup>[53]</sup>

## Testing the microbiome

At-home gut testing has evolved through three generations, and the progression tells me something about how much remains unknown. Culture-based methods came first, limited by the oxygen sensitivity of gut bacteria. Then 16S rRNA sequencing, which identifies who is present. Then functional metagenomics, which reveals both who is present and what they are metabolically doing.<sup>[54]</sup> Each generation is more informative, though none is conclusive by itself.

A stool test is a snapshot, not a portrait, and that caveat is probably the single most important one in this discussion. The microbiome changes dramatically within three days based on diet alone. A purely plant-based diet versus a purely carnivore diet for three days produces substantially different bacterial populations.<sup>[55]</sup><sup>[79]</sup> That volatility limits the interpretive value of any single test, though it does not eliminate its utility entirely. Gross dysbiosis, missing species, and pathogen detection remain valid reasons to test.

For parasite detection specifically (and the false-negative problem here surprised me) DNA and RNA-based stool tests detect organisms by genetic signature rather than visual observation, offering higher sensitivity than traditional ova-and-parasite testing, which carries a high false-negative rate for organisms that do not lay eggs continuously.<sup>[56]</sup>

In one clinician's testing of more than 1,000 patients with leaky gut or autoimmune disease using DNA-based methods, 95 percent did not have parasites as the root cause; the actual drivers were dietary.<sup>[57]</sup> That is a single clinician's population, not a controlled study, and to my eye it should be weighted accordingly. But it pushes back against the internet-driven parasite-cleanse industry with data rather than opinion.

Several consumer gut testing companies have failed commercially, Ubiome and Sungenomics among them. Anyone committing to longitudinal gut testing should consider company stability before investing in a platform.<sup>[58]</sup> The test may outlast the company, or the reverse.

One emerging claim merits careful handling, and I want to be precise about why. Some researchers suggest that stool-based microbiome profiling may detect bacterial shifts correlating with elevated risk for Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease years before symptom onset.<sup>[59]</sup> This is an extraordinary claim. The evidence base is correlational mapping to published studies, not prospective clinical validation, and the researchers involved have commercial interests. Watch this space. Do not act on it yet.

## The stalled gut: methane, SIBO, and slow transit

This section covers territory that fewer people discuss but that a meaningful number live with, and the mechanics of it are worth understanding even if you do not have symptoms. **SIBO**, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, occurs when bacteria that normally reside in the colon migrate into the small intestine and ferment sugars and starches there, producing gas, bloating, and distension.<sup>[60]</sup> If the microbiome is a garden, SIBO is what happens when the plants from the back bed invade the walkway.

The condition comes in subtypes, and the treatment differs sharply depending on which one you have. Methane-dominant SIBO, driven primarily by the archaeon Methanobrevibacter smithii, slows GI transit time and produces constipation. Hydrogen-dominant SIBO is associated with diarrhea. The two require different treatments.<sup>[61]</sup>

Diagnosis relies on breath testing: a lactulose challenge followed by measurement of exhaled hydrogen and methane over approximately 90 minutes. The newer "tri-test" adds hydrogen sulfide measurement.<sup>[62]</sup> Stool tests provide indirect clues but are not diagnostic for SIBO.<sup>[63]</sup> That distinction struck me as important, because the temptation to rely on a stool test for everything gut-related is understandable but, in this case, misleading.

The migrating motor complex, or MMC, is the between-meal housekeeping mechanism that sweeps the small intestine clean. It is activated by vagus nerve signaling. Poor vagal tone reduces both peristalsis and MMC function, creating conditions for bacterial stasis and overgrowth.<sup>[64]</sup> It is the difference between a garden bed with proper drainage and one where water pools and stagnates. Same soil. Different circulation. This is why SIBO often co-occurs with conditions that impair vagal function: chronic stress, post-viral syndromes, and autonomic dysfunction.

Treatment options span a range, and the protocol details proved more nuanced than I had expected. Pharmaceutical antibiotics (**rifaximin** for hydrogen SIBO, rifaximin plus neomycin for methane SIBO) are the current first-line standard of care.<sup>[65]</sup> Herbal antimicrobials, including grapefruit seed extract, garlic, oregano oil, and olive leaf extract, are used as alternatives, though they also kill commensal bacteria alongside the targets.<sup>[66]</sup>

One practical protocol detail worth flagging: probiotics should be started simultaneously with antibiotics, not after, and continued for the same duration post-antibiotic to maintain beneficial populations.<sup>[67]</sup> The instinct, and it was certainly my instinct, is to wait until the antibiotic course is finished. The evidence says otherwise.

After eradication, a low-FODMAP approach is typical initially, avoiding the fermentable carbohydrates (artichokes, garlic, onions, apples, wheat, fructans, sugar alcohols) that feed overgrown bacteria.<sup>[68]</sup> Most practitioners favor systematic FODMAP reintroduction over permanent avoidance, and the reasoning is sound: permanent restriction can itself reduce microbiome diversity.<sup>[69]</sup> The ecological parallel holds, and by now the gardening framework should feel familiar. You can starve a pest by withholding its food source, but withhold it permanently and you also starve the beneficial species. You end up with a depleted bed, not a healthy one.

A minority position deserves mention here: a gastroenterologist from Hong Kong University, presenting at a Microbiota Society meeting, argued that antibiotic treatment for SIBO may worsen outcomes by damaging the gut wall and mitochondria, and that dietary and acid-management approaches are preferable.<sup>[70]</sup> This contradicts guideline-based care. Rifaximin has RCT evidence for hydrogen SIBO and remains the standard recommendation.<sup>[80]</sup>

## The FMT frontier

Fecal microbiota transplant (**FMT**) is the most direct method of resetting a microbiome. The concept sounds extreme but the science behind it is straightforward. The procedure has evolved from invasive enema-based methods to odorless oral capsules, though fewer than 1 percent of potential donors pass the strict screening criteria.<sup>[71]</sup>

Ben Greenfield's self-experiment with capsule-based FMT provides one of the more detailed before-and-after datasets in the corpus. Pre-post stool testing showed a 5-fold increase in Prevotella, a 7-fold increase in Oxalobacter formigenes, disappearance of a drug-resistant Klebsiella strain, and approximately 80 percent reduction in IgA food reactivity markers.<sup>[72]</sup>

Research consistently uses a cleanout phase before transplantation to create ecological space for incoming microbes; antibiotic pretreatment increases FMT engraftment magnitude and duration.<sup>[73]</sup> The analogy to clearing invasive growth before replanting a garden bed is hard to avoid, and by this point in the chapter I suspect you can see why the gardening metaphor keeps returning.

This is n-equals-one data from a self-experimenter who hosts a health podcast, and my usual caution about such data applies. The numbers are worth stating because they are specific and verifiable against his published test results, which gives them more weight than a vague anecdote. But they cannot be generalized. FMT remains a procedure requiring medical supervision, and its outcomes vary substantially based on donor quality, recipient baseline, and preparation protocol.

## Lactobacillus and microplastics

One study in the corpus reports that Lactobacillus supplementation reduced blood microplastic levels by 82 percent.<sup>[74]</sup> It belongs here, though my confidence in it is low. The mechanism proposed is that gut bacteria help break down or bind **microplastics** for elimination. The study details are not fully named in the source, and whether this was an animal or in vitro finding remains unclear. The connection to the broader polyphenol discussion is indirect: polyphenol-rich diets feed Lactobacillus, which may then perform this clearance function. Treat this as a preliminary signal rather than an established finding.

The gut-hormone connection does not end here, and this chapter has raised more questions than it has settled. The microbiome modulates estrogen metabolism through the estrobolome, influences cortisol through the HPA axis, and produces short-chain fatty acids that affect insulin signaling throughout the body. The chapter that follows, on hormones, picks up that thread from the other end of the system.
