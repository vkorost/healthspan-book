# Chapter 11: The Brain

I confess I had always pictured the brain as a kind of archive. Not a glamorous one, but the sort of large, underfunded municipal repository where files are deposited at a front desk by someone who has already lost interest, catalogued by an overworked clerk with idiosyncratic filing habits, and shelved in rows that make sense to nobody except the clerk, who retired some time ago and left no forwarding instructions. You would go looking for a particular record, pull it from the stacks, blow off a thin layer of institutional dust, and there it would be, slightly foxed but essentially intact. It is a comforting picture. It is also, as it turns out, almost entirely wrong.

In the early 1950s, a surgeon removed the hippocampus from both sides of Henry Molaison's brain.<sup>[1]</sup> The seizures stopped. So did everything else. For the remaining fifty-five years of his life, Molaison could not form a single new memory. He could not picture tomorrow. He could not imagine a place he had never been. The operation revealed something nobody had understood: the hippocampus does not merely store the past. It builds the future. It constructs novel mental scenarios, assembles associations across time, and projects the self forward. Without it, the mind is locked in an eternal present, unable to learn, unable to plan, unable to imagine what has not yet happened. It was as though someone had walked into the archive and removed not a single volume but the entire catalogue system, the acquisitions desk, and whatever part of the institution knew how to process new holdings.

I paused on that detail for a long time: fifty-five years in that eternal present. The brain that could not remember yesterday could not construct tomorrow either. Memory and imagination, it turns out, share the same infrastructure. Lose one and the other goes with it.

That discovery sits at the center of what we now call **cognitive longevity**: the project of keeping the brain's learning, clearing, and connecting machinery intact across decades. The brain is not a fixed organ that simply decays. It rewires itself every day, clears its own waste every night, and responds to what you feed it, how you move, and whether you still ask it to do hard things. The levers are knowable. Most of them are already in your hands.

## Dementia Prevention: The Modifiable Risks

The Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention, which convenes roughly every four years to review the global evidence, lists a set of modifiable risk factors for dementia.<sup>[2]</sup> The word *modifiable* deserves a moment. These are not genetic inevitabilities. They are choices, exposures, and conditions that shift risk up or down depending on what you do about them. The distinction matters because it means the catalogue of things that go wrong with the aging brain is, in large part, a catalogue of things you can do something about.

The genetic piece is real but not final. APOE4, a variant of the apolipoprotein E gene, approximately doubles the lifetime risk of Alzheimer's disease with one copy and pushes it to roughly 50 to 60 percent with two.<sup>[3]</sup> Those are sobering numbers. Yet many carriers never develop the disease, and Peter Attia has made the point repeatedly: metabolic, dietary, and inflammatory interventions can modify APOE4 expression.<sup>[4]</sup> The Lancet Commission's framework makes the same argument from the population side. Address the modifiable risks, and the genetic hand you were dealt matters less.

Three prevention levers recur across the evidence, and I should lay them out plainly because the rest of this chapter circles back to each of them. Aerobic exercise acts through BDNF, **hippocampal neurogenesis**, and cognitive reserve, building a larger, denser hippocampus that delays symptom onset even in genetically predisposed individuals.<sup>[5]</sup> Sleep optimization enables glymphatic clearance during deep sleep, removing the amyloid and tau proteins most strongly linked to Alzheimer's pathology (see the section below and Chapter 7).<sup>[6]</sup> Blood-brain barrier integrity prevents endotoxins, heavy metals, and fibrinogen from entering brain tissue and triggering pathology that compounds over years.<sup>[7]</sup>

Among all the modifiable risks on the Lancet list, though, one stands out for both its size and its neglect.

## Hearing: The Overlooked Lever

Severe hearing loss is associated with roughly a 400 percent relative increase in dementia risk; even mild hearing loss pushes the figure to about 90 percent.<sup>[8]</sup> I read those numbers twice. They are relative risks, not absolute, and the epidemiological association does not yet have a definitive causal RCT behind it. A study that attempted to show the link was not causal received a partial retraction for methodological flaws, leaving the picture unsettled.<sup>[9]</sup> But the clinical community leans toward causality, and the mechanism is intuitive: deprive the auditory cortex of input and the connected brain networks reorganize in maladaptive ways.<sup>[10]</sup> The principle is use it or lose it, which the brain follows literally.

The encouraging finding, and the reason I dwell on this section, is that hearing aid use appears to reverse the elevated risk, pushing it back toward the population average in observational data.<sup>[11]</sup> The definitive RCT proving hearing aids prevent dementia has not been completed, but the signal is strong enough that early intervention has become a consensus recommendation.

The scale is worth pausing on. Over 1.5 billion people globally have hearing loss and roughly 500 million are disabled by it; the WHO projects another billion affected by 2050, and approximately 80 percent of people who live long enough will experience it.<sup>[12]</sup> Young people today are further along the trajectory than prior generations at the same age, with one in seven teenagers now showing measurable loss driven largely by high-volume earbud and headphone use.<sup>[13]</sup> The practical threshold is simple: if someone standing near you can hear the sound coming from your earbuds, you are likely inflicting permanent damage to the high-frequency hair cells in your cochlea.<sup>[14]</sup> Those cells do not regenerate.

Once they die, the brain shifts neurons from high to mid frequencies in a maladaptive reorganization that sets the stage for tinnitus, which affects 10 to 20 percent of the population chronically and is the number one military disability in the United States, costing roughly one billion dollars a year.<sup>[15]</sup>

I had expected tinnitus to be a footnote in the clinical literature; one billion dollars annually spent on it says otherwise.

Hearing loss also drives social isolation, and social isolation independently harms brain health, causing measurable brain atrophy and elevated dementia risk.<sup>[16]</sup> The Harvard Study of Adult Development, an 85-year longitudinal cohort started in the late 1930s, found that quality of social relationships was the strongest predictor of health and happiness in old age (see Chapter 8).<sup>[17]</sup> Hearing loss severs that connection in ways the person barely notices. The pathway runs from damaged ears to quiet rooms to a shrinking world.

The typical delay between recognizing hearing loss and seeking help is seven years.<sup>[18]</sup> Seven years of compounding neural reorganization and social withdrawal. Think of it as an archive that quietly removes the labels from its shelves, one aisle at a time, and then wonders why nobody comes to browse anymore. The lever is large and the fix is available.

## Exercise and the Brain

The exercise chapters (Chapters 2 through 4) made the case for the body. Here the question is narrower: what does physical movement do for cognition specifically?

At Stanford, Tony Wyss-Coray and Saul Villeda answered part of that question with a set of parabiosis studies that surprised even the researchers.<sup>[19]</sup> They took blood plasma from young mice that had been exercising and transferred it to sedentary mice. The sedentary mice got smarter. Their cognitive performance improved without a single step on a wheel. I had assumed the benefit required the body to move, and this result overturned that assumption cleanly.

The mechanism was that exercise produces circulating blood factors, including a liver-derived protein called **clusterin**, that reach the brain and replicate some of the cognitive benefits independently of the physical activity itself.<sup>[20]</sup> The blood, it turns out, carries chemical dispatches that the brain reads.

Wyss-Coray's lab also demonstrated that young cerebrospinal fluid infused into aged mice over one month regenerated brain tissue and strongly stimulated oligodendrocytes, the cells that maintain myelin insulation on neural wiring.<sup>[21]</sup> Whether equivalent factors in human plasma or CSF could produce similar benefits remains unproven, but the direction is consistent: something in young, exercised blood communicates with old brains. I noticed, reading this work, that the implication is not merely that exercise helps the brain. The implication is that the exercise need not even happen to the brain's owner.

A separate line of work from Eric Kandel's laboratory at Columbia traced a different pathway.<sup>[22]</sup> Load-bearing exercise stimulates the release of **osteocalcin** from large bones such as the femur. Osteocalcin crosses the blood-brain barrier and enhances hippocampal function and memory consolidation. The bone signals the brain that the body is under load, and the brain updates its circuitry in response.

Both the parabiosis and the osteocalcin findings are from animal models; the human translation is inferred, not proven. But two laboratories in different institutions, running different experiments on different animals, arrived at essentially the same conclusion: the rest of the body talks to the brain through the blood. I do not pretend that animal convergence settles a human question, but convergence from independent directions is difficult to set aside.

There is a human finding that sharpens the picture. Robert Sapolsky's rodent study showed that forced exercise produced no cognitive or brain health benefit; voluntary exercise produced significant benefit.<sup>[23]</sup> The anterior cingulate cortex, a region associated with willpower and effortful engagement, grows physically larger in people who voluntarily choose and follow through on difficult challenges, whether cognitive or physical.<sup>[24]</sup> The same activity, when forced, produces no comparable structural change. Choice matters. The brain rewards the effort only when the effort is chosen.

The practical implication is worth spelling out: the treadmill you dread and the trail you enjoy may not produce the same neural outcome even when the heart rate is identical. That is not the message most people receive at the gym.

Aerobic exercise also acts through BDNF, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which is central to neuroplasticity.<sup>[25]</sup> High-intensity work and blood-flow-restriction training produce greater BDNF elevation than moderate steady-state exercise, primarily through lactate accumulation signaling.<sup>[26]</sup> Lactate produced during intense exercise is taken up by the brain, where it benefits cognitive function and mitochondrial activity; it is the primary signal linking exercise intensity to BDNF production.<sup>[27]</sup> Dr. Wendy Suzuki has emphasized that 30 to 45 minutes of aerobic exercise is the minimum duration for hippocampal BDNF benefit; a 10-minute walk lifts dopamine and serotonin but does not reach the hippocampus.<sup>[28]</sup> The threshold is, to my mild dismay, half an hour of genuine effort.

Regular cardiovascular exercise also reduces depression in part by shunting kynurenine into muscle, preventing its conversion to quinolinic acid (a neurotoxin) and protecting the tryptophan-to-serotonin pathway.<sup>[29]</sup> The mental health benefit of exercise, as far as I can tell from the evidence, is not a vague mood lift. It is a specific biochemical rerouting.

The evidence that exercise protects cognition is among the most broadly supported findings in this book, converging across animal parabiosis, human brain imaging, and epidemiological cohorts.<sup>[30]</sup> Whether it works primarily through BDNF, through glymphatic clearance during sleep (see below), or through circulating blood factors is a weighting disagreement, not a fundamental contradiction. The archive is large and the shelves all point the same direction.

## Motivation and Dopamine

Dopamine is a prediction-error calculator.<sup>[31]</sup> I had expected the popular version to be roughly correct (dopamine equals pleasure), and the actual mechanism proved more interesting.

Dr. Read Montague, in conversation with Andrew Huberman, reframed the molecule precisely: dopamine encodes the gap between what the brain expected and what actually happened. When the outcome exceeds the prediction, dopamine fires. When it matches, dopamine stays flat. When it falls short, dopamine dips. That dip is what people experience as disappointment, and the chase to avoid it is what drives compulsive behavior.

This explains why the arrival fallacy fails. Reaching a goal produces only a momentary dopamine spike before the baseline resets.<sup>[32]</sup> Lasting satisfaction, to the extent it exists neurochemically, comes from the pursuit itself, not the destination.

The practical implications compound. Gambling machines, social media, and smartphones exploit variable-ratio reinforcement to hijack dopamine circuits, producing compulsive engagement despite net losses.<sup>[33]</sup> Drugs of abuse produce unnaturally large dopamine surges that make normal activities feel dull by comparison; tolerance and withdrawal-like states result from receptor changes that are difficult to reverse.<sup>[34]</sup>

The mechanisms that evolved to keep you alive (seek food, seek connection, seek novelty) are the ones that modern technology exploits most effectively. The popular story casts dopamine as the pleasure molecule. The actual story is about prediction, a reframing that changes the practical advice considerably.

GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide appear to broadly dampen dopamine-driven reward-seeking, reducing cravings not only for food but for gambling, compulsive buying, and alcohol (see Chapter 14 for the mechanistic discussion).<sup>[35]</sup> Whether that blunting extends to general motivation and drive, not just craving, is a concern flagged but not yet established in clinical literature.<sup>[36]</sup> The question is unresolved, and I concede it is a large one.

Morning bright light enhances dopamine output via a pathway from the eye to the perihabenular nucleus.<sup>[37]</sup> Avoiding artificial bright light between 10 PM and 4 AM protects dopamine tone and supports mood. This is free. It requires no supplement. It simply requires not flooding the retina with light at the wrong time.

## Overnight Clearance: The Glymphatic Connection

The brain's nightly waste clearance system, the glymphatic network discovered by Maiken Nedergaard in 2012 (see Chapter 7), clears beta-amyloid and tau, the proteins most strongly linked to Alzheimer's disease.<sup>[38]</sup> The sleep chapter covers the discovery and the selective deep-sleep deprivation experiment that established within-subject causality. Here the consequence is what matters: repeated nights of short or disrupted sleep escalate Alzheimer's risk by preventing this nightly washout. Think of it as an archive whose custodial staff arrives only after hours, so shortening the shift lets the dust compound.

People who habitually sleep seven hours or fewer show markedly greater amyloid accumulation on PET scans by their seventies and eighties compared to longer sleepers.<sup>[39]</sup> Seven hours sounds perfectly reasonable as a night's rest. Most people would call it responsible. That is what makes the finding uncomfortable.

Insomnia is associated with less deep sleep and elevated dementia risk specifically because of reduced glymphatic drainage.<sup>[40]</sup> Alcohol disrupts deep sleep despite initially aiding sleep onset, preventing glymphatic activation and contributing to next-day cognitive fogginess.<sup>[41]</sup> I had assumed the nightcap was harmless to the brain; the evidence says it undermines the custodial staff before they arrive.

Eating late at night diverts blood flow to digestion, impairing glymphatic clearance for roughly three to four hours after the meal.<sup>[42]</sup> If that impairment repeats every night for years, the amyloid accumulation compounds. The dinner cutoff, discussed in the nutrition chapters from a metabolic angle, turns out to be a brain-protection strategy as well. I concede the advice sounds pedestrian. It is also the sort of lever that compounds quietly over decades.

An intact blood-brain barrier is required for glymphatic function: a leaky barrier reduces the pressure gradient needed to drive cerebrospinal fluid perfusion and allows neurotoxic molecules such as fibrinogen into brain tissue, compounding the waste accumulation that the glymphatic system exists to prevent.<sup>[43]</sup>

Glymphatic clearance also diminishes with aging, which is why sleep quality becomes more consequential for brain health over time, not less.<sup>[44]</sup> Sauna use at moderate temperatures has been associated with reduced dementia risk in large Finnish cohorts (see Chapter 12 for the primary data), though temperatures above 212 degrees Fahrenheit were associated with increased risk in the same study.<sup>[45]</sup> The cleaning system, in other words, has limitations of its own.

## DHA and the Omega-3 Index

DHA is a structural component of neuronal membranes and synapses. Different researchers cite different figures for how much of the brain's fat content DHA constitutes, ranging from roughly 10 percent to 50 percent depending on what is being measured.<sup>[46]</sup> The range is wide, but what sits outside dispute is that without adequate DHA, neuron-to-neuron communication degrades and the brain's capacity for repair and plasticity narrows. If the archive metaphor still holds at all, DHA is closer to the paper the documents are printed on than to anything you could remove without consequence.

Dr. Bill Harris's omega-3 index research puts numbers on the problem.<sup>[47]</sup> The average American omega-3 index sits at roughly 5 percent. Japan's average is roughly 10 to 11 percent. The gap correlates with approximately a five-year difference in life expectancy between the two countries. Correlation is not causation, but the association holds across multiple observational datasets, and Harris's framework has become the standard metric for omega-3 status. I was struck by how modest the numerical gap appears (5 percent versus 10 percent) until you map it onto years of life.

Higher omega-3 blood levels are associated with larger total brain volume and larger hippocampal volume in imaging studies.<sup>[48]</sup> That association is specific enough to note: not just general brain health, but measurable volume differences visible on scans. In college football players, roughly 2 grams per day of DHA reduced neurofilament light, a biomarker of neuronal damage.<sup>[49]</sup> EPA, the other major marine omega-3, reduced anxiety symptoms in a meta-analysis published in *Translational Psychiatry* and improved major depressive disorder symptoms in studies published in the *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*.<sup>[50]</sup>

The strongest interventional finding comes from a Swiss randomized controlled trial combining omega-3 supplementation, vitamin D3, and resistance training.<sup>[51]</sup> In the three-intervention arm, participants showed approximately 66 percent lower likelihood of invasive cancer and approximately 3.8 months of epigenetic aging slowdown. Omega-3 was the only single intervention that independently slowed epigenetic aging clocks.

That last point is, to my reading, the most important datum in this section: of the three interventions tested individually, only the fatty acid moved the biological clock on its own. The trial design matters because this is not an observational association but a randomized experiment with a measurable biological outcome.

DHA and EPA, when metabolized, form specialized pro-resolving molecules called resolvins and protectins that actively resolve inflammation rather than merely suppressing it.<sup>[52]</sup> This distinguishes omega-3s from anti-inflammatory drugs, which block the inflammatory cascade without cleaning it up. The difference is instructive: one approach closes the complaints window at the front desk, the other sends someone to actually reshelve the returns.

Fish oil supplements are prone to oxidation; a rancid supplement may do more harm than good, which strikes me as a problem the supplement industry has been slow to address.<sup>[53]</sup> Look for a TOTOX (total oxidation) score under 6, use the IFOS database for third-party testing, and refrigerate after opening. ALA from plant sources converts to DHA at very low efficiency; marine sources or algal oil are necessary for adequate brain DHA.<sup>[54]</sup>

Nick Norwitz has argued that omega-3s from whole fish may cross into the brain more effectively than those from supplements, because whole fish contain additional protective compounds such as selenium that supplements lack.<sup>[55]</sup> The argument rests on one researcher's position. Other experts, including Gundry, Rhonda Patrick, and Huberman, broadly recommend supplementation as effective. The consensus holds: get your omega-3 index above 8 percent, by food or supplement, and verify it by test.

## Nootropics: What the Trials Show

The word *nootropic* covers everything from caffeine to prescription stimulants. What follows is what the clinical trial evidence actually supports, not what the supplement industry claims. The catalogue is shorter and more cautious than the marketing would have you believe.

**Lion's mane** has one well-cited RCT: a 2009 Japanese trial of 30 adults aged 50 to 80 with mild cognitive impairment, taking 750 milligrams three times daily for 16 weeks.<sup>[56]</sup> The treatment group showed significant cognitive improvement over placebo. Then the researchers stopped giving it, and within four weeks all gains had disappeared.

The compound contains hericenones and erinacines that cross the blood-brain barrier and stimulate BDNF and nerve growth factor synthesis.<sup>[57]</sup> But the 2009 trial is essentially the entire clinical case. Overall RCT results for lion's mane are mixed, and the complete reversal on discontinuation raises the question of whether it produces any lasting structural benefit or merely props up function for as long as you take it.<sup>[58]</sup> The data, read carefully, describes a compound that improves a measurement without improving the underlying system. The archive looks tidier while the temporary staff is on duty; when they leave, so does the effect.

**Transdermal nicotine** improved cognition in mild cognitive impairment patients over six months in a 2012 study published in *Neurology*.<sup>[59]</sup> Nicotine activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, triggering acetylcholine, dopamine, and norepinephrine release, which improves focus, recall, and processing speed. The addiction risk rises substantially above 10 milligrams per day, tolerance can develop within days of daily use, and blood pressure and insulin sensitivity may be impaired.<sup>[60]</sup> This is not a casual supplement, and I would not treat it as one. The inverse association between smoking and Parkinson's disease is epidemiologically documented, but whether clean nicotine delivery specifically accounts for it remains debated, and speakers promoting it often have commercial interests in nicotine products.<sup>[61]</sup>

**Creatine monohydrate** has a meta-analysis of 16 clinical trials confirming cognitive benefit specifically for memory tasks, with a 2022 study showing memory improvement in older adults and a 2024 study showing gains in memory, attention, and processing speed.<sup>[62][63]</sup> Cognitive doses (10 to 20 grams per day, split across meals to reduce GI distress) are considerably higher than the 3 to 5 grams typically used for exercise. The evidence is emerging but the risk profile is low, which is an unusual combination in this field. I mention the dose gap because it catches people by surprise: the cognitive amount is three to four times the muscle amount, and the two purposes call for different protocols.

**Magnesium L-threonate** is the only magnesium form shown to effectively raise brain magnesium levels by crossing the blood-brain barrier. Dr. Guosong Liu, who developed the compound at MIT's Picower Institute, conducted controlled human trials reportedly showing a 7.5-year brain-age reversal in six weeks with improved executive function.<sup>[64]</sup> The claim is extraordinary, the effect size is extraordinary, and the researcher has a direct commercial conflict of interest in the product. Independent replication is needed before this finding carries full weight.

In animal PTSD models from Liu's lab, magnesium L-threonate combined with extinction therapy improved extinction of negative memories, but the effect was absent when either the compound or the therapy was used alone.<sup>[65]</sup> The direction of plasticity depends on what the person is doing while the compound is active. I find that conditional result more revealing than the headline number, because it suggests the compound is not a switch but a context.

**CDP-choline (citicoline)** at 250 to 500 milligrams per day has solid human evidence for cognitive enhancement.<sup>[66]</sup> It crosses the blood-brain barrier and, via the Kennedy pathway, combines with DHA to build new synaptic membranes. Stacking it with a DHA source appears synergistic, which makes mechanistic sense: the raw materials for membrane construction arrive together. If DHA is the paper in the archive, CDP-choline is the binding.

**Bacopa monnieri** has probably the strongest human evidence of any natural nootropic for language, learning, and memory, but onset requires 8 to 12 weeks of consistent use.<sup>[67]</sup> It functions as an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, the same mechanism as the first-line Alzheimer's drug. Anyone expecting a noticeable effect in the first week will be disappointed and will likely quit before the compound has begun to work. Patience, in this case, is part of the protocol. The mechanism it uses, acetylcholinesterase inhibition, is the same one behind pharmaceuticals that cost considerably more.

**Spermidine** at 1 to 3.5 milligrams per day showed improved cognitive performance in adults aged 60 to 96 after three months in RCTs, working through neuronal autophagy and mitochondrial integrity in aging neurons.<sup>[68]</sup> The supplement shelf chapter (Chapter 14) covers the broader longevity data.

## Learning, Novelty, and the Aging Brain

Normal brain aging involves neuronal shrinkage, dendritic retraction, myelin deterioration, and declining neurogenesis.<sup>[69]</sup> Processing speed and working memory decline. The inventory of losses is real, and it would be dishonest of me to minimize it. But it is not the whole ledger.

Neuroplasticity continues throughout the entire lifespan, not only in childhood.<sup>[70]</sup> The human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons, each making approximately 10,000 connections, constantly plugging and unplugging in response to experience.<sup>[71]</sup> The archive never closes, though it processes new acquisitions more slowly.

Memory itself is encoded by four factors: novelty, repetition, association, and emotional resonance.<sup>[72]</sup> Novelty is the most powerful trigger for encoding new memories: the brain's attentional systems snap to unfamiliar stimuli, which the hippocampus then flags for long-term storage.

Repetition strengthens neural connections by firing the same neuron sequence repeatedly; each repetition increases connection strength and reduces the future repetitions needed for retrieval.<sup>[73]</sup> I noticed the circularity there: the more you remember something, the less effort remembering requires, which frees the system to encode the next unfamiliar thing. It is, in archival terms, a cataloguing system that gets faster with practice.

Play turns out to be a serious mechanism, and by "serious" I mean it has an identifiable neural pathway. It engages the periaqueductal gray, which releases endogenous opioids that allow the prefrontal cortex to explore more contingencies, take on more roles, and expand its operational repertoire.<sup>[74]</sup> Adults who reintroduce deliberate play expand cognitive flexibility. The brain treats play as a low-stakes rehearsal for high-stakes adaptation, with measurable neural benefits.

Mental engagement and continuous learning are listed as prevention levers against dementia in the BRIGHT MINDS framework: retirement and cessation of learning are modifiable risk factors.<sup>[75]</sup> Let me repeat that: cessation of learning is a modifiable risk factor for dementia. This is not a vague exhortation to stay curious. It is a structural claim: effortful, deliberate learning strengthens neural circuits more than passive media consumption.<sup>[76]</sup> Learning is also intrinsically pleasurable because it evolved as adaptive; the ventral striatum, the brain's reward center, activates during the pursuit of novel information, making learning self-reinforcing.<sup>[77]</sup> The brain rewards you for feeding it new material.

Knowledge-based fields allow practitioners to grow cognitively stronger with age because they continuously integrate new data with accumulated knowledge.<sup>[78]</sup> The subject matter may be less important than the sustained practice of organized, self-directed thought. A language, a musical instrument, a sport with complex movement patterns on unpredictable terrain: all produce similar neural benefits because the cortex is largely domain-general.

Mriganka Sur's ferret experiments at MIT demonstrated this with unusual clarity, and I think it is the single most surprising result in this chapter: rewiring visual input to auditory cortex caused that cortex to become visually responsive.<sup>[79]</sup> The hardware adapts to whatever you plug into it.

Physical activity with cognitive complexity matters here too, and the point connects back to the voluntary-exercise finding from the Sapolsky section in a way worth making explicit. Outdoor terrain, undulating and unpredictable, builds brain reserve better than a treadmill.<sup>[80]</sup> Combining strength, endurance, and complex movement patterns supports memory, executive function, and brain structure more than simple repetitive activity. The trail does more for the brain than the conveyor belt.

The NFL brain study at Amen Clinics showed that 80 percent of former players improved on a comprehensive rehabilitation program that included diet, supplements, brain training, and exercise.<sup>[81]</sup> Eighty percent is not the number I would have predicted. If brains that have absorbed thousands of sub-concussive hits can recover measurable function, the intact brain has considerable room it has not yet used.
