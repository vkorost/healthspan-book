# Chapter 1: Measure First

I had assumed, until I began reading for this book, that a routine annual physical amounted to a reasonably thorough inspection. A nurse takes your blood pressure, a doctor presses a stethoscope to your chest, somebody draws a vial or two of blood, and a week later the results come back: cholesterol fine, liver fine, thyroid fine. The word "fine" does a remarkable amount of heavy lifting in that sentence. It turns out the inspection was missing most of what mattered, in roughly the way that checking only the windscreen wipers and the cup holders would miss most of what mattered about a car.

A continuous glucose monitor, strapped to his arm during a routine viral illness, caught something no symptom had yet announced in the case of Michael Snyder: fasting glucose drifting upward in a pattern consistent with early insulin resistance. Snyder is a geneticist at Stanford who had already gone to the trouble of sequencing his own genome, where he discovered he carried the APOE4 variant, a marker that raises the odds of Alzheimer's disease. That genomic discovery, dramatic as it sounds, left his behavior unchanged; the glucose monitor was what actually moved him to adjust his diet and exercise before any doctor would have had reason to order a test.<sup>[1]</sup>

He did not discover a disease. He discovered a trajectory. The trajectory, not the diagnosis, is what mattered, and I keep coming back to that distinction, because it is the argument of this chapter and the reason it comes first. Every intervention in the chapters that follow, from aerobic training to sauna protocols to the supplement shelf, lands differently depending on whether you know where you are starting. A blood draw without context is a snapshot. A blood draw repeated over years, set beside a body-composition scan and a measure of cardiovascular capacity, becomes a map. The map is considerably more useful, in the way that knowing you are heading toward a cliff is more useful than knowing where you happen to be standing.

Call it **the baseline panel**: the small set of measurements that, taken together and tracked over time, carries most of the signal about where your body stands and where it is heading. I had not encountered the term before starting this project, which tells you something about how little the conventional physical communicates what it covers and what it leaves out.

## The blood draw

The conventional annual physical orders a lipid panel, a metabolic panel, and maybe a thyroid-stimulating hormone test. For decades that has been the default, and until recently nobody had much reason to question it. These tests are not wrong; they are incomplete, and the gaps they leave conceal the conditions most worth catching early.

Start with insulin. Standard panels measure fasting glucose and **HbA1c**, both of which reflect blood sugar control. What they miss is the years of silent deterioration that precede an abnormal reading. I reread the next figure twice because it seemed improbable. The Whitehall II study followed a large cohort and found that full-blown insulin resistance can develop over six to fourteen years with no detectable change in HbA1c or fasting blood sugar.<sup>[2]</sup>

Six to fourteen years. That is long enough to raise a child through elementary school while a metabolic fire smolders undetected beneath numbers your doctor pronounces normal. During that entire window, **fasting insulin** and a derived score called **HOMA-IR** are already elevated, but most primary care physicians never order them. One practitioner describes fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and **ApoB** as the three most underordered markers in adults; third-year family medicine residents, he notes, are typically never taught to request HOMA-IR at all.<sup>[3]</sup> No controlled trial has established that ranking. But the underlying observation is hard to dispute.

Consider what that means. A patient walks into a clinic with perfect **HbA1c**, perfect fasting glucose, and a metabolic fire burning silently beneath both numbers. The standard panel cannot see it. It was not designed to see it. By the time the glucose number finally moves, the damage has been accumulating for years. I found this to be one of the more sobering findings in the research literature.

Why **ApoB**? A standard lipid panel reports LDL cholesterol, but ApoB counts the actual lipoprotein particles carrying cholesterol into the arterial wall. Two people can share the same LDL number and have wildly different particle counts; the one with more particles faces considerably more risk. Think of two highways with the same posted speed limit, one carrying three times the truck traffic; the wear on the road surface will not be the same. ApoB provides better cardiovascular risk stratification than LDL alone and is increasingly treated as the primary cardiovascular risk marker by longevity-focused clinicians.<sup>[4]</sup> A normal LDL number can coexist with a dangerously high particle count. ApoB is the number that exposes the discrepancy.

Then there is **Lp(a)**, a lipoprotein particle that roughly one in five Americans carries at elevated levels.<sup>[5]</sup> It is almost entirely genetic. Statins do not lower it. Standard lipid panels do not include it. I wondered, when first encountering this marker, how many people carry a cardiovascular risk factor that no lifestyle change and no common medication can remove, and have simply never been informed. The test needs to be run only once; if Lp(a) is low, it stays low. If it is high, you and your physician can adjust everything else accordingly, pursuing aggressive ApoB reduction and inflammation control to compensate for a risk factor that is not going away.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (**hsCRP**) rounds out the inflammatory picture. It is described as the best available marker for cardiovascular inflammation and a predictor of longevity; an elevated reading demands attention even when blood sugar looks normal.<sup>[6]</sup> That description rests on a single attribution rather than a meta-analysis, which limits how much weight any individual should place on the claim.

These are the consensus markers named across multiple clinicians and channels in the source material: HbA1c, fasting glucose, **fasting insulin**, **HOMA-IR**, ApoB, **Lp(a)**, and **hsCRP**. They form the core of the baseline panel's blood component. A complete hormone panel (testosterone, thyroid function, and related markers) fills out the picture, and Chapter 10 covers those in detail. The point here is narrower: the tests most people receive each year leave out the markers that matter most.

Three practical notes before moving on. Do not draw blood the morning after a hard training session. Acute exercise transiently elevates liver enzymes, CRP, and inflammatory markers for several days, creating false impressions of chronic disease.<sup>[7]</sup> A single heavy leg day can make your CRP look like you have an active infection. Wait three days. I mention this because it is the kind of detail nobody else does.

A single snapshot is far less valuable than a series. Having a decade of longitudinal data on HbA1c, CRP, and lipids is, in the words of one researcher, "super informative." The panel's value multiplies with time: a trend line reveals things a single reading cannot. Quarterly bloodwork is recommended by functional longevity medicine practitioners as a minimum standard for adults seeking proactive monitoring; the conventional annual physical is, in this framing, insufficient.<sup>[8,9]</sup> That frequency is a practitioner recommendation, not a clinical guideline.

Comprehensive panels now routinely include environmental toxin markers: BPA, **PFAS**, and heavy metals alongside standard health markers.<sup>[10]</sup> I had not considered that a blood draw could function as an environmental sensor, but apparently it is a routine one. Chapter 13 covers those exposures in depth; the point here is that the baseline panel extends beyond metabolic and cardiovascular markers.

One more gap deserves mention. A study comparing 1.1 million hypothyroid patients on levothyroxine against 1.1 million healthy controls over twenty years found persistent health disparities despite treatment, raising questions about whether the standard **TSH**-only thyroid screen catches enough.<sup>[11]</sup> That is 2.2 million participants, a number large enough to command attention. A more complete thyroid panel, including **free T3** and **free T4**, costs little and reveals whether the gland is converting properly. The evidence is not yet definitive enough to call this consensus, but the sheer scale of the study makes it hard to wave away.

A note on reference ranges. Standard lab reference ranges are derived from population averages that include unhealthy individuals. Functional medicine practitioners argue that a fasting insulin of 25 µU/mL, which falls within the conventional "normal" range, represents insulin resistance by a wide margin; their threshold is closer to 7 µU/mL. Mainstream medicine uses validated clinical thresholds from large studies and warns against over-medicalization. The two sides do not agree. A values-in-context approach matters more than any single cutoff. A reading of 24 that falls within the "normal" range can still sit in the bottom 10 percent of that range, producing symptomatic dysfunction that a physician relying on the reference flag alone would never investigate.<sup>[12,13]</sup> The word "normal," it turns out, does nearly as much heavy lifting as the word "fine."

## Biological age

Chronological age counts birthdays. **Biological age** attempts to count wear. The dominant technology for measuring that wear is the **epigenetic clock**, which reads patterns of **DNA methylation**, chemical tags that control which genes are active and which are silent. Your DNA sequence accounts for approximately 20 percent of health outcomes; the remaining 80 percent, shaped by behavior, diet, sleep, and stress, leaves its fingerprint in the methylation pattern.<sup>[14]</sup> I noticed that ratio and paused. Four-fifths of the outcome is written not in the code but in the living.

The field has moved through generations rapidly, and keeping track of which clock does what requires some concentration. First-generation clocks, including the original **Horvath clock**, were trained to predict chronological age from methylation data. They are accurate but not especially useful for intervention, because knowing your methylation has shifted does not tell you which specific action to take. Second-generation clocks like **GrimAge** are trained to predict mortality outcomes directly and are statistically superior for longevity prediction. A newer metric, **DunedinPACE**, measures the speed of aging rather than a static age estimate, and one ranking study placed it first among longevity markers including grip strength and cardiorespiratory fitness.<sup>[15,16,17]</sup>

The technology is real. The actionability gap is equally real, which is the central limitation of the field at this stage of its development.

Peter Attia has expressed skepticism that whole-body epigenetic clocks can outperform chronological age for predicting individual lifespan; they are useful health markers, he argues, but not a crystal ball. Michael Snyder makes a related point: Horvath and GrimAge clocks give a number without a clear intervention target, making them less clinically useful than organ-specific or proteomic approaches. Tony Wyss-Coray's work on blood protein profiling of organ-specific aging (what he calls ageotypes) represents the next generation: clocks that identify which organs are aging faster than the rest, and by how much.<sup>[18,19,20]</sup> That level of granularity is where actionability begins. For group-level research, epigenetic clocks are well established; for individual decisions, the technology remains a compass pointing in a general direction, not a GPS guiding each step.

I went back to that passage. A test that reports your biological age as fifty-three is a conversation piece at dinner. A test that tells you your liver is aging twelve years ahead of schedule while your cardiovascular system remains solidly middle-aged is a reason to change what you do on Monday. The first kind of answer is decorative. The second is operational. The technology producing that second kind of answer is not yet available at consumer scale, though the research trajectory points clearly toward it, and the gap between those two kinds of answers is narrowing faster than most people realize.

What does move the needle on these clocks is not exotic. Omega-3 fatty acids slow biological aging partly by resolving chronic inflammation. And relationship quality, of all things, has been identified as outperforming every supplement tested for longevity effects on epigenetic aging metrics.<sup>[21,22]</sup> The most thoroughly researched longevity intervention available without a prescription turns out to be other people. No capsule tested to date has come close to matching it.

The most expensive biological age test in the world cannot substitute for the interventions covered in the chapters ahead: training, sleep, and nutrition. A good **epigenetic clock** score does not capture everything; a person can have excellent methylation age and still be vulnerable to falls, fractures, or **sarcopenia** if muscle mass is neglected.<sup>[23]</sup>

The multi-modal integration is beginning. Combining epigenetic data with proteomic data, organ-specific clocks, and wearable physiological data is the path toward making biological age measurement clinically actionable rather than merely informative.<sup>[24]</sup> That integration is not yet available at scale. For now, a biological age test is worth taking once to establish a reference point, then repeating after a sustained intervention to see whether the trajectory has shifted. It belongs in the baseline panel as a waypoint, not a destination. My impression is that the field is building something genuinely useful but has not quite finished building it.

## Body composition

Step on a bathroom scale and you learn one number. It tells you almost nothing useful about what lies beneath, which was, I confess, news to me when I first encountered the research.

A person with a normal BMI can carry pathologically high **visceral fat** while maintaining a lean silhouette. The clinical term is **TOFI**: thin outside, fat inside. BMI, which divides weight by height squared, cannot distinguish between a kilogram of muscle and a kilogram of abdominal fat. That distinction matters enormously because the two tissues behave differently in every metabolic pathway that determines long-term health. Converting five pounds of fat to five pounds of muscle produces no weight change but substantial health improvement.<sup>[25,26]</sup> The display shows the same number while the person standing on it has become materially healthier, and the scale has no way of knowing the difference.

**DEXA** (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) is the standard tool for resolving this ambiguity. It is a low-radiation scan lasting about ten minutes that uses two X-ray energies to distinguish bone mineral content, fat mass, and lean tissue. A basic body-composition scan costs roughly $100 to $150; a full segmental bone mineral density analysis runs up to about $400, and no doctor's referral is required.<sup>[27,28]</sup> For the price of a decent pair of running shoes, you learn more about what you are carrying around than the bathroom scale will reveal in a lifetime of daily consultations. I wish someone had mentioned this sooner, though in fairness the question had never occurred to me.

For clinical utility, the scan must provide segmental analysis: bone mineral density for left hip, right hip, and lumbar spine separately. A whole-body BMD reported as a single Z-score is insufficient for fracture risk assessment, because a weakening hip can hide behind a strong spine. BMD is reported as a **T-score**, measured in standard deviations from the mean of a healthy 30-year-old of the same sex. A T-score below negative 1 indicates **osteopenia**; below negative 2.5, **osteoporosis**.<sup>[29,30]</sup>

I paused at the arithmetic of that concealment. A test designed to measure bone strength can mask the very weakness it was built to detect, because the average of a strong spine and a failing hip is a number that looks reassuringly normal. The patient leaves the clinic confident, while the hip continues its quiet deterioration, invisible to the summary score that just cleared it.

The technology is not the limitation. A whole-body summary is an average, and an average is a remarkably effective place for bad news to hide. Segmental data, which reports each site on its own terms, costs nothing extra and reveals exactly what the summary was designed to smooth over.

Peter Attia runs a **DEXA** scan every six months. He reads both total lean mass and regional muscle distribution, tracking a metric called the **appendicular lean mass index** (ALMI) against age-adjusted normative data. Then he applies what he calls a ten-year forward projection: if nothing changes, where does the trajectory lead?<sup>[31]</sup> That is one clinician's protocol, not a guideline. The question it asks, however, is the right one. Not where you are; where you are heading.

DEXA can also detect muscle asymmetry between body segments. Differences greater than approximately 10 percent in lean mass between sides may signal functional issues or injury risk.<sup>[32]</sup> That kind of detail, I should note, is invisible to any scale, any mirror, and any BMI calculation.

Why the urgency around lean mass? The epidemiology is stark. Low lean mass on DEXA correlates with sharply elevated all-cause mortality. The hazard ratio for being weak versus strong is approximately 3.2 for all-cause mortality, higher than the hazard ratio for smoking.<sup>[33,34]</sup> I checked that comparison twice. Weakness, measured on a scan, is a stronger predictor of death than cigarettes. Lean body mass is inversely proportional to mortality risk. Sarcopenia, the clinical term for age-related muscle loss, was classified as a disease only in 2016, a result reflecting how recently the field has recognized it as a distinct and treatable condition.

DEXA has limits. It does not directly measure skeletal muscle mass; roughly 40 to 50 percent of what it calls lean tissue is actually skeletal muscle, while the remainder consists of organs, connective tissue, and water.<sup>[35]</sup> Nor can it detect **intramuscular adipose tissue** (IMAT), the fat deposited within and between muscle fibers that drives insulin resistance independently of total body fat. As muscle ages and becomes sedentary, intramuscular fat infiltrates the tissue in a pattern sometimes compared to the marbling of a steak (a comparison that makes the biology vivid and the steak less appetizing). IMAT appears to be more predictive of metabolic outcomes than overall body fat percentage, and it requires MRI or CT to measure.<sup>[36,37]</sup>

Regular exercise, even brief sessions, reduces IMAT regardless of total body fat changes.<sup>[38]</sup> The marbling reverses with movement, which was, in my reading, one of the few unambiguously encouraging findings in the section.

Hydration and glycogen status can shift DEXA readings from one day to the next, which is why standardizing conditions across serial scans is essential: fasting, time of day, hydration status.<sup>[39]</sup> Standard of care often delays bone mineral density screening until after menopause or after a first fracture. Experts advocate starting during perimenopause, because bone loss begins before periods fully stop.<sup>[40]</sup> That recommendation remains contested.

Still, for most people, DEXA is the most accessible and informative body-composition tool available. The alternative is a bathroom scale, which is polite, consistent, and lying to you every morning.

## The heart scan

A statistic that rearranged the way I think about cardiac screening: approximately 70 percent of all heart attacks occur without any preceding symptoms. The first sign of coronary artery disease in more than half of patients is the heart attack itself, and half of those patients do not survive it.<sup>[41]</sup>

I sat with that statistic for a while, and returning to it only made the implications sharper, because it reframes everything about cardiac screening. Silent disease forms in arteries roughly the diameter of a pencil dot, whether or not you feel fine.<sup>[42]</sup> The vessels responsible for keeping you alive are narrower than the lead in a mechanical pencil. There is not, it hardly needs saying, much room for error.

The **coronary artery calcium (CAC) score** is an X-ray-based test that detects calcium deposits in the walls of the coronary arteries. Calcification indicates advanced atherosclerosis. The test is fast, relatively inexpensive, and uses minimal radiation. Its greatest value falls in a specific window: roughly between ages 40 and 60. By age 60, the majority of American men already qualify as high cardiovascular risk by other measures, making the additional information from a **CAC score** less decisive.<sup>[43,44]</sup> The test has, in effect, an expiration date, which struck me as an oddly perishable quality for a medical scan to have.

A zero CAC score is reassuring, but it is not a clean bill, and understanding why matters. Plaque always begins as soft, non-calcified cholesterol deposits. Calcified plaque is the more advanced, stabilized form. You might assume that the hardened plaque is the dangerous kind. The opposite is true. Soft plaque is more dangerous: it is unstable, prone to rupture, and when it ruptures, it can trigger the coagulation cascade that completely occludes the artery within minutes. A person with high ApoB can have rapidly progressing atherosclerosis with no detectable calcium yet, because calcification comes later in the disease process. A paper by Wilkins and Lloyd-Jones at Northwestern, published in JAMA, showed that waiting for a positive CAC before starting treatment leads to worse outcomes.<sup>[45,46]</sup>

Attia recommends treating elevated ApoB regardless of the CAC score, precisely because the disease may be progressing in the soft-plaque stage that calcium scoring misses. Found plaque acts, in one clinician's analogy, like a fly trap for additional cholesterol deposition; soft plaque progresses more quickly than hard plaque.<sup>[47,48]</sup>

For a fuller picture, CT angiography (such as the Cleerly scan) uses contrast injection to visualize both calcified and non-calcified plaque, providing precise location and infiltration data. Each scan delivers approximately 5 to 15 millisieverts of radiation on older machines; modern fast machines deliver less radiation than a transcontinental flight. A minimum interval of roughly 18 months between scans is recommended with slower scanners, and if the initial scan is clean, a much longer interval before re-scanning is appropriate.<sup>[49,50]</sup> This is not a test to repeat casually. My understanding is that the contrast dye and radiation make it a procedure best spaced widely apart.

The practical takeaway is layered, and it helps to keep the levels distinct. A blood panel gives you ApoB and Lp(a). A CAC score tells you whether calcified plaque has formed. A CT angiogram tells you whether soft plaque is present before calcification. The question is how far down the imaging ladder you need to go. For someone with clean bloodwork, a clean family history, and no symptoms, a CAC score may be sufficient. For someone with elevated ApoB or a family history of early cardiac events, imaging that captures soft plaque changes the clinical picture entirely.

Blood cholesterol reflects circulating levels, not what has already deposited in tissue, which is why a blood panel alone is not sufficient. Many individuals with clean lipid panels have significant plaque on imaging.<sup>[51]</sup> The blood panel and the imaging are not redundant. They see different things, and I was surprised by how different those things turn out to be.

## Cardio ceiling

Cardiorespiratory fitness, unlike so many of the body's important qualities, is directly measurable. The metric is **VO2max**: the maximum rate at which the body can use oxygen during all-out effort, expressed in milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute.<sup>[52]</sup>

I had expected blood pressure or cholesterol to hold the title of strongest mortality predictor. Neither does. **VO2max** is the single greatest predictor of all-cause mortality among all measurable biomarkers, outperforming blood pressure, cholesterol, BMI, smoking, and chronological age. That claim rests on large epidemiological datasets, including one study of roughly 122,000 patients followed over a decade.<sup>[53,54]</sup> The number that best predicts whether you will be alive in ten years is not on any standard blood panel. It is not collected during any routine physical. Most people have never had it measured. That, to my mind, is a remarkable gap.

Chapter 2 develops the mortality data in full; the point here is narrower. VO2max belongs in the baseline panel because it is a measurement most people have never had, and the strongest predictor of whether you will survive the next decade is absent from every standard lab form and tested in precisely zero routine physicals.

The gold-standard test uses a metabolic cart: a face mask connected to a gas-exchange analyzer while you walk, then run, on a treadmill at progressively increasing intensity until oxygen uptake plateaus. I gathered from the descriptions that this is not a pleasant experience. The test captures more than a single number; it also yields fat-versus-carbohydrate oxidation rates, anaerobic and lactate thresholds, and maximum ventilation capacity. A properly conducted test requires full exhaustion. Tests lasting only five minutes, or tests where the subject holds back, do not produce a true VO2max. Food, sleep, and activity in the days prior should be standardized for reliable tracking.<sup>[55,56,57]</sup>

Lab access is not always practical, and the prospect of running to exhaustion while wearing a face mask is not universally appealing. Validated field alternatives exist. The Cooper Test, a twelve-minute or 1.5-mile all-out run, and a simpler two-minute step test are both scientifically validated submaximal alternatives that provide a reasonable estimate. Andy Galpin uses the Cooper Test as a program endpoint in his training protocols, suggesting it is reliable enough for real-world performance tracking.<sup>[58,59]</sup>

Consumer wearables estimate VO2max from heart rate and activity data, but their margin of error is too large for individuals with moderate-to-high fitness; they may be acceptable only for monitoring very low VO2max. One wearable company claims accuracy within roughly 1 ml/kg/min based on an internal machine-learning study, but independent replication of that claim has not been confirmed. A portable indirect calorimetry device validated by Olav Aleksander Bu's lab was found adequate for VO2 measurement in the field but less accurate for carbon dioxide output, making it unsuitable for fat oxidation calculations.<sup>[60,61,62]</sup>

Three considerations about the trajectory of this number. VO2max declines at approximately 10 percent per decade with aging, while the oxygen cost of daily activities (climbing stairs, lifting a bag of groceries) does not change. Eventually the declining ceiling meets the fixed floor, and capacity for those activities disappears. Think of it as the gap between what your body can do at full effort and what your Tuesday requires. Every decade, the ceiling drops. The floor stays where it is. That is the reason to measure VO2max: not to compete, but to know how much margin remains.

A study of lifelong cross-country skiers in their 80s and 90s found that they maintained VO2max values in the mid-30s; one 92-year-old recorded approximately 38 ml/kg/min.<sup>[63,64]</sup> They had preserved the margin. My first thought was to wonder what the rest of us have been doing instead.

Anyone, regardless of genetics, can reach the top 25th percentile of VO2max for their age and sex with sufficient training; genetics influence the ceiling but do not prevent meaningful improvement. Reaching the top 2 percent, however, is a two-to-three-year project, not a six-week one.<sup>[65,66]</sup>

VO2max is an integrator of sustained fitness work over years. It cannot be rapidly manipulated the way a vitamin D level can be changed with a pill. That resistance to quick fixes is precisely what makes it a superior longevity predictor, because unlike a supplement level it cannot be gamed, which gives me a degree of confidence in it as a metric that I find reassuring.<sup>[67]</sup>

## Wearables

The devices on the wrist and finger occupy a strange position in the measurement landscape. They measure constantly but often imprecisely, and their greatest risk may not be inaccuracy but obsession.

Consumer wearables (Oura Ring, WHOOP, Apple Watch, and others) provide continuous monitoring of **heart rate variability** (**HRV**), resting heart rate, sleep staging, and recovery metrics that were once accessible only in clinical settings. HRV, the beat-to-beat variability driven by vagal nerve activation of cardiac pacemaker cells, reflects the state of the autonomic nervous system. Higher parasympathetic HRV generally indicates better recovery and adaptability, though optimal values are strongly dependent on age, sex, and fitness level; a rough benchmark of 60 milliseconds and above is cited, but individual variation is significant. Low HRV can serve as an early warning of impending illness, sometimes detectable before symptoms appear.<sup>[68,69,70]</sup>

The data is valuable in ways that the precision, unfortunately, does not always match.

Step counts diverge wildly between devices; one host describes them as "totally inaccurate." Deep sleep scores show major cross-device discrepancies. Heart rate readings from two wearables worn during the same activity have been reported as far apart as 125 and 205 beats per minute.<sup>[71,72,73]</sup> I stopped at that figure. Same wrist, same run, eighty beats apart. Chest-strap heart rate monitors remain the gold standard for caloric expenditure during exercise; wrist-based sensors significantly overestimate calories burned. Wearables capture approximately 70 to 80 percent of NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) via step count but miss fidgeting and similar micro-activities entirely.

Andy Galpin, an exercise physiologist, offers a minimal viable tracking protocol: measure three things daily for roughly thirty days. An objective physiological metric such as HRV or resting heart rate. A subjective metric such as mood, energy, or perceived sleep quality. A performance anchor in whatever domain you are trying to improve. After a training block, review the data retrospectively to identify the load or volume thresholds at which injury risk or performance decline sharply increased. He compares it to pitch counts in baseball: the data tells you when to stop, not when to start.<sup>[74,75]</sup>

That retrospective approach is key. Galpin also describes wearable data as a tool for calibration, not daily judgment. Collect data over a training block without adjusting the program. Afterward, look back to find the patterns. The data should inform your decisions rather than govern them, a distinction that sounds simple but proves surprisingly difficult to maintain in practice.

Trends over weeks matter far more than individual daily readings. Clinicians at some longevity centers instruct patients to review trend data only, not daily numbers. The opposite approach, checking a sleep score every morning and feeling distressed when it dips below 80, is a recognized clinical problem. **CBT-I** specialists at the UCSF Osher Center routinely prescribe removing all sleep trackers for six months as part of treatment for sleep-performance anxiety, a condition sometimes called **orthosomnia**.<sup>[76,77]</sup> The prescription is not against tracking. It is against the particular pathology of letting a number on a screen override the body's own signals.

I suspected there was a broader tension here, and there is. Getting a fitness tracker can convert intrinsically rewarding exercise into extrinsically goal-driven step-chasing. The writer David Sedaris has described how a Fitbit turned enjoyable walking into obsessive counting. Rigid biohacking schedules (strict 10 PM bedtimes, absolute alcohol abstinence) can create friction with partners, and relationship quality is itself a longevity factor.<sup>[78,79]</sup> The device that optimizes one metric can degrade another.

The wearable belongs in the baseline panel, but on a leash. Use it to calibrate perceived effort against objective data, then consider removing it to restore proprioceptive intuition.<sup>[80]</sup> The device is a training wheel, not a permanent fixture. The goal, eventually, is to ride without it.

Continuous glucose monitoring, sleep tracking, and at-home gut testing are measurement tools covered in their home chapters (Chapters 6, 7, and 9 respectively). They extend the baseline panel into specific domains; they do not replace it. I began this chapter assuming the answer was obvious, and that confidence did not survive the reading.
