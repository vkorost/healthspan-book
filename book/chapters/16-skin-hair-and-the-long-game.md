# Chapter 16: Skin, Hair, and the Long Game

I had expected, coming into the last body chapter of this book, that the surface would be straightforward. The interior, after all, had been bewildering enough: hormones operating on feedback loops within feedback loops, a gut microbiome as dense and politically fractious as a small country, a brain that nobody fully understands despite it being the organ doing the understanding. Surely the outside, the part you can actually look at, would be simpler. Skin is skin. Hair is hair. You brush your teeth and move on.

It is not simpler. The outside, it turns out, is a readout of the inside, and a more honest one than most people would prefer.

A four-year randomized controlled trial in Australia set out to answer a question that sounds almost too plain for a clinical protocol: what happens to the face when sunscreen is applied daily, without fail, versus when it is applied at the user's own discretion? The answer, published and peer-reviewed, was visible in photographs. Consistent daily use significantly reduced facial aging compared to discretionary use.<sup>[1]</sup> Not a cream marketed as anti-aging. Not a laser. Not a peptide backed by a proprietary trial and a sponsor conflict. Sunscreen, applied every morning as a matter of habit rather than of judgment, outperformed the decision to apply it whenever it seemed like a good idea.

I read that result twice, not because the finding itself was complicated but because of what it implies about the entire industry sitting downstream of it. The appearance business, from retinoid serums to fractional lasers to copper peptide creams, operates downstream of a single upstream variable: cumulative ultraviolet radiation. UV is the dominant external driver of photoaging, the wrinkles, pigmentation changes, and texture degradation that people associate with getting older but that actually track sun exposure more closely than birth year. A 2021 study of 350 patients from two healthcare centers in Colombia, published in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, documented the relationship directly and added a second variable most people would not have guessed: regular exercisers showed significantly less photoaging than non-exercisers.<sup>[2]</sup>

Sunscreen and exercise, not the answer anyone is selling at the beauty counter.

**Appearance and the long game** is the frame for this chapter because appearance is the place where internal health becomes external and visible. Skin quality, hair density, and oral health are not cosmetic trivia; they are readouts. A face that ages faster than its chronological age is telling you something about UV exposure, inflammation, glycation, hormonal status, or all four at once. This chapter covers skin and sun, hair preservation, oral health and its systemic connections, and closes with a reckoning: whether the frontier interventions introduced in Chapter 14 are bets worth watching or bets worth skipping.

## Skin and Sun

The mechanism by which UV ages skin is not subtle, which is part of what makes it so easy for people to ignore. UV damages DNA in keratinocytes and melanocytes, degrades collagen and elastin, and redistributes melanin into the uneven pigmentation that people call age spots.<sup>[3]</sup> Think of a watch face left in a shop window for years on end: the dial yellows, the indices fade unevenly, the crystal develops a cloudiness that has nothing to do with the movement ticking away inside. That is photoaging in miniature. When the upper epidermis sustains UV damage, stem cells in the deeper dermis mobilize to repair the injury, and each repair cycle depletes the reserve a little further. Repeated damage over years and decades exhausts that capacity entirely.<sup>[4]</sup> The visible result is skin that sags, spots, and wrinkles in patterns that follow sun exposure rather than chronological time. Two people born in the same year, one who worked outdoors and one who spent a career at a desk, will show dramatically different aging on every sun-exposed surface, while the skin on their torsos, hidden from UV for a lifetime, will look nearly identical.

A board-certified dermatologist on the Attia channel called sunscreen the single most important topical skin product, full stop: the minimum effective sun-protection intervention.<sup>[5]</sup> The Australian RCT confirmed the claim with the kind of evidence most skincare products never produce. Four years. Randomized. Controlled. Photographs. I noticed that the protocol did not test a superior product; it tested whether people used an ordinary one consistently. The distinction matters more than the SPF number on any label.

Not all sunscreens are equivalent, and this is one of those cases where the practical detail matters as much as the principle. Mineral formulations based on zinc oxide and titanium dioxide sit on the skin surface and physically block UV through reflection. Chemical formulations containing avobenzone and oxybenzone absorb UV energy instead, but they lose blocking efficacy within approximately one hour of outdoor sun exposure, even at high SPF, requiring frequent reapplication.<sup>[6]</sup>

That degradation timeline is a practitioner estimate; the actual rate varies by product and by formulation. But the practical implication is clear enough, and it deserves the blunt version: a person who applies chemical sunscreen once in the morning will need to reapply well before the day is out.

The chemical versions raise an additional concern that has nothing to do with UV blocking. Studies demonstrated that avobenzone and oxybenzone are absorbed into the bloodstream in measurable quantities after topical application, prompting the FDA to mandate animal safety studies on their systemic effects.<sup>[7]</sup> Hormonal disruption in humans from these compounds is suggested but not definitively proven. The absorption is documented. The harm from that absorption is not. That is a gap in the evidence, not a finding. Mineral sunscreens avoid the absorption question entirely, which is why they turn up as the preferred recommendation across every channel that discusses the distinction.

The sun question, though, is not as settled as the dermatology consensus suggests, and the dissent comes from inside the field. Richard Weller, a dermatologist-researcher at the University of Edinburgh, conducted a re-analysis showing that high-sun-exposure populations have lower all-cause mortality.<sup>[8]</sup> Lower. Not higher. I paused on that finding because it does what the best uncomfortable results always do: it takes a question you thought was closed and opens it again. His argument runs through a mechanism connecting skin to the cardiovascular system: UV exposure triggers nitric oxide release from the skin, which dilates blood vessels and confers cardiovascular benefit. The cardiovascular gain, in his analysis, partially or fully offsets the skin cancer risk from non-burning exposure. Weller's position is that only sunburn, not general sun exposure, should be avoided.

This is a minority view within dermatology. Mainstream consensus strongly supports broad sun avoidance and daily sunscreen use. Weller's data is real, his re-analysis is published, and his conclusion contradicts the field he works in. The most deadly melanoma subtypes often occur on sun-unexposed areas of the body, suggesting non-UV causation for the most dangerous forms of skin cancer.<sup>[9]</sup> Mainstream epidemiology confirms that some melanoma subtypes are UV-independent, but UV remains a major overall melanoma risk factor. I am not going to pretend that contradiction is resolved.

The prudent reading: sunscreen remains the first-line defense against photoaging and most skin cancers. The possibility that moderate, non-burning sun exposure carries net cardiovascular benefit through nitric oxide release is worth tracking, but it is not grounds for abandoning protection, particularly for people with fair skin or a history of sun damage. The gap between "some sun is fine" and "sunscreen is optional" is the gap between Weller's data and Weller's recommendation, and most people, including me, do not have the clinical expertise to navigate that gap on their own. When the evidence is this unresolved, the conservative choice is the defensible one.

Beyond UV, skin ages through glycation. Glucose binds non-enzymatically to collagen and elastin, forming advanced glycation end products, or AGEs, that stiffen structural proteins and turn skin yellowish and inelastic.<sup>[10]</sup> The analogy that came to me is a watchmaker's mainspring losing its temper (the metallurgical kind, not the emotional kind): accumulated chemical change robs the material of its ability to flex. That crosslinking happens in the collagen beneath your skin every time blood sugar stays elevated for too long. High-temperature cooking methods (grilling, roasting, charring) substantially increase the dietary AGE load, compounding what the body already produces internally.<sup>[11]</sup> The relative contribution of dietary versus endogenous AGEs to visible skin aging is debated, but the mechanism itself is not in question. The same processed-food, high-sugar dietary pattern that drives metabolic disease (see Chapter 8) shows up on the face as well.

Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, often sustained by that same dietary pattern, activates matrix metalloproteinases that degrade collagen and elastin from the inside.<sup>[12]</sup> I found myself rereading that sentence because of what it says in plain English: the body's own enzymes, switched on by inflammation, dismantle the structural scaffolding that holds skin taut. They are demolition workers on the payroll of the building they are pulling down. Alcohol accelerates the process through three simultaneous mechanisms: it acts as a diuretic depleting skin moisture, it triggers systemic inflammation, and it promotes collagen breakdown.<sup>[13]</sup> Smoking rapidly depletes vitamin C, the essential cofactor for collagen synthesis, so that the body cannot rebuild the collagen it is losing.<sup>[14]</sup> Everything this book's nutrition and metabolic chapters warn against for internal health reasons also writes itself across the face. because the skin is honest about what you feed it.

The exercise finding from the Colombian study points to a mechanism running in the opposite direction, a constructive one. Skeletal muscle during exercise secretes myokines, including IL-15, into the bloodstream, which stimulate collagen synthesis in skin.<sup>[15]</sup> The asymmetry deserves a moment of attention: inflammation takes collagen apart, and exercise, acting through an entirely different pathway, builds it back up. The specific myokine-to-skin chain is stated mechanistically without a direct citation tying it to a single study, so the precise pathway should be held lightly. But the broader finding, that exercise protects against photoaging, comes from observational data in a named journal with a stated sample size.

A 2025 comprehensive review on what some researchers are calling "skin span" found that both aerobic and resistance training improved extracellular matrix gene expression, collagen density, and measurable skin quality metrics.<sup>[16]</sup> The review details are incompletely cited in the source material, so the specific publication cannot be independently confirmed, and that caveat matters. But the direction of the finding is consistent across multiple lines of evidence. The specific magnitude remains uncertain. What is not uncertain is that the same training covered in Chapters 2 through 4 appears to protect the organ people look at in the mirror. The interventions that work best for the inside also work best for the outside, and nobody has yet figured out how to bottle either one.

Four primary mechanisms of facial aging are described in the dermatological literature, and understanding them explains why the beauty industry is so large and why most of it misses the point. Changes in skin quality (texture, pigmentation, collagen loss), volume loss from fat atrophy and bone resorption, structural support loss from ligament laxity, and skin and muscle descent from gravity operate simultaneously and at different rates in different people, which is why two people the same age can look a decade apart.<sup>[17]</sup> What makes that taxonomy useful is not the names but the proportions: the industry targets the first mechanism almost exclusively. The other three are structural, and no product in a jar addresses them. You cannot cream your way out of bone loss.

Estrogen decline during menopause accelerates two of those four mechanisms: facial bone loss and fat compartment atrophy.<sup>[18]</sup> The effect is not cosmetic in the trivial sense. A substantial fraction of facial and skin collagen is lost as estrogen declines during perimenopause and menopause.<sup>[19]</sup> Hormone therapy has a documented role in mitigating this loss.

But the Women's Health Initiative press conference in 2002 (see Chapter 10 for the full account) collapsed hormone therapy prescriptions and removed one of the most effective interventions against these losses from most women's reach. Fewer than 4% of eligible women are currently on any form of hormone therapy.<sup>[20]</sup> That number bears repeating. An intervention with documented evidence behind it, reaching fewer than 4% of the people it could help, that gap between documented benefit and actual use is among the starkest in this book.

The inside-out model of skin health, addressing diet, inflammation, gut health, and systemic wellbeing as primary levers rather than chasing topical solutions, is endorsed by a double board-certified cosmetic dermatologist who has seen enough patients to know that no cream fixes what a bad diet breaks.<sup>[21]</sup> The endorsement is a single practitioner's view. But the logic behind it, that a topical cannot fix what a systemic problem is continuously producing, is harder to argue with, and the reasoning holds on its own terms.

The topical interventions that do carry evidence are fewer than the shelves at the drugstore would suggest, and sorting them from the noise requires exactly the kind of patience this book has been asking of you. **Retinoids**, the vitamin A derivatives that accelerate epidermal cell turnover, are considered a foundational anti-aging topical with strong RCT evidence for improving photoaged skin.<sup>[22]</sup> They work. One dissenting voice raises the concern that the speed of turnover may trade off against long-term skin resilience, an interesting hypothesis that is not established in large trials and runs against the weight of the mainstream evidence.<sup>[23]</sup> Until data says otherwise, retinoids belong in the "works" column.

A copper tripeptide called **GHK-Cu**, a naturally occurring fragment of albumin whose levels decline with age, was tested in a proprietary trial reporting that 80 to 90 percent of subjects aged 33 to 77 showed improvements in skin firmness and density.<sup>[24]</sup> No peer-reviewed publication accompanied the result. The product developer conducted the trial.

The finding sounds impressive in a sentence. Strip the sentence to its structure, a company testing its own product without a comparator group and then selling it, and the impression changes. The finding is interesting. It is not evidence. That structure has appeared elsewhere in this book, and the pattern is always the same: the number is real, the design is not.

Silica, a trace mineral involved in converting proline to hydroxyproline for collagen formation, showed improved skin smoothness, elasticity, and reduced hair and nail brittleness in a 20-week study of women.<sup>[25]</sup> Study design details are not fully cited, which limits the strength of that claim.

Vitamin C, meanwhile, is not merely the antioxidant it is commonly marketed as; it is a direct driver of collagen synthesis and an essential cofactor for collagen cross-linking.<sup>[26]</sup> That role is well established in the biochemistry literature, and the practical implication is plain: anything depleting vitamin C, whether smoking, chronic stress, or poor diet, directly undermines the structural protein that keeps skin intact. The cheapest intervention for skin collagen may be ensuring adequate vitamin C intake and not smoking.

Polypodium leucotomos extract, derived from a tropical fern, has moderate evidence for protection against UV-induced oxidative stress when taken orally.<sup>[27]</sup> It is not a replacement for topical sun protection but may function as a supplementary layer.

An RCT published in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology found that eating one avocado per day for eight weeks, likely through its lutein and zeaxanthin content, helped protect skin from UV damage and oxidative stress in women.<sup>[28]</sup> A single small RCT; sponsored research cannot be excluded. But as dietary interventions go, an avocado is low-risk.

Oxidative imbalance, not chronological age alone, is proposed as a primary determinant of visible aging, with UV, poor diet, smoking, and alcohol as the factors pushing the balance toward damage.<sup>[29]</sup> Genetics plays a significant role too, which is why some people who do everything wrong still look young and some people who do everything right still show their years. That is not fair. It is also not actionable. I realize how unsatisfying that is as a conclusion to a section full of mechanisms and interventions, but it has the advantage of being true. You control the inputs, you do not control the genetics, and the inputs are where the practical advantage actually sits.

## Hair Preservation

DHT, dihydrotestosterone, is the molecule that ends most hair stories, and it is worth understanding why. A byproduct of testosterone's conversion by the enzyme 5-alpha reductase, DHT miniaturizes hair follicles and is the primary driver of androgenic alopecia in both sexes.<sup>[30]</sup> The genetics are polygenic, with the androgen-receptor gene on the X chromosome as a major contributor, meaning maternal inheritance plays a large role but is not the whole picture: paternal contributions matter too.<sup>[31]</sup> The popular belief that you can predict your hair future by looking at your mother's father is partially correct and partially a simplification.

Androgenic alopecia affects millions globally, and the clinical window matters more than most people realize.<sup>[32]</sup> I had to read the next finding twice before I trusted the number. Miniaturized follicles enter a dormant phase of roughly ten years before becoming permanently necrotic; intervention during that window is critical.<sup>[33]</sup> That timeline is a clinical heuristic rather than a finding from a specific published study, but it shapes how hair-loss practitioners think about urgency. Once the follicle is gone, it is gone. The window is closer to a clockwork mechanism than you might expect: while the mainspring holds its coil, a neglected movement can still be restored; let the spring unwind completely, and the metal fatigues past repair. No topical, no laser, no supplement will resurrect a dead follicle.

The non-prescription landscape is noisy and thin on controlled evidence, which is exactly the kind of market that attracts both genuine innovation and outright exploitation. I say that not to dismiss it but to describe the terrain.

**Saw palmetto** functions as a mild natural DHT blocker used by both men and women, but the evidence rests on observational data and expert opinion with few high-quality randomized trials.<sup>[34]</sup> Low-level laser therapy, or photobiomodulation, carries the strongest published evidence in the non-prescription toolkit, with clinical efficacy data published in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology, particularly for people who respond poorly to conventional treatments.<sup>[35]</sup>

Red light devices appear in the personal protocols of several contributors, but the evidence is characterized as "promising but mixed" by one of its own advocates, and most reports remain single-person anecdotes with specific brands involved.<sup>[36]</sup> That characterization, "promising but mixed," is honest. I wish more interventions were described that candidly. Platelet-rich plasma injections concentrate a patient's own platelets and deliver them to the scalp, requiring a physician, with variable protocols and no standardized evidence base.<sup>[37]</sup> Topical caffeine and scalp microneedling are suggestive but uncontrolled.<sup>[38]</sup>

A clinical hierarchy proposed by one hair restoration specialist makes practical sense: start with the least invasive interventions first, meaning nutrition, topicals, and low-level laser therapy, before escalating to surgical procedures.<sup>[39]</sup> That is sensible medicine. It is also, as I understand the pattern, not how most people approach hair loss. Most people start with panic and a credit card.

The prescription side carries harder trade-offs. **Finasteride** blocks 5-alpha reductase and is FDA-approved for androgenic alopecia. The mechanism is clear, and the efficacy data is strong. But post-finasteride syndrome, featuring decreased libido, impotence, depression, and mood disturbance persisting after stopping the drug, is a documented and serious risk. A study by Dr. Khera followed 25 men with post-finasteride syndrome; 8% committed suicide during the follow-up period.<sup>[40]</sup> I realized, reading that figure, that the entire cost-benefit calculation of a cosmetic intervention had inverted in a single sentence. Canada, France, and the UK have added package insert warnings for suicidal ideation in response.

The frequency and mechanism of post-finasteride syndrome remain contested, with prevalence estimates ranging from 5 to 15% across studies. Some researchers cite baseline psychiatric vulnerability as a confound in the suicide data; others propose epigenetic methylation as the mechanism by which finasteride creates lasting changes even after discontinuation.<sup>[41]</sup> The debate is real. The stakes are real. I am not going to resolve it here.

One urologist on the Attia channel does not recommend finasteride for hair loss in any patient, citing limited efficacy for lower hairlines and safety concerns that outweigh the cosmetic benefit.<sup>[42]</sup> That is not the mainstream dermatological position, but the safety signal is real and the potential consequences severe enough that the decision deserves careful clinical conversation, not a casual prescription.

Topical finasteride, applied directly to the scalp to deliver the DHT-blocking effect locally with reduced systemic absorption, is used in some clinical settings but is not FDA-approved and still produces measurable systemic exposure.<sup>[43]</sup> Dutasteride mesotherapy, localized scalp injections administered by a physician, targets specific thinning areas; the same compounding and approval caveats apply.<sup>[44]</sup> Neither option eliminates the core trade-off; both merely relocate it.

The DHT story has a paradox that is difficult to set aside. DHT promotes beard growth while causing scalp recession; 5-alpha reductase inhibitors blunt both effects.<sup>[45]</sup> The same hormone that thickens a jaw-line beard is the one thinning the hairline above it. The duality is built into the receptor biology, which is to say, into the wiring. Biology is not obligated to be consistent, and in this case it is almost perversely not.

A common fear, that creatine supplementation accelerates hair loss by raising DHT, has been directly tested. An RCT of 45 males (see Chapter 3 for the full study design and statistics) found no significant effect on DHT levels or hair density.<sup>[46]</sup> An earlier, smaller study in rugby players had reported that creatine loading raised the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, which is where the fear originated. One contributor claims approximately 20% of people carry genetic variants making them susceptible to creatine-induced hair loss and suggests genetic testing, but that individual-susceptibility claim is unverified and the population-level data does not support it.<sup>[47]</sup> The verdict, and I am satisfied that the data supports it: creatine is likely not your hair problem.

The androgen-receptor saturation model, drawn from the Moira study cited on the Attia channel, offers a partial reassurance for people worried about testosterone and hair. The study estimated that androgen receptors in prostate and follicle cells reach saturation at relatively low testosterone levels, roughly 200 to 250 ng/dL, suggesting that raising testosterone above that threshold may not proportionally worsen androgenic alopecia.<sup>[48]</sup> If the model holds, a man with testosterone of 250 and a man with testosterone of 800 might have similar scalp DHT exposure, because the receptors were already occupied. The theory is not universally accepted and its clinical implications remain debated. It is a hypothesis worth knowing about, not a guarantee.

Beyond DHT, several reversible causes of hair loss are frequently overlooked, and each of them deserves attention before anyone reaches for a hormone blocker. I want to be clear about the word "reversible." It means the hair comes back when the deficiency is corrected. That is a different category of problem from androgenic alopecia, and it calls for a different kind of attention. Iron deficiency, assessed by ferritin and iron panel rather than CBC alone, is a common contributor, particularly in women, though over-supplementation is harmful and testing before treating is essential.<sup>[49]</sup> Thyroid dysfunction, often driven by iodine deficiency, is the most common non-DHT cause of hair loss seen in functional medicine settings.<sup>[50]</sup>

A specific risk that struck me for how quietly it operates: switching from iodized table salt to Himalayan or plain sea salt, a common wellness trend, can worsen iodine deficiency and downstream thyroid function.<sup>[51]</sup> It is the kind of harm that arrives dressed as a health upgrade. Copper deficiency, sometimes driven by excess zinc supplementation through competitive absorption at the intestinal level, can cause both hair loss and premature graying because copper is essential for both hair pigmentation and follicle structural integrity.<sup>[52]</sup> Nutrient deficiencies in protein, amino acids, zinc, and biotin are also implicated, though the evidence base varies by nutrient.<sup>[53]</sup>

Telogen effluvium, the diffuse shedding triggered by illness, surgery, anesthesia, severe stress, or crash dieting, is a different condition entirely from androgenic alopecia, and it is typically self-resolving once the stressor is removed.<sup>[54]</sup> Aggressive or prolonged fasting combined with insufficient protein produces the same diffuse pattern; correcting protein intake addresses it.<sup>[55]</sup> The distinction matters practically: telogen effluvium resolves when the trigger is identified and removed. A person treating telogen effluvium with a DHT blocker is solving the wrong problem.

Estrogen decline in menopause removes a protective effect on follicles, accelerating thinning in women through a mechanism distinct from DHT.<sup>[56]</sup> Progesterone fluctuations during perimenopause drive hair loss that is often misattributed to other causes, including creatine.<sup>[57]</sup> The misattribution matters, and it is worth making explicit: a woman losing hair during perimenopause who blames her creatine supplement and stops taking it has lost the cognitive and muscular benefits of creatine (see Chapter 3) without addressing the actual cause.

Lifestyle factors, including chronic stress, poor blood sugar control, and a pro-inflammatory diet, worsen or accelerate hair loss regardless of the underlying mechanism.<sup>[58]</sup> The same metabolic health that the nutrition chapters defend for internal reasons shows up on the scalp as well. That is the same readout principle that opened this chapter, running through a different surface, and one worth keeping in view.

The honest summary of the non-prescription hair-preservation toolkit: it has options, but very few carry the kind of evidence that makes a confident recommendation possible. Most of the interventions are somewhere between intriguing and unproven, which is not the same as useless, but it is not the same as validated either. The strongest move, and the one I would start with, is to rule out reversible causes first. Check iron. Check thyroid. Check copper. Check protein. Address stress. Then, and only then, consider the interventions that try to block a hormone or stimulate a follicle.

## Oral Health and the Body

The human mouth hosts over 700 bacterial species.<sup>[59]</sup> Over 700. I stopped on that number. That figure alone should reframe oral health as something far larger than cavities and fresh breath. The mouth is a gateway, and when the gateway is inflamed, what passes through it reaches the rest of the body.

When gum tissue becomes chronically inflamed, bacteria cross into the bloodstream through what some practitioners call "leaky gums," a concept analogous to the intestinal permeability discussed in Chapter 9: the barrier fails, and organisms that should stay local go systemic.<sup>[60]</sup> The consequences, documented epidemiologically, are not small. A National Institute of Aging study following more than 8,000 adults aged 65 and older found that those with gum disease had significantly higher risks of cardiovascular disease and related mortality.<sup>[61]</sup> The association is observational; causation is not established.

But the epidemiological link between oral inflammation and systemic disease extends well beyond the cardiovascular system: gum disease is associated with increased risk of dementia and Alzheimer's disease, with oral bacteria capable of migrating to the brain and triggering neuroinflammation.<sup>[62]</sup> Streptococcus mutans, the primary cavity bacterium, can enter the bloodstream and potentially cross the blood-brain barrier.<sup>[63]</sup> That is a finding that changes the way you think about brushing.

I had assumed, coming into this section, that the oral material would be the quietest part of the chapter, a practical note about flossing before the book moved on to its conclusion. and I could not have been more wrong.

Poor oral hygiene is linked to inflammatory bowel disease as well; oral bacteria can survive the trip through stomach acid, colonize the gut, and contribute to conditions including IBD.<sup>[64]</sup> The epidemiological connections run wider still. Whole-body bone health, metabolic disease, and systemic inflammation on one side; the state of the gums on the other. The association is consistent across multiple epidemiological studies.<sup>[65]</sup><sup>[66]</sup> I had not expected the mouth to be this central, and the evidence leaves little room for doubt.

The oral microbiome does something else that most people never learn, and this is among the most practically important findings in the entire chapter: oral bacteria convert dietary nitrate precursors into nitric oxide, the molecule that dilates blood vessels, activates mitochondrial biogenesis, supports cognition, and underpins erectile function.<sup>[67]</sup> Antibacterial toothpastes and mouthwashes kill the nitrate-converting bacterial population indiscriminately. Daily antiseptic mouthwash use has been shown to reduce systemic nitric oxide production and raise blood pressure (see Chapter 8 for the full mechanism and the human studies supporting it).<sup>[68]</sup>

Consider what that means in practice. A person uses mouthwash every morning because they believe it is good hygiene. That mouthwash kills bacteria they need for blood pressure regulation, vascular function, and possibly cognitive and sexual health. A "cosmetic" oral care habit producing a measurable cardiovascular effect is the sort of finding that rearranges your understanding of what counts as health behavior. I would not have guessed, before reading the evidence, that a bottle of mouthwash could be meaningfully connected to blood pressure, but the evidence says the connection is real.

The practical oral care toolkit, once you strip away the marketing and the shelf after shelf of products competing for attention, is simpler than the industry makes it look. Flossing introduces oxygen under the gum line, killing the anaerobic bacteria responsible for bleeding gums, and is beneficial even when no food is visibly trapped between teeth.<sup>[69]</sup><sup>[70]</sup> The mechanism works by changing the chemistry beneath the surface rather than removing trapped food. Tongue scraping, an Ayurvedic practice, reduces bacterial load from the tongue surface and is cited across multiple contributors as an evidence-based component of oral hygiene.<sup>[71]</sup>

Oil pulling, swishing coconut or ozonated oil for five to ten minutes upon waking, has anecdotal reports of shifting oral microbiome profiles from high-risk toward healthier patterns.<sup>[72]</sup> The dramatic improvements described rest on individual reports, not controlled trials. The sample sizes are small. The claims are large. The gap between those two things is where caution lives.

Hydroxyapatite toothpaste, a biocompatible mineral the body recognizes and incorporates into enamel surfaces, is recommended over fluoride toothpaste in several integrative protocols for remineralization without the toxicity concerns that fluoride carries at high doses.<sup>[73]</sup> The fluoride-versus-hydroxyapatite debate is ongoing, with some comparative RCT data favoring hydroxyapatite for remineralization in specific populations, though fluoride remains the mainstream standard endorsed by public health bodies.<sup>[74]</sup> The dose-response for fluoride neurotoxicity at water-fluoridation concentrations is actively debated, and integrative practitioners raise concerns about fluoride that public health consensus does not share.<sup>[75]</sup> I confess I cannot settle that question. The data pulls in opposite directions and neither side has a decisive trial.

Jaw alignment and bite balance influence the nervous system, vagal tone, and postural balance in ways that most people do not associate with a trip to the dentist.<sup>[76]</sup> The connection sounds implausible until you consider that the jaw is anchored to the base of the skull, and tension in one affects the other. A diet requiring significant mastication, the crunchy, whole, unprocessed foods that the nutrition chapters recommend for other reasons, promotes proper jaw development and facial symmetry.<sup>[77]</sup> Ultra-processed soft-food diets contribute to inadequate jaw development. Mouth breathing during rest and sleep is associated with deteriorated sleep quality, reduced oxygenation, and altered jaw development.<sup>[78]</sup> Mouth taping during sleep, which promotes nasal breathing, has anecdotal reports of improved blood oxygen saturation, though the evidence base is limited to small samples without formal replication.<sup>[79]</sup>

Spouses and romantic partners share very similar gut and oral microbiome profiles, likely through salivary exchange via kissing.<sup>[80]</sup> The oral microbiome, in other words, is relational. The person you live with shapes the bacteria in your mouth, and those bacteria shape your nitric oxide production, your gum health, and potentially your cardiovascular and cognitive trajectory. I filed that finding alongside the mouthwash result: two pieces of evidence most people would never connect, both pointing toward the same conclusion, that oral health reaches further than anyone's morning routine assumes.

The biological dentistry community holds more aggressive views: that root canals, retained wisdom teeth, and metal fillings create hidden chronic infection sites burdening the immune system and contributing to autoimmune disease.<sup>[81]</sup> These are minority positions explicitly contested by mainstream dentistry, and following them without qualified guidance could result in unnecessary procedures. The tooth-organ meridian system, in which specific teeth are mapped to specific organs, is not recognized in mainstream dentistry or medicine.<sup>[82]</sup> The claims are noted here because they circulate widely in integrative health channels. They should be evaluated with a clinician who understands both frameworks, not adopted from an episode.

## The Long Game

This is the final chapter of the body of this book, and it is the right place to ask whether the frontier interventions catalogued in Chapter 14 add up to something worth acting on.

The longevity shelf (NAD precursors, spermidine, urolithin A, sulforaphane, senolytics) and the prescription frontier (rapamycin, metformin, statins, TRT, growth-hormone secretagogues) represent the field's best guesses at interventions that might extend healthspan beyond what training, sleep, nutrition, and the other chapters in this book can deliver. The word is "guesses."

Rapamycin extends lifespan in every model organism tested but has no completed human longevity RCT (Chapter 14). The TAME trial, the first FDA-sanctioned trial with aging as a clinical endpoint, is still running (Chapter 14). Metformin blunts aerobic and strength adaptation in older adults, creating a direct trade-off with the exercise gains described in Chapters 2, 3, and 4 (Chapter 14). NAD precursor supplements vary wildly in quality, with one study finding that many commercial preparations do not contain their labeled amounts (Chapter 14). Senescent cells accumulate in the scalp and elsewhere, creating pro-inflammatory microenvironments that degrade tissue health over time, and senolytic approaches to clearing them are theoretically appealing, but the science is early-stage and most evidence remains in vitro or in initial clinical trials.<sup>[83]</sup>

None of this means the frontier is empty. It means the frontier is unfinished. I am not dismissing any of it; I am describing where the evidence currently stands. The difference between a bet worth watching and an actionable protocol is the distance between a hypothesis and a confirmed result. That distance, for most of these compounds, is measured in years of trials not yet concluded.

What is not unfinished is the material in the preceding fifteen chapters. Sunscreen, applied daily, reduces facial aging in a four-year RCT. Exercise protects skin in a 350-patient observational study. Fixing an iron deficiency, a thyroid problem, or a copper imbalance can stop hair loss that no laser helmet will reverse. Flossing introduces oxygen that kills the bacteria linked to cardiovascular disease and dementia. Skipping antibacterial mouthwash preserves the nitric oxide pathway that supports blood pressure, cognition, and erectile function. These are sourced, cited, and, in several cases, controlled.

I am aware of how unremarkable that list sounds. These are not dramatic interventions. They do not require a subscription, a compounding pharmacy, or a physician willing to prescribe off-label. They require consistency. The Australian sunscreen trial tested not whether a better sunscreen existed, but whether people used the one they had. The ones who did looked younger.

The long game in appearance is the same long game that runs through this entire book. The interventions with the strongest evidence are the ones that are the least exciting to talk about. They have no proprietary trials funded by the company that makes them. They work, slowly, over years, in people who do them without stopping (rather like a well-built clock running on nothing more remarkable than regular winding).

The frontier will catch up. Some of the compounds on the longevity shelf will be validated in human trials. Some will turn out to have been expensive placebos. The prescription frontier will produce its first completed human aging trial, and the field will finally have data instead of extrapolations from mice and cell cultures. Until then, the actionable material is in the chapters behind this one, the work that compounds quietly across years without waiting for a trial to end.

What any single marker says today matters less than what it says next year, and the year after that. This book began with measurement because measurement is how intervention becomes iteration: you establish a baseline, you change a behavior, and you retest to learn whether the change did what you expected. That loop, introduced in Chapter 1, is what converts a health intention into a health outcome. The skin and the scalp and the gums are the visible surface of that loop.

I began this chapter expecting the surface to be simple, and found instead a mirror of everything beneath it. The mechanisms that govern it, UV protection, inflammation control, nutrient sufficiency, consistent exercise, are the same mechanisms that govern everything underneath. When the numbers improve inside, the surface tends to follow, and the mirror becomes a kinder piece of furniture.
