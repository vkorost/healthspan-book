# Chapter 5: Protein, Carbs, Fats

A sleeve of Oreo cookies every day for two weeks dropped LDL cholesterol by 71 percent. The reduction was larger and faster than the statin previously taken in the same body. The person running the experiment on himself was Nick Norwitz, an MD-PhD candidate who had spent years studying the ketogenic diet, and he published it as a peer-reviewed case report. The point was not that Oreos are healthy. The point was a mechanism: in a lean, insulin-sensitive person running on fat, the liver exports LDL particles to shuttle fuel to tissues; feed that person carbohydrates and the export pathway shuts down.<sup>[1]</sup> The study proved nothing about Oreos and everything about the complexity of the relationship between what you eat and what happens in your blood. I will admit that a sentence containing both "Oreo cookies" and "peer-reviewed case report" got my attention immediately. That complexity, the sheer unpredictability of how food behaves once it enters the body, is the subject of this chapter.

The named concept here is **the macro decisions**: how much protein, which carbohydrates, and what fats. Think of them as the base recipe, the flour-butter-eggs proportions that determine the dish before anyone reaches for the spice rack or the garnish. These are the structural choices that shape a diet before anyone talks about supplements, timing, or biohacking. They are also the choices on which the loudest voices in health media disagree most sharply. Several of the positions that follow are genuinely contested, with credentialed researchers on both sides citing real data. The chapter presents them as the live debates they are, not as settled answers.

## Protein: How Much and Why It Matters

The Recommended Dietary Allowance for protein, 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, was established using nitrogen balance studies conducted on lean, sedentary young men weighing roughly 65 to 70 kilograms.<sup>[2]</sup> It represents the minimum intake to prevent clinical deficiency. It does not represent the intake needed to build or maintain muscle, support bone density, or sustain metabolic health in an active adult. I had to read that distinction twice, because the gap between "enough to avoid deficiency" and "enough to function well" turns out to be enormous. That is the difference between "not deficient" and "actually sufficient." The 2025 to 2026 U.S. Dietary Guidelines raised the recommendation to 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day, meaningfully above the old RDA and closer to what most researchers in this space now advocate.<sup>[3][89]</sup> That is a doubling at the upper end, and it took decades of accumulated evidence to move the official number that far.

The rationale for higher protein targets rests on a simple physiological fact. Muscle is not just a tissue that moves limbs. It functions as an endocrine organ comprising roughly 40 percent of body weight, and its maintenance depends on both exercise stimulus and adequate protein supply, particularly essential amino acids.<sup>[4]</sup> After approximately age 40, muscle mass declines at roughly 3 percent per year, which is an unsettling rate once you stop to calculate what it means over a decade or two. MRI the evidence indicates that what appears to be thigh muscle on a standard measurement is increasingly replaced by intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), which drives insulin resistance independently of overall body-fat percentage.<sup>[5]</sup> The limb looks unchanged from the outside. Inside, it has been quietly marbling. This process is called anabolic resistance: the muscle-protein-synthesis response to both protein and exercise becomes blunted with age. Inactivity, however, is a larger driver of this blunting than aging itself, and resistance training is the primary way to override it.<sup>[6]</sup> The encouraging part of that sentence is worth underlining: the largest contributor is the one you can change.

Bones are approximately 50 percent protein, a proportion I would not have guessed before reading this material, and it is why adequate intake matters for skeletal integrity as well as for muscle.<sup>[7]</sup> Frailty from sarcopenia is one of the most devastating health outcomes in later life, and preventing it is the practical rationale for higher protein targets regardless of the longevity-pathway concerns discussed below.<sup>[8]</sup>

What does "higher" mean in practice? This is where the agreement ends and the arguments begin. The range across the major voices is wide enough to give anyone shopping for certainty a headache. Peter Attia targets 1 gram per pound (roughly 2.2 grams per kilogram) of body weight per day, a target shared publicly by Andy Galpin, Andrew Huberman, and Dave Asprey.<sup>[9]</sup> Dominic D'Agostino recommends approximately double the old RDA, roughly 1.6 grams per kilogram, after his own experience re-evaluating protein needs.<sup>[10]</sup> For people carrying extra weight, the consensus is to calculate targets against goal body weight, not current weight.<sup>[11]</sup>

One particular caution applies to users of GLP-1 drugs. Roughly 40 percent of weight lost on these medications comes from lean mass rather than fat, making adequate protein and resistance training especially important for anyone on semaglutide or tirzepatide.<sup>[12]</sup> That number bears repeating. Nearly half of what these drugs remove is the tissue you most want to keep.

### The Protein use Hypothesis

Steve Simpson and David Raubenheimer, working in Australia, proposed that animals consume food until they have obtained sufficient protein for genetic fitness. If the protein density of the available food is dilute, the animal keeps eating, potentially driving overcaloric intake.<sup>[13]</sup> The hypothesis has been demonstrated in animal models. Whether it applies meaningfully to humans in free-living conditions is debated, but it offers a plausible explanation for why high-protein diets produce spontaneous caloric reduction even without deliberate restriction: the body stops seeking food because the protein signal has been satisfied. The implication, if the hypothesis extends to humans, is striking. It suggests that the portion size matters less than the protein concentration of what is on the plate; that the body is not simply counting calories but monitoring the nutritional density of each bite, and will keep demanding food until the protein quota is met. A dilute protein supply, in this framework, is quite literally a recipe for overeating.

### The mTOR Debate

This is a genuine disagreement, not a matter of one side being wrong. High protein intake activates mTOR and IGF-1, which are pro-growth signals associated with faster biological aging in animal models.<sup>[14]</sup> Valter Longo argues that epidemiological data from the Physicians' and Nurses' Study, Harvard studies, and Swedish cohorts consistently associate high animal protein with increased diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular mortality. He points to the Okinawan longevity population, historically among the lowest protein consumers globally, and to a century of rodent data showing that protein restriction and methionine restriction are the only dietary interventions besides caloric restriction that reliably extend lifespan.<sup>[15]</sup> A century of animal data pointing in the same direction is not nothing, and the Okinawan example adds an observational human population to the case.

The counterargument is equally concrete, and I notice it rests on a different kind of evidence. Attia and Rhonda Patrick argue that the risk of sarcopenia and frailty in older adults is immediate and documented, while the mTOR aging risk is theoretical in humans and derived primarily from rodent and C. elegans models.<sup>[16]</sup> They also note that exercise, an established healthspan intervention, activates mTOR at higher magnitudes than dietary protein does. If mTOR activation were categorically harmful, resistance training would shorten lives, yet decades of evidence show it does the opposite and extends both function and longevity.

Both sides have commercial interests, and this is worth stating plainly. Longo is commercially associated with Prolon, a **fasting-mimicking diet** product that aligns with protein restriction. Attia, Huberman, and Galpin are affiliated with a protein bar company.<sup>[17]</sup> Neither disclosure invalidates either argument. But both exist. It does mean both should be read with the ingredients list visible, so to speak.

I am not going to pretend this debate is settled, because it is not. The evidence supporting higher protein targets for muscle preservation and metabolic health in active adults rests on consistent mechanistic data, updated dietary guidelines, and broad expert consensus across the field. The evidence that this level of intake accelerates biological aging in humans, while mechanistically plausible from animal models, has not been confirmed by long-term human trials. The two positions remain a live scientific debate.<sup>[90]</sup>

## Protein Timing and the Animal-Plant Question

It is not only total daily protein that matters. Distribution across meals determines how effectively the body uses what it receives. Leucine, an essential amino acid, acts as the primary trigger for the mTOR pathway that initiates muscle protein synthesis (MPS).<sup>[18]</sup> Each meal must deliver enough leucine to independently activate this response. Think of leucine as the pilot light on a stovetop burner: without it, the gas flows but nothing ignites. Distributing protein evenly, rather than loading it into one large evening meal, produces superior net daily anabolism.<sup>[19]</sup>

The first meal of the day produces the largest MPS response to dietary protein, adding a circadian argument to the muscle argument: an early protein-rich meal may function as a zeitgeber, phase-shifting the body clock earlier.<sup>[20]</sup> That is two reasons to front-load protein, yet survey data consistently show that most people load the bulk of their intake into the evening meal.

How much per meal? The estimates depend on who did the training and how much of it. The classic figure is roughly 20 grams per meal, but that number comes from studies using low training volumes. When McNaughton and colleagues tested 20 versus 40 grams after a full-body resistance session of roughly 24 sets, the 40-gram dose produced greater MPS.<sup>[21]</sup> Jorn Trommelen took the question further: 25 versus 100 grams of slow-digesting milk protein post-exercise. The 100-gram dose produced significantly greater synthesis, directly challenging the idea of a fixed per-meal ceiling.<sup>[22]</sup> I had assumed the body could only use so much protein at once, since a figure of roughly 30 grams has floated around dietary advice for years. The Trommelen result overturned that assumption cleanly. The practical range for most people falls between 30 and 50 grams of high-quality protein per meal, with the higher end appropriate after demanding training sessions.<sup>[23]</sup>

Women face specific challenges in this space, and the challenges begin with the research itself. Most MPS dose-response research has been conducted on male subjects.<sup>[24]</sup> That gap matters.

Women's blood glucose returns to resting baseline within roughly 60 minutes post-exercise, while men's takes several hours, which means the post-exercise anabolic window may be shorter for women and immediate post-exercise protein more important.<sup>[25]</sup> I had not expected that asymmetry. Women who compress their feeding window through **time-restricted eating** risk failing to reach daily protein targets, and protein sufficiency should take priority over eating-window compression in this population.<sup>[26]</sup>

The animal-versus-plant protein question is primarily a question of leucine density and bioavailability. Plant protein sources are lower in both, meaning that achieving equivalent MPS from plant sources requires higher total gram intake.<sup>[27]</sup>

The Stanford Twins Study, published in JAMA Network Open in 2024, randomized identical twins to vegan versus omnivore diets for eight weeks. The vegan arm showed lower LDL and lower fasting insulin, which are favorable cardiovascular markers.<sup>[28]</sup> I thought the twin design was particularly clever, and for anyone skeptical of dietary studies: it controls for genetics entirely, which is the variable that haunts most dietary comparisons and makes so many of them inconclusive. A well-planned plant-based diet can meet amino acid requirements. It is achievable. But it requires supplementation to cover nutrients naturally present in animal foods: B12, heme iron, and complete essential amino acid profiles.<sup>[29]</sup>

Steven Gundry sits at the opposite extreme, recommending no more than 20 grams of animal protein per day, roughly three eggs or three ounces of grass-fed beef, and preferring plant-based sources for the remainder.<sup>[30]</sup> This is the minority position among the speakers sampled. I note how far it sits from the majority view. The majority, including Attia, Patrick, Gabrielle Lyon, D'Agostino, Galpin, and Huberman, place the practical daily range between 1 and 2.2 grams per kilogram of total body weight, distributed across three or four meals.<sup>[31]</sup>

One observation from Gundry's framework warrants mention even for those who reject his overall position. He distinguishes between animal protein sources based on their sugar content: beef, lamb, pork, and cow's milk contain Neu5Gc, a sugar molecule against which humans develop antibodies, while chicken and fish contain Neu5Ac, which lines human joints and blood vessels without triggering the same immune reaction.<sup>[32]</sup> This is Gundry's hypothesis, not mainstream consensus, and the human clinical relevance of Neu5Gc antibodies remains debated. The practical advice that emerges from it, though, to favor fish and poultry over red meat as primary animal protein sources, aligns with broader dietary guidance regardless of whether the specific mechanism holds. I take some comfort in that convergence, since it suggests the practical advice survives even if the underlying mechanism does not.

## The Carbohydrate Range

No single carbohydrate level is optimal for all humans. Christopher Gardner's DIETFITS and A-to-Z studies demonstrate this directly: individual responses to low-carb and higher-carb diets vary enormously, and when total calories and protein are equated, no significant difference in fat loss emerges between the two approaches.<sup>[33][91]</sup> I expected those studies to produce a winner. They did not. The highest-evidence universal recommendation on carbohydrates is to reduce ultra-processed refined carbohydrates regardless of macronutrient target.<sup>[34]</sup>

Carbohydrates are the dominant driver of insulin secretion among macronutrients. Fat produces a minimal insulin response. Protein, a moderate one.<sup>[35]</sup> For anyone managing blood sugar or insulin sensitivity, carbohydrate quantity is the primary dietary lever.

I want to be precise here, because this point is easily misread. The processing of carbohydrates matters as much as the quantity. Grinding, juicing, removing fiber, and dehydrating all concentrate sugar molecules and dramatically accelerate the blood glucose rise that drives downstream metabolic harm.<sup>[36]</sup>

The distinction is roughly that between a whole apple sitting on a counter and the same apple run through a blender: the sugar content is identical, but what changes is the speed at which it arrives in the bloodstream. One is a slow simmer. The other is a flash in a hot pan.

### Ketogenic Eating

The ketogenic diet restricts carbohydrates to roughly 50 grams per day or less, with approximately 75 percent of calories from fat and 20 percent from protein.<sup>[37]</sup> Fifty grams is roughly a cup of rice or a single large banana. I mention those equivalents because the number itself is abstract; the banana is concrete. A high-protein variant, endorsed by D'Agostino, keeps the same carbohydrate floor but shifts the ratio toward more protein and less fat, and is generally considered more practical for body composition goals.<sup>[38]</sup>

A well-controlled ketogenic diet consistently produces substantial weight loss when adhered to. The mechanism is primarily spontaneous caloric reduction from protein satiation and the elimination of hyper-palatable carbohydrate-fat combinations, not a unique metabolic advantage.<sup>[39]</sup> When protein and calories are carefully matched against a higher-carbohydrate diet, the apparent fat-loss advantage disappears.<sup>[40]</sup>

I noticed that finding and paused on it, because the implication is counterintuitive. The ketogenic diet works. It works well. But it works by making you eat less, not by unlocking a special metabolic pathway. That finding is acknowledged by multiple speakers and represents something close to consensus in the research community, even among keto advocates.

Where the ketogenic diet has its strongest clinical footing is in epilepsy, where it reduces seizure frequency through GABA and glutamate modulation.<sup>[41]</sup> Its use for treatment-refractory depression shows decent early evidence through the same pathway, though the data are not strong enough for first-line recommendation.<sup>[42]</sup> Attia views ketogenic eating as a prescription-level metabolic therapy best suited for epilepsy, specific metabolic conditions, and obesity with insulin resistance, not as a lifestyle default.<sup>[43]</sup> The word "prescription" is doing real work in that sentence, and Attia uses it deliberately.

The practical challenges are predictable. The kidneys excrete sodium at approximately four times the normal rate during ketosis, making electrolyte supplementation essential during adaptation.<sup>[44]</sup> The "keto flu" that discourages many newcomers is largely a sodium deficit. Not a rejection signal. A plumbing problem. The social difficulty of avoiding carbohydrates in a food environment built around them is a genuine barrier regardless of metabolic benefit.<sup>[45]</sup> Attia recommends a gradual transition over four to six weeks rather than abrupt carbohydrate elimination.<sup>[46]</sup>

A lean mass hyper-responder phenotype affects a minority of lean, insulin-sensitive individuals on ketogenic diets, causing dramatic LDL elevation through a specific liver fat-export mechanism. This is the mechanism Norwitz demonstrated with his Oreo experiment: feeding carbohydrates to an LMHR shuts down the fat-export pathway and LDL plummets.<sup>[47]</sup> Lipid monitoring, particularly ApoB, is warranted before and during ketogenic dieting for this reason. APOE4 carriers, roughly 30 percent of the population, may have additional adverse lipid responses to high-fat diets and should consider genetic testing before starting.<sup>[48]</sup>

### Long-Term Strict Ketosis

Both Gundry and Greenfield state that permanent strict ketosis is not a natural or optimal long-term state. I find the reasoning here worth following closely. Ancestral dietary patterns, they argue, involved alternation between carbohydrate abundance and scarcity. Cyclical approaches, with very low carbohydrates most of the time and deliberate carbohydrate refeeds one to two times per week, are positioned as more physiologically natural.<sup>[49]</sup>

Greenfield has reported what he calls "nagging issues" after years on strict keto, including concerns about thyroid output and testosterone, and shifted toward a higher-carbohydrate approach.<sup>[50]</sup> That shift is telling. Extended strict carbohydrate restriction may suppress thyroid function and secondarily testosterone in some individuals; periodic refeeds appear to mitigate this for some practitioners, though the evidence is primarily anecdotal and mechanistic rather than trial-based.<sup>[51]</sup>

Not all carbohydrates are banished even in a keto-adjacent framework. Starchy carbohydrates have a legitimate role in high-intensity training and in sleep. Consuming carbohydrates in the evening supports the tryptophan-to-serotonin-to-melatonin conversion pathway, and people who eliminate evening carbohydrates may experience impaired sleep quality.<sup>[52]</sup>

A tactical approach used by some high-intensity athletes runs ketogenic at breakfast and lunch, with carbohydrates consumed only around training sessions to support glycogen restoration.<sup>[53]</sup> I was struck by how precisely timed this protocol is. It treats carbohydrates less as a food group and more as a tool to be deployed at specific moments.

The Jay Feldman bioenergetic view holds that carbohydrates are foundational fuel and that chronic fat-burning is a stress response rather than a healthy metabolic state. Greenfield explored this personally and did not reach a definitive conclusion.<sup>[54]</sup> It is a theoretical counterpoint to the metabolic-flexibility paradigm, not a consensus dissent.

## The Mediterranean Pattern

If forced to name a single dietary pattern with the strongest evidence base, most speakers across these channels point to the Mediterranean diet, while immediately noting its weaknesses: a definition loose enough to permit indulgent interpretations, and a risk of inadequate protein intake.<sup>[55]</sup> It is the rare nutritional recommendation that almost everyone endorses and almost everyone qualifies in the same breath.

The core protocol is specific enough to be actionable: extra-virgin olive oil daily (four to five tablespoons), fatty fish at least twice per week, nuts and seeds daily, abundant fruits and vegetables, moderate whole grains, and minimal added sugars and **seed oils**.<sup>[56]</sup> The label matters less than the contents. The PREDIMED study, the primary randomized controlled trial supporting Mediterranean eating for cardiovascular risk reduction, found significant benefit in high-risk individuals randomized to Mediterranean diet supplemented with either extra-virgin olive oil or nuts. The study was partially retracted in 2018 due to randomization protocol deviations and republished after re-analysis; the overall cardiovascular benefit finding was upheld, but the retraction history warrants disclosure.<sup>[57]</sup> I bring up the retraction because it matters. A study cited this often should have its full record visible.

Extra-virgin olive oil at 30 grams per day (approximately one shot glass) is associated with the lowest all-cause mortality in dose-response studies, with mechanisms including improvement in blood lipid profiles, nitric oxide production from **polyphenols**, and anti-atherogenic effects.<sup>[58]</sup> One shot glass. That is the dose, which works out to about two tablespoons more than most people use.

Quality matters considerably. An estimated 80 percent of U.S. extra-virgin olive oil is reportedly fraudulent or adulterated.<sup>[59]</sup> I confess that number stopped me. Genuine high-polyphenol oil should be stored in dark glass, consumed before expiration, and should produce a spicy throat sensation indicating adequate polyphenol content. The 80 percent fraud figure is an industry estimate, not a peer-reviewed finding. But if it is even roughly correct, the ingredient most central to this diet is the one most likely to be counterfeit on the shelf.

Longo views the Mediterranean diet as good but incomplete, ranking it below his full longevity diet protocol.<sup>[60]</sup> He notes that historical Mediterranean populations followed strict seasonality rules now abandoned, and that southern Europeans are "among the most frail in Europe" despite their dietary reputation. He attributes the gap to modern populations no longer following the traditional pattern. I cannot adjudicate that claim, but it is a useful corrective to anyone who treats the label itself as a guarantee.

The deeper problem with "Mediterranean diet" as a recommendation, and I think it is a serious one, is that individual responses vary significantly. Gut microbiome composition determines roughly 20 to 30 percent of glucose variability, with genetics contributing another 20 percent.<sup>[61]</sup> What works metabolically for one person eating olive oil, fish, and vegetables may not work for another with different gut bacteria and different genetic predispositions. The same recipe, different kitchens, different results. A minority of people on Mediterranean-style diets develop persistent autoimmune issues that resolve on elimination diets, suggesting that genetic and microbiome factors limit universal applicability.<sup>[62]</sup>

The Mediterranean pattern has the broadest epidemiological support among named dietary approaches. But the majority of its longevity data is observational, and confounders (lower stress, more physical activity, stronger social ties, less ultra-processed food) are difficult to separate from the diet itself. The specific protocol items, not the label alone, are the actionable guidance.<sup>[92]</sup>

## Omega-3 Fats: The Clearest Signal

**DHA** (docosahexaenoic acid) constitutes roughly 97 percent of the omega-3 fatty acids in the brain.<sup>[63]</sup> That is not a rounding error or a rough estimate. Virtually all of the omega-3 in the organ responsible for every thought you have ever had is a single molecule. Higher blood levels of omega-3s are associated with larger brain volumes in older adults and with better cognitive test performance and lower dementia risk, though these are observational associations, not causal demonstrations.<sup>[64]</sup>

**EPA** has antidepressant effects that parallel SSRI mechanisms, operating through inflammation reduction; omega-3 supplements are effective for major depressive disorder, particularly in people with high inflammatory markers.<sup>[65]</sup> A fat that the brain depends on for structure and that also modulates mood is not a trivial nutrient. I notice that this is one of the few supplementation signals in the entire book where the evidence lines up without a major asterisk.

The practical question has a clear answer. If you are not eating fatty fish (salmon, sardines, anchovies, herring, mackerel) twice a week, omega-3 deficiency is almost certain.<sup>[66]</sup> Plant-based ALA from chia, flax, and hemp seeds converts to EPA and DHA at less than 5 percent efficiency, which means it cannot substitute for marine sources.<sup>[67][93]</sup> Less than 5 percent. That is not a conversion; it is a rounding error with a nutritional label. The supplementation dose range for general health is 1,000 to 2,000 milligrams of EPA plus DHA daily; many people need 2 to 4 grams to achieve an adequate **omega-3 index**.<sup>[68]</sup> For vegans, algae oil supplements providing EPA and DHA directly are the appropriate alternative.<sup>[69]</sup>

The omega-6 to omega-3 ratio in ancestral human diets was approximately 2 to 3:1. Modern diets high in seed oils have pushed this to ratios of 10:1 to 40:1, and the imbalance drives systemic inflammation through competitive enzyme inhibition: excess omega-6 crowds out omega-3 on the elongation pathways that produce EPA and DHA.<sup>[70]</sup> I paused on those numbers. A shift from roughly 3:1 to as high as 40:1 is not a gradual dietary drift; it is a wholesale inversion of the balance the body evolved under.

One caution applies to high-dose supplementation. Reports of increased atrial fibrillation risk at 3,000 milligrams per day or above have surfaced, particularly in people with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions.<sup>[71]</sup> Causation versus correlation is not established. The evidence is mixed. Some practitioners have reduced from 3 grams to 2 grams as a precaution while others continue higher doses.

Oxidized fish oil creates inflammation rather than reducing it, so quality, freshness, and storage matter.<sup>[72]</sup> A supplement taken for its anti-inflammatory properties that has gone rancid on the shelf is worse than no supplement at all.

Grass-fed beef carries a better omega-3 to omega-6 ratio than conventional grain-fed beef.<sup>[73]</sup> Pastured egg yolks provide omega-3 fats along with lutein and zeaxanthin; omega-3-enriched eggs are produced by feeding chickens flaxseed or algae.<sup>[74]</sup>

The Cochrane meta-analysis of 2017 found a 29 to 31 percent cardiovascular risk reduction from substituting polyunsaturated fats for saturated fats, though the omega-3s included in the analysis may account for part of the benefit, making it uncertain whether omega-6 PUFAs alone drive the reduction.<sup>[75]</sup>

Of all the fat-related supplementation signals in this chapter, marine omega-3s are the clearest. The brain needs them. The mood data support them. Most modern diets fall short. That is about as close to a consensus recommendation as nutrition science ever gets.

## The Seed Oil Debate

This is the most contested nutrition topic in the dataset, and the chapter would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. I have now read the arguments from both sides several times, and I cannot tell you which is right. The disagreement is not between researchers and quacks. It is between credentialed people with data who read the same literature and reach opposite conclusions. That alone should give any consumer pause, and it is a reason to read both sides with their funding sources visible.

The positions are worth stating carefully, because the usual simplification does not survive contact with the actual arguments. On one side: Layne Norton, Alan Aragon, and Peter Attia argue that seed oils are not uniquely harmful under realistic dietary conditions. The ill effects attributed to them are largely explained by the ultra-processed food context in which they appear. Remove the processed food and the apparent seed oil signal disappears.<sup>[76]</sup> Ben Bikman's cell incubation experiments found that linoleic acid alone did not produce insulin resistance in vitro, which challenges the most direct toxicity claims.<sup>[77]</sup>

On the other side: Dave Asprey, Gary Brecka, Paul Saladino, and (with more nuance) Steven Gundry argue that seed oils cause harm through oxidized lipid accumulation in cell membranes, inflammatory metabolite production, and mitochondrial disruption. Linoleic acid, the dominant fatty acid in most seed oils, has an estimated half-life of 600 to 792 days in adipose tissue and cell membranes; if the harm is real, it is slow to clear.<sup>[78]</sup> That half-life number is worth sitting with. It means the oil you ate two years ago is, in a meaningful biochemical sense, still with you.

Both sides agree on certain points, and I suspect those points of agreement may be more useful than the argument itself. Trans fats from partially hydrogenated vegetable oils are unambiguously harmful.<sup>[79]</sup> No disagreement there.

Large industrial processing of seed oils, involving high-heat refining, chemical solvent extraction with hexane, bleaching, and deodorization, produces a fundamentally different product than cold-pressed oil.<sup>[80]</sup> The difference is not subtle. Heating and reheating oils in restaurant settings accelerates PUFA oxidation and generates aldehyde compounds, meaning the same oil behaves differently in a sealed bottle at home and in a commercial fryer.<sup>[81]</sup> And omega-6 fats are not inherently harmful; arachidonic acid is essential for cell membranes and mitochondrial membranes. The harm, if it exists, arises from excess and from imbalance with omega-3.<sup>[82]</sup>

The Minnesota Coronary Experiment, cited by Mark Hyman, is worth examining. It placed roughly 9,000 subjects in a psychiatric hospital on butter versus corn oil. The corn oil group achieved lower LDL but showed no improvement in cardiovascular mortality, which Hyman argues demonstrates that LDL lowering via seed oils is insufficient for cardiovascular protection.<sup>[83]</sup> Norton interprets the same data differently. I will concede that the same study producing opposite conclusions depending on the reader is not a confidence-building feature of this literature. Meanwhile, a meta-analysis comparing canola oil to olive oil found that canola outperformed olive oil at lowering LDL, a result that sits uncomfortably for both sides of the debate.<sup>[84]</sup>

The practical answer is straightforward: avoiding seed oils is a pragmatic strategy for a simple reason: it eliminates much of the ultra-processed food in which they are found. The primary benefit may be from removing the processed food rather than from any unique toxicity of the oils themselves.<sup>[85]</sup>

For home cooking, replacing seed oils with extra-virgin olive oil, avocado oil, coconut oil, or pastured butter is a low-cost, low-risk substitution that even the "not uniquely harmful" camp does not object to.<sup>[86]</sup> That is rare. Consensus of any kind in this debate is unusual in a field this contested. If you choose to eliminate seed oils, the replacement should be a fat that displaces saturated fat (olive oil, for instance) rather than simply adding more saturated fat to the total.<sup>[87]</sup>

The U.S. has seen a roughly 1,000-fold increase in soybean oil consumption since 1900.<sup>[88]</sup> A thousandfold. Whether that increase is independently causal for worsened health or merely correlated with the rise of ultra-processed food is the question this debate has not resolved. It may not resolve it for years. In the meantime, cooking with olive oil and eating whole foods does not require waiting for the answer.<sup>[94]</sup>
