# Chapter 6: Timing, Fiber, and the Rest

Here is a question that the people who study nutrition cannot settle, and I want to put it on the table before anything else: does it matter when you eat your carbohydrates? Multiple speakers in the podcast corpus insist carbohydrates are better tolerated earlier in the day, when insulin sensitivity peaks.<sup>[18]</sup> Opposing voices argue that evening carbohydrates support sleep through the tryptophan-to-serotonin-to-melatonin conversion pathway and aid athletic glycogen replenishment.<sup>[19]</sup> One randomized controlled trial compared eating carbohydrates early versus late versus a Mediterranean diet pattern and found no significant long-term difference in blood sugar, weight, or insulin resistance.<sup>[20]</sup> So one camp says morning. Another says evening. The trial that tested both shrugged. I spent a long time reading those three positions against each other, and the patient accounting is this: the question is genuinely unresolved. Ghrelin, the hunger hormone, peaks naturally at approximately eight in the morning, one in the afternoon, and six in the evening, driven by circadian programming; aligning meals with those peaks may reduce appetite dysregulation, though the evidence is mechanistic rather than clinical.<sup>[21]</sup> Nobody has settled this, and the best any current reading of the evidence can offer is a provisional leaning toward morning carbohydrates for most metabolic profiles.

That carbohydrate dispute sits inside a much larger question about meal timing, and the larger question has firmer ground. A study measuring blood pressure continuously through the night found that people who stopped eating at least three hours before bed had roughly 20 percent fewer cardiovascular events than those who ate later.<sup>[1]</sup> The finding was not about what they ate but about when they closed the valve. A separate trial confined identical food to a six-hour window instead of twelve and measured significantly better twenty-four-hour glucose control, again without altering the food itself.<sup>[2]</sup>

The macronutrient decisions from the previous chapter set the architecture of a diet. This chapter covers everything else on the plate: when the food goes in, what fiber does once it arrives, the polyphenol question, coffee, salt and water, and the glucose monitor on the arm. Together these form **the rest of the plate**, the structural details that determine how well a diet actually works once the protein, carbohydrate, and fat decisions have been made.

## When You Eat

The average American eats across a window of fourteen to eighteen hours per day, with roughly eleven separate eating events scattered from morning to late night.<sup>[3]</sup> Eleven events. That is a system running flow nearly around the clock, with barely a pause for the pipes to clear. I had assumed the number would be four or five, perhaps six for a heavy snacker. Research from San Diego confirmed that half the population eats across a fifteen-hour window or longer.<sup>[4]</sup>

Compressing that window produces metabolic changes independent of caloric intake, and I should be clear about what that means: the food does not change. The controlled trial that placed identical calories in a six-hour versus twelve-hour window found lower glucose excursions, reduced insulin resistance, and lower blood pressure in the shorter window, with no change in the food or its quantity.<sup>[5]</sup> Same input. Different schedule. Better readings, produced by nothing more than shifting the hours during which the food arrived.

The mechanism is partly circadian. **Insulin sensitivity** follows a daily rhythm and is highest in the morning; tissues handle carbohydrates more efficiently earlier in the day.<sup>[6]</sup> Eating activates the sympathetic nervous system. Digesting a large meal takes four to six hours, which means food consumed close to bedtime keeps the body metabolically active during the hours when it should be recovering.<sup>[7]</sup>

Late eating diverts blood flow from the brain to the gut during sleep, impairing the glymphatic cleaning process discussed in Chapter 7 and reducing the nocturnal blood pressure dip that protects the cardiovascular system.<sup>[8]</sup> I paused on that detail and went back through it. The body runs a low-pressure maintenance cycle during sleep, and a late meal forces the whole system back to full operating pressure at precisely the moment it needs to be idling. It is like opening the main line when the overnight maintenance crew is already on the floor.

The practical recommendation from multiple speakers across the dataset is straightforward: start eating at least one hour after waking, stop at least two to three hours before bed.<sup>[9][113]</sup> For most people that means finishing dinner by seven or eight. Front-loading calories, eating a substantial protein-rich breakfast and a lighter dinner, aligns with insulin sensitivity rhythms and produces better glycemic control than the reverse pattern.<sup>[10]</sup> I will concede that the simplicity of the advice is disarming, given the complexity of the circadian machinery behind it.

There is a sex-specific finding that deserves mention. A population-level study found that active women who held their fast until noon or ate in a noon-to-six window had more obesogenic outcomes than women eating from eight in the morning to four or five in the afternoon.<sup>[11]</sup> Extended morning fasting may be beneficial for metabolic health in men but detrimental to muscle and hormonal function in active women; the findings should not be generalized across sexes without qualification.<sup>[12]</sup> The same protocol produces different results in different bodies. That is a recurring theme in this chapter.

One caveat, and I suspect it is underweighted in most of the advice that follows: chronotype matters. For people with a genuinely late circadian rhythm, anchoring the eating window to sleep timing rather than the clock is more appropriate.<sup>[13]</sup> Meals also function as **zeitgebers**, time cues that help reset the body clock. A meal at destination-morning light can accelerate recovery from jet lag; a meal during destination-night delays it.<sup>[14]</sup>

Gut bacteria have their own circadian rhythm, and meal timing relative to that rhythm is a pillar of gut health that rarely surfaces in the fasting conversation.<sup>[15]</sup> Continuous grazing throughout the day paralyzes the **migrating motor complex**, the gut-cleaning mechanism that activates only during fasting states. Think of a drain system that never gets flushed: debris accumulates in the pipes and eventually the flow stops entirely. Creating a daily repair window, a period with no food intake, is necessary for the gut's mucosal lining to maintain itself.<sup>[16]</sup> Two to three meals per day with defined no-eating windows is the structural recommendation that follows from both the circadian and the gut-motility evidence.<sup>[17]</sup>

### The Fasting Question

Three distinct fasting approaches appear repeatedly in the podcast corpus: daily time-restricted eating, extended multi-day fasting, and the fasting-mimicking diet. They share a theoretical foundation but differ substantially in evidence quality and practical risk. I want to be careful with the accounting here, because the enthusiasm for fasting in the podcast world often runs well ahead of the clinical data.

**Time-restricted eating** compresses the daily feeding window to six to eight hours. The metabolic logic is that sustained low blood glucose during the fasting period sensitizes muscles and brain to insulin, and ketone production signals mitochondria to repair and replicate.<sup>[22]</sup> Multiple speakers endorse this approach on the basis of mechanistic reasoning and personal experience.

But Tony Wyss-Coray, a well-credentialed Stanford researcher, stated plainly on Huberman's podcast that "no clinical studies clearly demonstrate a benefit of fasting in humans" and cited primate data showing possible kidney harm.<sup>[23]</sup> That dissent is not peripheral. It reflects a genuine gap between the strength of the animal and mechanistic data and the state of human clinical confirmation.

For athletes and heavy exercisers, time-restricted eating can impair glycogen restoration and performance. I am not sure that point gets the emphasis it deserves in the podcast corpus, but it matters: the longevity optimization population and the performance optimization population are not the same, and the protocol should be specified accordingly.<sup>[24]</sup> For women, compressing the feeding window risks failing to reach adequate daily protein targets; protein sufficiency should take priority over window narrowing.<sup>[25]</sup>

A systematic review of forty studies on intermittent fasting showed average weight loss of seven to eleven pounds, but the protocols were heterogeneous and the window for extrapolation is narrow.<sup>[26]</sup> I read that range carefully. Seven to eleven pounds across forty studies, with no reliable way to tell which protocol produced which outcome. That is a wide spread for a confident recommendation.

Early time-restricted feeding, with the window ending between three and six in the afternoon, improved insulin sensitivity, lowered blood pressure, and reduced oxidative stress in men at risk for type 2 diabetes in a controlled trial from Courtney Peterson's lab.<sup>[27]</sup> The metabolic results were solid. The window is socially impractical for most people, which limits the finding's real-world applicability regardless of its validity.

**Extended fasting** of forty-eight to seventy-two hours is practiced by some longevity researchers and endorsed by several podcast hosts in the corpus. Animal data clearly show activation of beneficial cellular pathways: reduced inflammation, improved protein turnover, better clearance of damaged organelles.<sup>[28]</sup> In animals the evidence is substantial. Human translation is unconfirmed, and Wyss-Coray's dissent about the gap between animal findings and human clinical data applies here with the same weight it carries for daily time-restricted eating.

A strict zero-calorie water fast in someone with existing gut inflammation may spike cortisol and damage intestinal tight junctions, worsening gut health rather than improving it.<sup>[29]</sup> That risk is mechanistic reasoning from a single source, not an established clinical finding. I note it because it is worth flagging for anyone with a compromised gut barrier, even if the evidence is thin.

Metabolically impaired individuals, estimated at 70 percent or more of American adults by some clinical frameworks, may not produce the expected **autophagy** response or the typical muscle-protecting growth hormone rise during extended fasts; partial fasting approaches may be more appropriate for this population.<sup>[30]</sup>

The **fasting-mimicking diet**, developed by Valter Longo at USC, tries to split the difference: five days consuming roughly 800 to 1,100 calories per day from specific low-protein fats and complex carbohydrates designed to avoid triggering mTOR and insulin signaling while still providing calories.<sup>[31]</sup>

In mice, monthly **FMD** reversed high cholesterol, heart problems, and insulin insensitivity even without changing the underlying poor diet; that study was published in Nature Metabolism.<sup>[32]</sup> Human trial data remain preliminary. The optimal frequency for humans has not been established in randomized controlled trials; monthly is the animal-tested frequency, quarterly is the clinical assumption.<sup>[33]</sup>

Longo created and is commercially associated with Prolon, the commercial FMD product, and Steven Gundry, who endorses it as a "game changer" from quarterly use with patients, has discussed the product directly with Longo.<sup>[34]</sup> Those relationships do not invalidate the science, but they belong in the reader's line of sight. I would say the same about any protocol where the researcher is also the seller.

The honest summary of the fasting question is this: the mechanistic and animal evidence is strong, the human clinical evidence is thin, and the gap between the two is wider than the enthusiasm of most advocates suggests.<sup>[114]</sup> Compression of the eating window to something shorter than the American default of fifteen-plus hours is likely beneficial and carries minimal risk for most people. Multi-day protocols carry real risks for specific populations and lack the human trial data to justify broad recommendation, which is not a verdict against fasting as a concept but a precise description of where the evidence currently runs out.

## Fiber: The Substrate That Feeds the System

The average American consumes roughly five grams of prebiotic fiber per day. Hunter-gatherers consumed 100 to 150 grams from dozens of plant sources.<sup>[35]</sup> I found myself returning to those numbers because the scale of the gap is genuinely difficult to absorb. The system was built to process twenty to thirty times the substrate it currently receives. Ninety-five percent of Americans fail to meet even the modest recommended daily fiber intake.<sup>[36]</sup> Ninety-five percent.

The mechanism is direct. Gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber into **short-chain fatty acids**, primarily **butyrate**, propionate, and acetate. Butyrate fuels the cells lining the colon, strengthens the intestinal barrier, and sends anti-inflammatory signals systemically.<sup>[37]</sup> The same fermentation process produces GLP-1, a satiety hormone that travels to the brain and suppresses hunger.<sup>[38][115]</sup> Without fiber, the bacteria that produce GLP-1 starve, and the hunger-suppression signal is blunted.

This parallel between fiber-fed bacterial GLP-1 production and pharmaceutical GLP-1 agonists is noted by multiple speakers, though the potency difference between dietary and pharmaceutical GLP-1 is enormous.<sup>[39]</sup> The plumbing comparison writes itself: the pharmaceutical version is a fire main; the dietary version is a garden hose. Both deliver water. The flow rates differ by orders of magnitude.

Mouse the evidence indicates that four generations of fiber deprivation cause irreversible microbiome loss: the species that ferment fiber simply disappear and cannot be recovered by reintroducing fiber alone.<sup>[40]</sup> That is animal data and may not translate directly to humans, but it illustrates what is at stake in sustained fiber absence. Once certain pipes corrode past a certain point, turning the water back on does not restore the system. Ultra-processed foods compound the problem from both directions: they crowd out whole-food fiber sources, and their artificial sweeteners have been shown in Weizmann Institute research to damage gut bacteria directly.<sup>[41]</sup>

Fiber also binds estrogen in the gut and prevents its reabsorption through the enterohepatic circulation, which means low fiber intake raises circulating estrogen levels.<sup>[42]</sup> Cruciferous vegetables are a key source for this pathway. Fiber modestly improves glycemic control and modestly lowers LDL cholesterol, effects confirmed across meta-analyses.<sup>[43]</sup> Neither effect is dramatic alone. Both are consistent. I note this because the modesty of each individual benefit obscures the breadth of fiber's influence.

The best natural sources of soluble prebiotic fiber include chicory-family vegetables (radicchio, endive, frisee), asparagus, artichokes, garlic, onions, and leeks.<sup>[44]</sup> **Resistant starch**, a form of fiber that reaches the colon undigested, feeds beneficial bacteria when it arrives. Cooking and then cooling starchy foods increases resistant starch content; the bacteria that ferment resistant starch need to be present first, and the benefit increases only as the microbiome is restored.<sup>[45]</sup> That last point is worth pausing on: the same food works differently depending on which bacteria are already present. Prebiotic fiber diversity matters more than quantity alone; different gut species require different substrates, and rotating among plant families provides broader coverage.<sup>[46]</sup>

Mushrooms, including lion's mane, contain polysaccharides with prebiotic properties distinct from plant fiber, adding yet another avenue of diversity.<sup>[47]</sup> Psyllium husk taken before meals slows glucose absorption and modestly lowers HbA1c, though dosing should start gradually to avoid gastrointestinal distress.<sup>[48]</sup> A large salad with fermented vegetables dressed in extra-virgin olive oil before the main meal is a practical pre-load that delivers both fiber and polyphenols in a single dish.<sup>[49]</sup> Even on a ketogenic diet, the carbohydrate portion should consist largely of fiber from non-starchy vegetables; the point is plain: keto carbohydrates should prioritize fiber over digestible starch.<sup>[50]</sup>

Non-digestible prebiotic fiber does not break a fast in the metabolic sense: it provides no absorbable calories and can be consumed during fasting windows without disrupting the fasting state.<sup>[51]</sup> That is a useful detail for anyone combining fasting with fiber supplementation. A Chinese study cited by Gundry placed volunteers on a fourteen-day water fast; half received 100 calories per day of non-absorbable soluble fiber and reported no hunger, while the water-only group was hungry throughout.<sup>[52]</sup> The study is described informally, without author, journal, or year, which limits its evidentiary weight. The directional finding aligns with the SCFA-to-GLP-1 mechanism described above, but the sourcing falls short of what you would want before acting on it.

One clinical observation is : an inability to tolerate fiber-rich foods is itself a sign of significant gut microbiome disruption.<sup>[53]</sup> In other words, the intolerance is the diagnosis. The safe approach is to increase fiber gradually, start with soluble types, and drink adequate water alongside. Fiber is one of the four most commonly deficient dietary factors in women's health, alongside protein, omega-3s, and key micronutrients.<sup>[54]</sup>

## Polyphenols

Polyphenols are plant-protective pigments: the orange, red, yellow, and purple compounds that evolved to shield plants from ultraviolet light, insects, and oxidative stress. When consumed by humans, they activate analogous stress-resistance pathways.<sup>[55]</sup> The highest dietary concentrations are not in colorful fruits and vegetables, as commonly assumed, but in herbs, spices, teas, and coffee.<sup>[56]</sup> I had expected the bright berries and peppers to lead the list, but the spice rack turned out to be a quiet correction to that assumption.

Gut bacteria are essential intermediaries in this process. They metabolize polyphenols into postbiotic compounds (including urolithins from ellagitannins and equol from isoflavones) that often have greater biological activity than the parent molecule.<sup>[57]</sup> Only roughly one-third of people carry the gut bacteria capable of converting pomegranate ellagitannins into **urolithin A**, a compound that feeds **Akkermansia** muciniphila, a keystone species for gut barrier integrity.<sup>[58]</sup> Two out of three people lack the microbial equipment for that conversion. Two thirds of everyone. That is a gut-microbiome finding discussed further in Chapter 9; the supplemental form is covered in Chapter 14.

Extra-virgin olive oil is a concentrated source of polyphenols, particularly hydroxytyrosol and oleocanthal, which nourish gut bacteria and protect against oxidative stress.<sup>[59]</sup> High-polyphenol oil can be identified by a peppery throat sensation from the oleocanthal; heat destroys these compounds, which is why finishing dishes with uncooked oil preserves the benefit.<sup>[60]</sup> Dark chocolate at 72 percent cacao or above is another high-polyphenol source, though adding milk binds the polyphenols and significantly reduces their bioavailability.<sup>[61]</sup> Milk cancels the chocolate. That is an unwelcome finding, but the chemistry is clear.

Anthocyanins from blueberries, blackberries, and black currants cross the blood-brain barrier and have been linked to reduced cognitive decline in observational studies.<sup>[62]</sup> Green tea's dominant polyphenol, **EGCG**, constitutes roughly 30 to 40 percent of its extract; a JAMA meta-analysis linked green tea consumption to reduced all-cause mortality.<sup>[63]</sup> I notice that two of the polyphenol sources with the best evidence are also the most widely consumed beverages after water: tea and coffee. Chlorogenic acid, found in coffee and green coffee beans, acts as a mitochondrial uncoupler and has shown blood pressure reduction and improved endothelial function in clinical trials.<sup>[64]</sup> White mulberry leaf tea contains compounds including **quercetin** and chlorogenic acid; a study published in Diabetes Care found it reduces post-meal glucose spikes.<sup>[65]</sup>

Avocado consumed with a meal dramatically increases absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, minerals, and polyphenols from the same meal, a finding from a human study underscoring how food combinations affect nutrient uptake.<sup>[66]</sup> That is not a property of avocado alone; it is a demonstration of how the fat in avocado acts as a solvent for compounds that are otherwise poorly absorbed.

A disclosure is necessary here. The polyphenol section of the podcast corpus leans heavily on a single channel. Steven Gundry is the source for the majority of specific polyphenol claims, food recommendations, and mechanistic frameworks in this area, and he has commercial olive oil, supplement, and product lines tied to polyphenol-rich foods.<sup>[67][116]</sup> The mechanisms he describes (gut bacterial metabolism of polyphenols into bioactive postbiotics, prebiotic effects of plant pigments, collagen protection from oxidative stress) are broadly consistent with the published literature. But the specific framing, including the relative ranking of food sources and the emphasis on particular compounds, reflects one practitioner's clinical perspective rather than multi-source consensus. The science may be sound, but the sourcing is narrow enough that readers should weight these claims as one informed practitioner's framework rather than settled multi-source consensus.

A Mediterranean diet rich in polyphenols is associated in multiple interventional trials with improved epigenetic biological age markers.<sup>[68]</sup> That association is observational, and confounders are difficult to separate from the diet itself, as discussed in the previous chapter. The direction of the evidence is consistent. Increasing plant diversity (including shoots, sprouts, microgreens, and herbs) is a low-risk way to broaden polyphenol intake regardless of the strength of any individual claim.<sup>[69]</sup>

## Coffee

Caffeine is the most widely consumed psychoactive substance on Earth, used daily by roughly 90 percent of adults worldwide.<sup>[70]</sup> It works by blocking **adenosine** receptors in the brain, suppressing the fatigue signal while leaving the underlying recovery need intact.<sup>[71]</sup> Think of a pressure-relief valve held shut: the pressure is still building behind it, but the gauge reads normal. The fatigue accumulates. You simply cannot read it. Caffeine also increases adrenaline and noradrenaline, producing the alertness and focus that make it useful and the jitteriness that makes excess counterproductive.<sup>[72]</sup>

The metabolic benefits of coffee extend well beyond the caffeine molecule. I did not expect this section to be as long as it is; there turns out to be quite a lot happening in a cup of coffee. Chlorogenic acid improves blood flow and endothelial function, reduces blood pressure, and acts as a mitochondrial uncoupler contributing modestly to fat reduction.<sup>[73][74]</sup> Coffee has negligible oxalate content, roughly 2 milligrams per serving, making it acceptable for people managing kidney stones or oxalate sensitivity.<sup>[75]</sup>

Here is a finding I kept coming back to. Caffeine's performance-enhancing effects are most pronounced in caffeine-naive individuals. For habituated daily users (which is most of the adult population), the performance benefit observed during exercise is likely withdrawal reversal rather than true enhancement.<sup>[76][117]</sup> The morning boost regular drinkers feel may simply be the restoration of a baseline they lowered the night before. That is a sobering distinction. This methodological problem haunts most caffeine research: getting regular users to abstain for a two-week washout is difficult, and most studies fail to achieve it.<sup>[77]</sup> Caffeine and stimulants like green tea or spicy food contribute to fat loss primarily through behavioral motivation to exercise, not through directly boosting resting metabolic rate; the direct thermogenic effect is modest.<sup>[78]</sup>

**L-theanine** combined with caffeine at roughly a 2:1 ratio produces calm, focused attention without jitteriness by increasing alpha brain wave activity.<sup>[79]</sup> Green tea naturally contains both compounds. The combination is one of the few supplement stacks with a straightforward mechanism, minimal risk, and a body of supporting evidence solid enough to act on without waiting for larger trials.

The advice to delay morning coffee by 30 to 90 minutes after waking, to avoid compounding the cortisol awakening response, has become a widely repeated protocol attributed to Huberman.<sup>[80]</sup> The cortisol-caffeine interaction on which it rests is primarily documented in non-habituated users; regular coffee drinkers show a blunted cortisol response to caffeine.<sup>[81]</sup> For someone who has been drinking coffee every morning for years, the practical significance of the delay is an open question. I am not going to pretend that one is settled either.

One claim in the coffee data requires the cold register. Dave Asprey attributes coffee jitteriness to mold toxins in conventional beans rather than to caffeine itself, including a proposed connection to autoimmune thyroid effects.<sup>[82]</sup> Asprey owns Danger Coffee, a brand marketed as mold-free. The mold-in-coffee autoimmune claim is not supported by named peer-reviewed studies. Caffeine is the primary driver of jitteriness, and the commercial interest in promoting an alternative explanation is direct and should be weighed accordingly.

## Salt, Electrolytes, and Water

Sodium, potassium, and magnesium are the three core electrolytes essential for the function of all cells. Neurons cannot fire action potentials without adequate sodium. Even 1 percent dehydration measurably decreases physical output and mental performance, and proper hydration requires electrolytes, not just water.<sup>[83]</sup> Water alone is insufficient. The dissolved minerals are what make the fluid functional.

The body's sodium sensor, the **OVLT** (organum vasculosum of the lateral terminalis), detects blood sodium concentration and drives thirst through two distinct pathways: osmotic thirst from high sodium concentration and hypovolemic thirst from low blood volume.<sup>[84]</sup> Both drive seeking of water and salt simultaneously. When blood osmolarity is high, the hypothalamus releases vasopressin, causing the kidneys to retain water; when diluted, vasopressin is suppressed and the kidneys excrete freely.<sup>[85]</sup> One sensor reading two variables, adjusting a single valve, and doing so continuously without conscious input from the person it is regulating.

The origin of the "eight glasses per day" rule is worth knowing. It traces to a 1945 U.S. Food and Nutrition Board recommendation for 2.5 liters per day, which included the caveat that most of that water comes from food.<sup>[86]</sup> The caveat was lost as the guideline spread through popular media. That tends to happen with nutritional advice: the number travels and the footnote stays behind. Leafy greens, vegetables, coffee, and tea all contribute substantially to daily hydration, and caffeine does not meaningfully dehydrate habituated users.<sup>[87]</sup>

Salt also regulates appetite for other nutrients, including sugar and carbohydrates, via brain regions that monitor sodium levels and influence cravings.<sup>[88]</sup> The implication is that the salt on your food is not merely flavoring; it is talking to your brain about what else you need. Dehydration is one of the most common and overlooked causes of constipation; the colon reabsorbs water from stool, and chronic dehydration hardens it. Adequate hydration is a first-line intervention before reaching for anything more complex.<sup>[89]</sup>

The practical guidance on hydration is simple, and I think worth stating directly: steady flow throughout the day is more effective than infrequent large intakes, because the body can only absorb a limited amount at once.<sup>[90]</sup> A slow, continuous trickle outperforms periodic flooding. After six to eight hours of sleep, the body is in a naturally dehydrated state; morning water with electrolytes or a pinch of mineral salt restores fluid and electrolyte balance.<sup>[91]</sup> During extended exercise, especially in heat, sodium replacement alongside carbohydrate fueling is essential for maintaining performance and preventing hyponatremia.<sup>[92]</sup>

On a ketogenic diet, the kidneys excrete sodium at elevated rates. Most keto-flu symptoms (the fatigue, headache, and irritability that discourage newcomers) are caused by sodium loss, not by carbohydrate withdrawal itself. Replacing sodium with mineral salt prevents and rapidly reverses these symptoms.<sup>[93]</sup> I will state that plainly: the symptoms most people attribute to carbohydrate withdrawal are a plumbing problem. The system is losing salt, not missing sugar.

During extended fasting, sodium is likewise excreted and must be replaced; sea salt in water is sufficient for shorter fasts.<sup>[94]</sup> I mention this because fasting advocates rarely include electrolyte guidance alongside the protocol itself.

Iodized salt is the recommended cooking salt because the thyroid requires iodine to synthesize T4 and T3, and many people fall short.<sup>[95]</sup> Most commercial salted butters use non-iodized salt, which means that relying on butter for sodium does not address the iodine gap. It is a detail that matters more than its size suggests.

Over-hydration, surprisingly, is a greater risk than dehydration for slower marathon runners who spend four to six hours on the course.<sup>[96]</sup> Drinking too much plain water dilutes blood sodium and chloride, which can cause cardiac arrhythmias. Electrolyte-containing drinks at aid stations are essential for this population. Avoiding large volumes of water close to bedtime prevents sleep disruption from nocturia.<sup>[97]</sup>

One contested area in the salt data deserves direct mention. Multiple speakers in the dataset argue for higher sodium intake than official guidelines recommend, with some claiming that following low-sodium guidelines increases heart attack risk.<sup>[98]</sup> This directly contradicts WHO, AHA, and FDA recommendations. The evidence on sodium and cardiovascular risk is complex, and optimal intake varies by individual cardiovascular risk profile, but the blanket claim that "more salt prevents heart attacks" is not supported by current meta-analyses. The trade-off belongs to you: the 2026 U.S. Dietary Guidelines do distinguish sodium from highly processed foods, which is targeted for restriction, from sodium from whole food sources, which is no longer categorically restricted.<sup>[99]</sup> That distinction is useful, while the broader podcast tendency to frame established sodium guidelines as straightforwardly harmful is not supported by the meta-analytic record.

## The Glucose Monitor on the Arm

A **continuous glucose monitor** inserts a small sensor under the skin that measures glucose in interstitial fluid every few minutes and transmits readings to a smartphone.<sup>[100]</sup> Sensors are worn for roughly fourteen days and enable real-time, painless glucose tracking without finger-prick testing. Dexcom's Stelo, launched in early 2024, was the first FDA-approved over-the-counter **CGM** available without a prescription, making the technology accessible to non-diabetic users for the first time.<sup>[101]</sup> For anyone accustomed to the opacity of standard blood panels, which offer one reading from one moment, a CGM is a different category of instrument.

The core value proposition is personalization. Individual glucose responses to identical foods vary markedly between people, a finding demonstrated by Eran Segal's lab at the Weizmann Institute and confirmed by Michael Snyder's lab at Stanford.<sup>[102]</sup> A food that spikes glucose in one person may produce a flat response in another. CGM makes that variation visible in a way that glycemic index charts and periodic blood tests cannot. Same food. Different bodies. Different readings. That is not a calibration error but the central insight the technology was designed to surface.

Even people without diabetes can have significant post-meal glucose spikes that contribute to metabolic disorders over time; a Cell Metabolism study confirmed this pattern.<sup>[103]</sup> That finding is easy to miss in a chapter about diabetes technology, but it is not restricted to diabetics. Large glucose peaks and valleys cause brain fog, fatigue, irritability, and hunger; post-meal spikes cause sleepiness.<sup>[104]</sup> A brief post-meal walk of ten to fifteen minutes uses contracting muscles to absorb excess glucose directly from the bloodstream, measurably reducing the spike, and CGM makes the effect visible in real time.<sup>[105]</sup> I noticed that finding and went back to reread it. The walk does not change the food or the person. It changes the flow rate. Muscles act as a second set of drains, pulling glucose out of the main line.

CGM also reveals patterns that require context to interpret correctly, and I would say this is where the technology becomes genuinely tricky. Coffee, exercise, and sauna all transiently raise blood glucose; these spikes do not indicate metabolic dysfunction and should not be acted on as dietary problems.<sup>[106]</sup> Alcohol suppresses blood glucose on CGM by diverting liver metabolism, a pattern easily misread as beneficial while underlying metabolic disruption persists.<sup>[107]</sup> The dawn phenomenon, a rise in blood glucose upon waking caused by hepatic glucose release, is a normal physiological response rather than a dietary alarm.<sup>[108]</sup> Proper CGM interpretation requires understanding these contexts. Without that understanding, the data can drive anxiety rather than insight. The gauge is precise. Reading the gauge correctly is harder than wearing it.

The recommended approach is to use CGM as a time-limited discovery tool rather than year-round obsessive monitoring.<sup>[109][118]</sup> Fourteen days is enough to see personal food-glucose relationships across a representative range of meals and activities.<sup>[110]</sup> The goal is to learn which foods spike your glucose individually, discover the blunting effect of post-meal movement, and then apply that knowledge without needing the sensor permanently. I think this is a sensible limit. The instrument teaches you something; then you take it off.

One hour less sleep raises next-day average glucose by roughly 9 percent.<sup>[111]</sup> A single hour. Nine percent. Eating close to bedtime, especially high-carbohydrate meals, impairs sleep quality and elevates next-day blood glucose; CGM overnight data reveals this pattern clearly.<sup>[112]</sup> When you eat affects not only what happens to the food, but what happens to your sleep, and what happens to your blood sugar the following morning. The timing chapter and the glucose chapter end in the same place because they describe the same system.
